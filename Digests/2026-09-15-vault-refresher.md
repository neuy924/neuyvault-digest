### Philosophy - Skin-Ego Theory - Anzieu and the Architecture of the Self - Review
*Review/Philosophies*

Didier Anzieu's *Le Moi-peau* (1985) proposes the psyche is organized around nine tactile functions of the skin: containment (Sac), protection and stimulus-filtering (Screen), intersensoriality, and sign-inscription, among others — a topological self rather than a hydraulic one. Pathologies of the skin-ego — the Moi-écorché (flayed ego), Sieve-ego (unretained excitation), Vampire Ego (extracting from others) — map clinical experience onto failures of the psychic envelope. The review extends Anzieu into digital life (smartphone as tactile mirror, data breach as narcissistic rupture) and aesthetic practice (Bacon, Beckett as artists of failed containment).

**Key concepts:** skin-ego · psychic envelope · Sieve-ego · intersensoriality · somatic aesthetics

### History - Qing Dynasty Fall - Inner Asian Frontier Nationhood - Review
*Review/Historiques*

The review reframes 1911 not as a Chinese national revolution but as the fragmentation of a multiethnic Inner Asian empire that governed Mongolia, Tibet, Xinjiang, and Manchuria through differentiated mechanisms: Buddhist patronage, multilingual bureaucracy, banner systems, and tribute hierarchy. Multiple post-Qing futures briefly existed — pan-Mongolic, pan-Buddhist, Turkestani, pan-Turkic — before three closure mechanisms foreclosed them: Chinese territorial nationalism, Japanese imperial capture, and Soviet-managed fragmentation. The Manchuria case study shows its transformation from dynastic homeland to railway-imperialism battleground to Manchukuo puppet state, illustrating how a shared imperial space was cannibalized into mutually exclusive nationalist projects.

**Key concepts:** Inner Asian frontier · Qing multiethnic governance · pan-Mongolic futures · banner systems · closure mechanisms

## Cross-Note Thread

The most surprising thread across this digest is how colonial and diasporic survival consistently operates through the same move: appropriating the master's tools and making them unrecognizable as such from outside the community. The Glocalized Pantry's Cola Chicken and Banana Ketchup, the Chinese Diaspora Kitchen's Chop Suey as legal sanctuary strategy, and the Code-Switching reference's account of mimicry (Bhabha: "almost the same but not quite") all describe how dominated groups occupy imperial forms — military-issue Spam, the English language, fried-food American taste — and bend them into something with a different meaning from inside. The Qing Fall review adds a historical dimension: the Inner Asian multiethnic empire's dissolution into mutually exclusive nationalist projects shows how the imperial space that enabled this kind of hybrid legibility gets foreclosed once nationalist closure mechanisms win. The South Asian History wiki entry makes the same argument from the other direction — the BJP's attempt to expunge Mughal legacy fails because the legacy is now infrastructure: it is in the language being spoken, the food being eaten, the music being played, and it cannot be cleanly excised without tearing the fabric of the culture claiming to expel it. Taken together, these four notes suggest cultural creolization is not just aesthetic but politically load-bearing: it survives precisely because it is illegible to the nationalizing gaze.

A second thread connects the ego-boundary notes. Anzieu's Skin-Ego (with its Sieve-ego and Vampire Ego pathologies) and the Sampat essay's theory of queer friendship-as-excess both argue that the self is a permeable membrane, not a solid core. Sampat friendships work precisely because both parties agree to suspend normal ego-buffering — to be excessively available to each other's affect without that constituting a demand or a boundary violation. The Bodies & Practice wiki's personhood entry (buffered individual vs. porous dividual, after Strathern) gives this a cross-cultural frame: the hard-boundaried "buffered individual" of Western modernity is a local historical invention, not a universal fact. The Naming Systems note adds an unexpected dimension: the choice of a child's name across the Sinosphere is itself an ego-boundary act, projecting a social self before it exists, embedding it in semantic fields (nature, affect, prestige) that pre-shape its permeability to others. What a name permits phonologically and orthographically is, in Silverstein's terms, an indexical first-order act — and the Hanja restriction list is a state-level intervention in that permeability, determining which selves are available to be made legible in which registers.

Finally, two notes converge specifically on Ireland's position in AI governance: the Medical Oncology AI reference and the Bodies wiki's AI Scribes entry both track the HSE AI for Care 2026–2030 strategy, the first from the oncologist workflow side and the second from clinical governance. The picture that emerges is of a health system caught between genuine enthusiasm for AI tools (ambient scribing pilots, Aidoc radiology triage, trial-matching experiments) and structural fragmentation that keeps AI at the department level rather than system level. The EU AI Act appears in both as the regulatory frame shaping what gets built and what gets delayed — an institutional equivalent of the cultural sieve the Naming Systems note identifies in Korea's Hanja restriction list. The sieve metaphor connects back to Anzieu: a Sieve-ego fails to retain excitation; a Sieve-state fails to consolidate AI capability across its membrane. Both are problems of insufficient envelope function — of a containing structure that lets too much through without integration.
