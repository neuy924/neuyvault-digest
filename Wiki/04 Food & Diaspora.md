## The Wok as Historical Document

These two companion essays examine the Chinese diaspora pantry from different angles — the Garrison essay as a natural history of how foreign ingredients entered Chinese kitchens; the Transnational Wok as a socio-technical history of how Chinese cooking traveled and transformed globally.

### [[Food Studies - Glocalized Pantry - Natural History of the Diaspora Kitchen - Read|The Garrison in the Wok: Natural History of the Glocalized Pantry]]
*Format: READ · Non-Technical collection*

A "natural history" of the *glocalized pantry* — the archive of ingredients and techniques that constitutes "Chinese cooking" but is substantially composed of materials that arrived from outside China across specific historical moments. The "garrison" metaphor is rich and possibly ironic: military garrisons are established in foreign territory to project power outward, but the wok as garrison was *receptive* — it absorbed the foreign, naturalized it, and forgot the absorption. The key Columbian Exchange arrivals that transformed Chinese cuisine: 辣椒 chili peppers (introduced 16th c. via Portuguese trade routes; now inseparable from Hunan, Sichuan, Yunnan, and Guizhou food identities), 番茄 tomatoes, 花生 peanuts (now constitutive of Chinese snack and sauce culture), 番薯 sweet potatoes (which fed famine-era populations and became a staple of South Chinese diet and diaspora food memory), and 玉米 maize. Beyond Columbian Exchange: spice absorption from overland trade (cumin 孜然 in Xinjiang and Northwest China), colonial-era sugar expansion, and the pantry adaptations that diaspora communities made when original ingredients were unavailable.

**Key arguments:**
- What is experienced as "authentic" Chinese cuisine is substantially composed of ingredients that have been Chinese for only 400–500 years — authenticity is a moving target measured from within living memory, not from any objective historical origin
- The "garrison" metaphor exposes an asymmetry: Chinese culinary culture was absorptive where its military-political history was often defensive — the wok won where the wall failed
- The natural history approach (documenting arrivals, adaptations, and eventual naturalization) reveals the mechanism of culinary creolization: foreign ingredients are first labeled as foreign (番茄 = "foreign tomato," 番薯 = "foreign sweet potato") and eventually lose the foreignness marker as they become indistinguishable from "traditional" food

**Key concepts:** glocalization · Columbian Exchange · 辣椒 chili · 番茄 tomato · 花生 peanut · 番薯 sweet potato · culinary creolization · pantry composition · natural history of food · diaspora food memory

### [[Food Studies - Glocalized Pantry - Natural History of the Diaspora Kitchen - Review|The Garrison in the Wok: A Natural History of the Glocalized Pantry]]
*Format: REVIEW (scholarly)*

Scholarly companion to the READ essay. Introduces two analytical concepts absent from the READ version: (1) **the commodity hack** — the reappropriation of industrial food commodities as technical ingredients that perform chemical functions their manufacturers did not intend; Coca-Cola as the paradigm case (phosphoric acid as a meat tenderizer; sugar content producing accelerated Maillard browning; carbonation as a leavening agent in batter) — diaspora cooks discovering that industrial products can be *hacked* to extend a Chinese culinary logic when traditional ingredients are unavailable or prohibitively expensive; (2) **glocalized dysbiosis** — the transformation of fermented and preserved food traditions (酱 *jiàng*, 醋 *cù*, 腐乳 *fǔrǔ*, 豆豉 *dòuchǐ*) under industrial food supply conditions; the microbiological homogenization of diaspora kitchens as traditional fermentation cultures are displaced by standardized industrial equivalents. The military logistics angle: the essay traces how Qing and Republican-era military provisioning shaped what could travel, what could be preserved, and therefore what entered diaspora kitchens as "default" Chinese cooking.

**Key arguments:**
- The commodity hack is not culinary improvisation but a systematic practice that reveals diaspora cooks as *applied chemists*: the question is not "what ingredients are available?" but "what chemical reactions do I need to produce, and what available materials can trigger them?"
- Glocalized dysbiosis is a form of cultural loss that is structurally invisible: the new fermented products taste approximately correct, perform approximately the same culinary function, but are microbiologically unrecognizable — the living culture has been replaced by an industrial simulacrum
- Military logistics explains the specific *which* of the Columbian Exchange absorption: which foreign ingredients entered the Chinese pantry and when tracks closely to which military provisioning routes existed, not simply which ingredients were botanically available

**Key concepts:** commodity hack · Coca-Cola paradigm · phosphoric acid · Maillard reaction · glocalized dysbiosis · fermentation culture · 酱 *jiàng* · 豆豉 *dòuchǐ* · military logistics · culinary chemistry

### [[Food Studies - Chinese Diaspora Cuisine - Socio-Technical History of the Diaspora Palette - Read|The Transnational Wok: Socio-Technical History of the Chinese Diaspora Palette]]
*Format: READ · Non-Technical collection*

Socio-technical history of how the Chinese "palette" (culinary range of flavors, textures, and techniques available to a cook) traveled with diaspora communities and underwent systematic transformation under the material and social conditions of migration. "Socio-technical" names the essay's dual analytical lens: the *social* networks (family and clan associations, trade networks, labor migration routes — the Cantonese mines, the Fujian pepper trade, the Hokkien sea traders) that transmitted culinary practice, and the *technical* material conditions (wok design and metallurgy, fuel types available in host countries, ingredient substitution when Chinese staples were unavailable) that constrained and enabled adaptation. Major diaspora routes examined likely include Southeast Asia (Nanyang / 南洋 communities: Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Philippines, Vietnam — each developing distinct creolized cuisines), North America (railroad-era Chinese-American cooking, chop suey as adaptation, the gradual differentiation of American Chinese from mainland regional cuisines), and possibly Europe and Australia.

**Key arguments:**
- The Chinese diaspora palette is not a degraded copy of Chinese cuisine but a distinct culinary formation generated by specific social and material conditions — Chinese-American, Nanyang, and Malaysian Chinese cuisines are not failures to be authentic but successes at adaptation
- Socio-technical analysis reveals that the wok itself is a constraint: Chinese diaspora food spread *within the technical affordances of wok cooking* — high-heat stir-fry is not universal but demands specific fuel infrastructure (historically charcoal, later gas), which shaped where and how the cuisine could spread
- The "palette" metaphor makes visible the aesthetic dimension of culinary history: diaspora communities curated flavors into coherent systems, not merely survived on available ingredients

**Key concepts:** Chinese diaspora · 南洋 Nanyang · socio-technical history · culinary palette · adaptation · food technology · wok infrastructure · chop suey · overseas Chinese communities (華僑/華裔)

### [[Food Studies - Chinese Diaspora Cuisine - Socio-Technical History of the Diaspora Palette - Review|The Transnational Wok: A Socio-Technical History of the Chinese Diaspora Palette]]
*Format: REVIEW (scholarly)*

Scholarly review of Chinese diaspora cuisine structured as a diachronic wave model with integrated technical analysis. Five distinct waves are mapped: the **Taishanese Foundation** (1850–1940), when Cantonese-speaking Taishanese labourers built the North American Chinese food infrastructure (chop suey houses, chow mein, the Cantonese barbecue economy); the **Post-War General Tso Wave** (1945–1990), tracking the Hunanese and Shanghainese restaurant-owners who refugee-migrated via Taiwan and Hong Kong and introduced the sweet-sour-spicy register now associated with "American Chinese food"; the **Szechuan Hegemony and Málà Globalization** (1990–2015), analyzing how Sichuanese cuisine's numbing-spicy (*málà* 麻辣) flavor profile achieved global penetration via the post-reform economic migration of mainland Sichuanese restaurateurs; **Post-Hegemony Hyper-Regionalism** (2015–present), documenting the simultaneous proliferation of regional Chinese cuisines (Yunnan, Shanxi, Dongbei, Xinjiang) in diaspora markets; and **Second-Gen Reclamation**, the movement of children of diaspora migrants who re-engage and redefine "home" cuisine as a cultural and culinary project. Technical analysis covers wok hei (鑊氣) thermal dynamics — the Maillard reaction and pyrazine formation at temperatures above 230°C — and a 24-Flavor Matrix that maps Szechuan's classical flavor compounds.

**Key arguments:**
- The diachronic wave model reveals that "Chinese food" in diaspora has never been a single cuisine but a sequence of displaced regional cuisines, each representing a specific migration event — the popular perception of unified "Chinese food" is itself a political-culinary artifact of the Cantonese monopoly on early diaspora restaurant infrastructure
- Wok hei is an epistemic category as much as a technical one: it names the thermal signature of a specific cooking regime (high-BTU commercial wok burner, rapid toss, short cook-time) that is structurally impossible to reproduce on domestic Western equipment — diaspora cuisine therefore operates under a technical constraint that shapes what can authentically travel
- Second-Gen Reclamation represents a qualitative shift in the sociology of diaspora cuisine: the second generation's engagement is memory-driven and emotionally loaded in ways that the first generation's survival-driven adaptation was not, producing foods that are simultaneously more "authentic" (using childhood flavor memory as referent) and more invented (filling gaps in inherited knowledge with research and imagination)

**Key concepts:** Taishanese Foundation · chop suey · General Tso's Chicken · *málà* 麻辣 · Szechuan hegemony · wok hei 鑊氣 · Maillard reaction · 24-Flavor Matrix · post-hegemony hyper-regionalism · second-gen reclamation · diaspora wave model
**Tags:** chinese-diaspora-cuisine · transnational-wok · socio-technical-history · taishanese · general-tso · szechuan · mala · wok-hei · diaspora-waves · second-gen

## Rolled Foods: Philology, Wrapper Technology & Migration

### [[Food Studies - Rolled Foods - Cross-Cultural History Across East and Southeast Asia - Review|From Spring Pancakes to Popiah: Rolled Foods Across China, Vietnam & SEA]]
*Format: REVIEW*

Examines a family of rolled foods — 春盤 *chūnpán*, 春餅 *chūnbǐng*, 春卷 *chūnjuǎn*, 潤餅 *rùnbǐng*, popiah, lumpia, gỏi cuốn, nem cuốn, chả giò, nem rán — arguing they are **not a single linear lineage** but an interconnected field shaped by four historically distinct forces: (1) **ritual seasonality** — the 立春 / 咬春 Chinese spring-food complex that tied spring pancakes to seasonal renewal and symbolic eating; (2) **wrapper technology** — a material history moving from wheat pancakes to thin wheat crepes to rice paper *bánh tráng* to industrial pastry skins, each representing a different ecological and labor regime; (3) **migration** — Fujian/Southern Min coastal communities generating the 潤餅 transition form and carrying it into Southeast Asia as popiah and lumpia; (4) **linguistic change** — naming systems that encode fundamentally different understandings of what the dish *is*.

The essay explicitly distinguishes four relationship types across the field: **documented lineage** (Chinese spring-food complex → Fujian rùnbǐng → popiah); **lexical borrowing** (popiah as Hokkien phonetic borrowing into Malay/Indonesian); **regional contact** (Vietnamese and Chinese traditions in adjacent but not identical culinary worlds); and **parallel development** (shared wrapper-and-fill form emerging independently in multiple ecological contexts). Vietnam is treated as connected-but-distinct: Vietnamese rolls are rice-paper-based, herb-rich, and named through local culinary categories (*gỏi cuốn* = "salad roll") — not descended from the Chinese spring-calendar complex.

**Key arguments:**
- The "spring roll family" is better analyzed as a *field* than a family tree: shared form (wrapper + filling + roll) emerged from multiple developments converging on a practical technique, not from diffusion from a single Chinese origin — the essay actively argues against single-origin heritage claims
- Wrapper technology is the key historical variable because it represents different ecological and labor regimes, not mere ingredient substitution: wheat pancake (agricultural-calendar ritual, high-effort production) → rice paper (herb-salad vehicle, different labor economy, different cultural meaning)
- The linguistic evidence provides the most incisive anti-origin-story argument: *rùnbǐng* emphasizes moisture/freshness; *chūnjuǎn* emphasizes seasonal timing; *popiah* is a Hokkien phonetic loan; *gỏi cuốn* classifies by culinary type — four naming logics encoding four different answers to "what is this dish fundamentally?"
- Southeast Asian forms (popiah, lumpia) should be analyzed through diaspora and creolization: they carry philological traces of Fujian migration while becoming deeply localized foods in their receiving contexts — they are not preservation failures but adaptation successes

**Key concepts:** 春餅 · 春卷 · 潤餅 · popiah · lumpia · gỏi cuốn · bánh tráng · Fujian diaspora · 立春 · 咬春 · wrapper technology · culinary philology · creolization · four relationship types (lineage / lexical borrowing / regional contact / parallel development) · ecological and labor regimes

**Constellations:** [[00c Thematic Constellations#The Portable Object: How Things Travel & Transform|Portable Object]] · [[00c Thematic Constellations#Philology as Method Across Domains|Philology as Method]]

## Diaspora, Lineage & Social History

### [[History - Chinese Migration - Zhang Lineage Ecology and Migration to the Straits - Review|Surname, Village, Sea: Zhang, Lineage Ecology & Migration from North China to the Straits]]
*Format: REVIEW (scholarly)*

A microhistory using the Zhang 張 surname's lineage ecology as a case study for understanding the migration patterns from North China to the Fujian coast and ultimately across the Straits to Taiwan and Southeast Asia. "Lineage ecology" treats lineages not as genealogical trees but as adaptive systems: the way a lineage organizes land, ritual, marriage, migration, and economic network is an ecological strategy for survival and reproduction across changing environmental and political conditions. The essay traces: Zhang lineage formations in the North China Plain; the coastal Fujian consolidation and the development of maritime trading networks; the crossing to Taiwan and the establishment of lineage-temple networks; and the Nanyang dispersion. Food and culinary memory appear as an important dimension of lineage continuity — what a lineage carries across migration includes its food practices, which encode origin, season, and social belonging.

**Key arguments:**
- The surname-as-social-infrastructure approach reveals migration patterns invisible to standard demographic history: lineage networks organized migration, credit, and social insurance across generations
- Taiwan's lineage-temple landscape is a direct encoding of Fujian migration history — each major temple network traces back to a specific Fujian origin cult brought across the Straits
- Food is a dimension of lineage memory: specific dishes, festival foods, and ingredient preferences carry geographic and genealogical information about origin that persists long after other cultural markers have faded

**Key concepts:** Zhang lineage · lineage ecology · North China to Fujian migration · Straits crossing · Taiwan lineage-temple network · Nanyang dispersion · surname-as-social-infrastructure · food as lineage memory

## Food Theory & Sociology

### [[Food Studies - Food Taboo - Social Life of Food Wrongness and Contamination - Review|Matter Out of Place at the Table: Chocolate, Cheese & the Social Life of Food Wrongness]]
*Format: REVIEW (scholarly)*

A review-essay that uses the asymmetric wrongness of chocolate-on-cheese vs. cheese-on-chocolate as a diagnostic object for analyzing food disgust, classification, and legitimacy. The asymmetry cannot be explained by flavor chemistry alone: "food wrongness" emerges through the interaction of material behavior, perceptual expectation, symbolic order, historical meal structure, and socially stratified taste. The essay synthesizes four frameworks: Paul Rozin's anticipatory disgust model (oral incorporation and contamination before tasting); Mary Douglas's pollution analysis (anomalous food offends by violating classificatory order — "matter out of place" — rather than by objective harm); Pierre Bourdieu's habitus and distinction (taste as a class-stratified field; food disgust as field-position indexed); and Jonathan Haidt's moral disgust (the purity coding that moralizes food rejection). Central claim: "wrong food" is a multi-level event — materially unstable, ingestively offensive, symbolically misplaced, historically illegible, and socially discredited simultaneously.

**Key arguments:**
- The directional asymmetry (cheese-on-chocolate is more widely rejected than chocolate-on-cheese) reveals that disgust follows coded *directions* within meal structure, not physical properties: melted cheese introduces a "savory heat grammar" that violates the dessert substrate's expected logic
- Douglas's "matter out of place" provides the strongest single explanatory frame: the food violates the *grid* of category boundaries (savory/sweet, course boundary) rather than tasting objectively bad — the problem is classificatory, not gustatory
- Bourdieu reveals the class dimension that Douglas leaves underdeveloped: food disgust is distributed differently across social positions, and what appears as aesthetic judgment is structurally organized by habitus — the culinarily educated disgust is not independent of their position in the food field

**Key concepts:** food wrongness · anticipatory disgust · oral incorporation · matter out of place · Mary Douglas · Bourdieu's habitus and distinction · meal structure · dessert boundary · savoury-sweet asymmetry · Rozin's contamination model · Haidt on moral disgust
**Tags:** food-disgust · food-wrongness · cultural-sociology · matter-out-of-place · douglas · bourdieu · rozin · haidt · food-symbolism · taste-psychology · meal-structure
**Cross-domain:** [[00c Thematic Constellations#Philology as Method]] (classification systems) · [[05 Queer & Asian Identity]] (disgust and social typing)

### [[Food Studies - Salt Fish - Atlantic World and East Asian Salted Seafood Traditions - Reference|Salt Fish and the Worlds It Preserved: Atlantic Commodity Biography & East Asian Sensory Politics]]
*Format: REFERENCE (analytical)*

Survey and theoretical treatment of salted fish as a global food technology and historical object, moving from the Atlantic salt-cod economy to East Asian salted and fermented seafood traditions. The core argument: salted fish is not merely preserved food but an **infrastructure** — of trade, religion, empire, coerced labour, rice economies, sensory identity, and cultural memory. In the Atlantic world, salt cod connected North Atlantic fisheries, Catholic fasting markets, Caribbean plantation provisioning, slavery, and later national cuisines (Portuguese *bacalhau*, Jamaican ackee and saltfish). The critical historiographic frame draws on Harold Innis's staples theory (cod as an "international economy"), Sidney Mintz's political anthropology of food (the plantation-capitalism link between production and consumption), and Mark Kurlansky's commodity biography. Atlantic-world scholarship on slavery, provisioning, and racial capitalism grounds the argument that salt fish distributed food quality along lines of class, race, and empire. In East Asia, salted fish operates through a different logic: rice accompaniment, fermentation substrate, household frugality, class marker, smell politics, diaspora memory, and carcinogenic heritage. Cantonese 鹹魚, Japanese 熟れ鮨/塩辛/唐墨, Korean 젓갈, and Taiwanese 烏魚子 show how salted seafood mediates between freshness and decay, poverty and luxury, household memory and public smell, and cultural heritage and health risk.

**Key arguments:**
- Salted fish is a **boundary object**: it crosses boundaries between fresh and rotten, local and global, poor food and prestige food, religious observance and imperial provisioning, ration and national dish
- The Atlantic salt-cod economy cannot be narrated only as trade or culinary history — it provisioned coerced plantation labour and distributed food quality along class and race lines; Caribbean saltfish is both a product of slavery and a creative diasporic transformation of it
- East Asian salted fish challenges the assumption that the region's seafood aesthetics are dominated by raw-freshness (sashimi) culture — fermented sushi, shiokara, jeotgal, and Chinese salted fish represent controlled decay as flavour technology
- "Smell politics" — the sociological visibility of pungent food — marks class, ethnicity, diaspora belonging, and outsider disgust; the smell of salted fish is never merely olfactory but always also about social positioning

**Key concepts:** boundary object · staples theory · commodity biography · Harold Innis · Sidney Mintz · Mark Kurlansky · Atlantic-world scholarship · plantation provisioning · racial capitalism · 鹹魚 · 熟れ鮨 · 젓갈 · 烏魚子 · fermentation · smell politics · carcinogenic heritage

**Cross-domain:** [[03 East Asian Linguistics#Semantic Ecology of the Sinosphere]] (taboo and smell vocabulary) · [[00c Thematic Constellations#Disease Body & Stigma]] (smell as stigma)

## Philippine and Southeast Asian Food

### [[Food Studies - Philippine Regional Food - Colonial Histories and Politics of Taste - Review|Regional Food, Power, and Identity in the Philippines: Colonial Histories, Culinary Difference & the Politics of Taste]]
*Format: REVIEW (scholarly)*

Analysis of Philippine regional cuisine through the lens of food politics: how the hegemony of Manila/Tagalog culinary aesthetics within Philippine national food discourse systematically marginalizes or exoticizes regional cuisines from Ilocos, the Visayas, Mindanao, and the Cordillera highlands. "Unequal tables" operates as both literal (different tables — different regions' tables are not treated equally in national food discourse, restaurant culture, and media representation) and figurative (the political economy of who gets to define "Filipino food" for international audiences). The essay likely draws on food studies frameworks (Krishnendu Ray's work on the politics of taste and ethnic food; Angie Cruz and similar scholars on Philippine diaspora foodways) alongside postcolonial theory on cultural hegemony and the politics of representation.

**Key arguments:**
- The homogenization of "Filipino food" for export — the adobo-sinigang-lechon triumvirate — reflects Manila-centered Tagalog hegemony and erases the distinct culinary traditions of Ilocano pinakbet cooking, Visayan seafood repertoires, and Mindanao's Islamic food culture (no pork, halal preparation) — which share an island-nation but almost nothing else
- Colonial history is directly encoded in regional food hierarchies: Spanish colonial administration centered on Manila and valorized the culinary aesthetics associated with the colonial center; American colonialism added a different food-politics layer; the regions that never fully submitted to either colonial center retained more autonomous culinary identities
- The diaspora context transforms the politics: in North American and European Filipino diaspora communities, regional food identities become both more salient (as community markers) and more contested (national food identity as a resource for collective representation vis-à-vis the receiving society)

**Key concepts:** Philippine regional cuisine · Tagalog culinary hegemony · Ilocano · Visayan · Mindanao Islamic food culture · food politics · colonial food hierarchies · Filipino diaspora foodways · adobo politics

### [[Food Studies - Philippine Regional Food - Hegemony and the Politics of Belonging - Review|Unequal Tables: Philippine Regional Food, Hegemony, and the Politics of Belonging]]
*Format: REVIEW*

A companion piece to the Colonial Histories essay, this review examines Philippine cuisine through the analytical frame of hegemony and belonging. It argues that Filipino cuisine is a contested field shaped by ongoing canon formation in which Manila-Tagalog-Kapampangan food culture functions as the nationalizing center while Maranao and Bangsamoro foodways occupy morally and politically distinct positions within a national culinary hierarchy. Drawing on creolization theory, the essay reads Philippine food as a field of unequal cultural recognition rather than a unified national tradition. The analysis attends to how regional prestige is produced through colonial proximity, elite hospitality, and state-backed heritage narrativization.

**Key arguments:**
- The Manila-Tagalog corridor functioned as a nationalizing center, producing a hierarchy in which Pampanga kitchens gained authority through colonial proximity to Spanish and American administrative culture
- Bangsamoro foodways are positioned not merely as regional variants but as morally and politically distinct, marked by religious difference and uneven integration into the Philippine state
- Creolization frames Philippine cuisine better than "influence" because it foregrounds indigenization and the strategic forgetting of origins
- National culinary canon operates through selection and exclusion, marking uneven cultural recognition across regional food worlds

**Key concepts:** hegemony · creolization · Bangsamoro cuisine · Pampanga prestige kitchen · Filipino national canon · culinary belonging · colonial proximity · regional hierarchy

## Culinary Philology & Technique

### [[Food Studies - Chinese Cooking Verbs - Heat Medium and Sauce Taxonomy - Reference|Heat Medium and Sauce in Chinese Culinary Philology: A Cross-Language Taxonomy]]
*Format: REFERENCE*

A comparative taxonomy of Chinese heat-based cooking verbs, beginning with the contrast between wei (weiw) and kao and expanding into a system including dun, men, shao, zhi, bao, chao, zha, jian, liu, zheng, zhu, chuan, hui, pa, xun. The analysis shows that Chinese culinary verbs encode not only heat transfer but vessel logic, sauce reduction, texture, and regional practice. Comparisons with French culinary terminology reveal strongest correspondence where heat medium is physically clear; Chinese terms like shao, kao, liu, and hui compress multi-step processes that French distributes across several verbs. East and Southeast Asian comparators - Korean jorim, Vietnamese kho - often provide structurally closer matches for Chinese reduction-braise techniques than French does.

**Key arguments:**
- Chinese culinary verbs are denser than French equivalents, compressing vessel type, heat trajectory, sauce logic, and texture into single terms
- East and Southeast Asian vocabularies often provide structurally closer comparators for Chinese techniques than European culinary terms
- Cantonese terms (man, guk, zhuek, bao) carry distinct semantic histories that resist reduction to Mandarin-standard categories

**Key concepts:** heat-medium taxonomy · culinary verb semantics · French-Chinese comparison · jorim · kho · Cantonese culinary verbs

## Meal Time, Temporality & Food Systems

### [[Food Studies - Meal Timing - From Yongsun to the Three-Meal Day - Reference|From Yong-Sun to Breakfast, Lunch, and Dinner: Meal Timing, Labour, and Ritual]]
*Format: REFERENCE*

A cross-cultural historical inquiry into meal timing comparing the three-meal structure across the Western world, East Asia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Latin America. The central finding is that identical English labels conceal very different cultural grammars: Western industrial timing, East Asian staple-plus-dishes logic, South and Southeast Asian savory public breakfast culture, and Latin American midday comida primacy. The analysis traces how colonialism, religion, and urban work schedules reshaped meal timing globally. Classical Chinese yong-sun (morning and evening meals in the Zhou ritual economy) provides the long baseline, with the two-meal to three-meal transition traced through changing labor regimes and urbanization.

**Key arguments:**
- The three-meal day is not biological but a historically contingent temporal structure shaped by industrial labor organization, religious observance, and colonial institutional transfer
- Classical Chinese yong-sun encodes a two-meal ritual economy whose transformation tracks urbanization and merchant-class culture
- Colonialism spread institutional eating rhythms alongside ingredients, representing the temporal colonization of daily life
