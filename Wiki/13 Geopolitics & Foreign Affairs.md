## US Foreign Policy

### [external] [[New Republic - Guyer - One Big War - External|One Big War]]
*The New Republic* · Jonathan Guyer · 2026-05-01

The Trump administration has initiated multiple simultaneous conflicts — particularly with Iran and Venezuela — without coherent exit strategies, diplomatic frameworks, or congressional authorization. Guyer argues this reflects a structural preference for military pressure over sustained diplomacy, compounded by the dismantling of the State Department and USAID infrastructure. The piece frames the result as an accumulation of open-ended engagements: not wars in name, but wars in consequence.

### [external] [[Foreign Affairs - Cooper - Asia After America - External|Asia After America]]
*Foreign Affairs* · Zack Cooper · 2026

Zack Cooper argues that the US pivot to Asia has structurally failed: for 15 years successive administrations affirmed the need for deep regional engagement but failed to match words with action, creating a Lippmann gap between declared commitments and material investment. With the Trump administration's 2025 National Security Strategy narrowing US objectives to first-island-chain defense, China is filling the political and economic vacuum across Southeast Asia, South Asia, and the Pacific Islands. The article warns that US allies are beginning to reassess alignment decisions, and that the longer the gap persists the greater the risk of deterrence failure around Taiwan.

**Tags:** geopolitics · US-foreign-policy · Asia · China · Taiwan · deterrence · pivot-to-Asia · Trump

### [external] [[New Yorker - Marcus - End of Limits on Presidents Wars - External|The End of Limits on a President's Wars]]
*The New Yorker* · Ruth Marcus · 2026

Ruth Marcus argues that Trump's attack on Iran — conducted without congressional authorization — represents not merely a norm violation but the effective collapse of the legislative war-powers framework built up since the Vietnam era. Past presidents had already eroded that framework incrementally; Trump has collapsed it categorically. The piece reads the War Powers Resolution as now functionally dead and asks what institutional checks, if any, remain on a president's ability to initiate armed conflict.

### [external] [[Wired - Griffiths - What Would Secession Look Like - External|What Would Secession in America Look Like?]]
*Wired* · Ryan D. Griffiths · 2026-05-01

Political scientist Ryan Griffiths examines whether American secession — increasingly discussed in the wake of political violence and executive overreach — is practically achievable, and finds the structural obstacles overwhelming. Unlike historical secessionist movements, the US lacks geographically coherent red or blue blocs; the country is too intermixed for a clean territorial partition. Griffiths argues that while no current political leadership advocates secession, a disputed election or crisis could surface the question — and that the absence of a peaceful exit mechanism makes any serious secessionist episode inherently prone to violence.

### [external] [[New Yorker - Lepore - Presidential War Powers - External|Do U.S. Presidents Have the Power to Declare War?]]
*The New Yorker* · Jill Lepore · 2026

Jill Lepore traces the constitutional history of war-making authority from the framers' intention — that Congress alone could declare war — through the 1964 Tonkin Gulf Resolution's pivotal role in transferring practical authority to the executive. The piece argues that the ambiguity was not inadvertent but was engineered: the Resolution's open-ended language was deliberately crafted to give LBJ maximal flexibility while preserving the appearance of congressional sanction, establishing a template subsequent presidents have exploited. With any president now able to define an "emergency" unilaterally, Lepore reads the war-powers framework as functionally collapsed — and the constitutional debate as a question the courts have declined to answer.

**Tags:** US-foreign-policy · war-powers · constitutional-law · Congress · presidential-authority · Tonkin-Gulf · Vietnam  
**URL:** [source](https://www.newyorker.com/news/the-lede/do-us-presidents-have-the-power-to-declare-war)

### [external] [[New Yorker - Markham - Seeking Second Passport - External|Seeking a Second Passport]]
*The New Yorker* · Lauren Markham · 2026

Lauren Markham documents the growing movement of Americans pursuing ancestral citizenship in European countries as political insurance against an increasingly uncertain domestic future. Drawing on her own experience securing her daughter an Irish passport and interviews with heritage citizenship lawyers and applicants, the piece shows how the process — costly, bureaucratic, and dependent on documented ancestry — reproduces existing class and ethnic hierarchies even as it reflects a broadly felt anxiety about civic stability. The trend is read not as emigration but as hedging: a growing segment of Americans seeking an exit option they hope never to use.

**Tags:** US-politics · dual-citizenship · migration · political-anxiety · ancestry · immigration · passport

### [external] [[The Economist - American Chinese Submarines - External|American v Chinese Submarines]]
*The Economist* · 2026

A detailed assessment of undersea military competition in the Pacific, built around a visit to the USS Annapolis operating out of Guam. American nuclear attack submarines retain a decisive acoustic stealth advantage, but China is closing the gap faster than US industrial capacity can compensate: Chinese shipbuilding operates at four to five times the throughput of American yards, and Russian technology transfers have enabled rapid capability gains. The piece identifies the US response — AUKUS submarine partnerships, expanded drone and undersea sensor networks — as the beginning of a structural shift toward a distributed, alliance-based deterrence architecture rather than sole US dominance.

### [external] [[The Economist - The Telegram - External|The Telegram]]
*The Economist* · 2026-05-09

A historical essay using famous diplomatic blunders — the Zimmermann Telegram and its analogues — as a frame for reading contemporary leadership failure. The central argument is that catastrophic strategic errors are structural rather than individual: they emerge from decision-making processes in which institutional incentives suppress dissent, filter uncomfortable information, and reward certainty over accuracy. The piece warns that these conditions are present in multiple great powers today, and that the consequences, as with past empires' decline, tend to be felt long after the original misjudgment.

## European Politics & Liberal Democracy

### [external] [[The Economist - The Invention of Wales - External|The Invention of Wales]]
*The Economist* · 2026

Plaid Cymru's likely emergence as the Senedd's largest party in May 2026 marks a turning point for Welsh independence — from fringe fantasy to a position comparable to Scotland before the 2014 referendum. The Economist traces the intellectual and political history of Welsh nationalism, examining how a movement once too marginal to animate even a novelist's readership has reached 32% public support. The piece frames Wales as the third pillar of a UK constitutional crisis — alongside Scotland and Northern Ireland — now approaching structural, not merely rhetorical, seriousness.

Following Peter Magyar's electoral defeat of Viktor Orbán in April 2026, this piece examines how the leader of a small, poor country became the EU's most disruptive force for 16 years, blocking Ukraine aid, cultivating an international nationalist network, and undermining Brussels from within. *The Economist* · 2026

The EU's latest push to deepen its single market confronts the same structural obstacles that have frustrated integration since Delors: regulatory overproduction (€150bn per year in compliance costs), national interest divergence, and a fundamental gap between the EU's collective economic weight and its political capacity to coordinate. The piece maps the reform program — simplifying rules for cross-border services, reducing reporting burdens, harmonizing fragmented national standards — while acknowledging that the EU's decision architecture systematically favors the status quo. The question is whether external economic pressure from the US and China has finally produced sufficient political alignment to do what individual member states have repeatedly voted against.

### [external] [[New Yorker - Tharoor - Iran War Shifting Power to China - External|How the Iran War Is Shifting Power Toward China]]
*The New Yorker* · Ishaan Tharoor · 2026

The two-month US-Iran conflict has ended in a fragile ceasefire that has exposed, rather than restored, American power: an open-ended Gulf blockade, near-misses with Iranian forces, and a Pew finding that — for the first time in over two decades — a majority of Americans believe their country ignores the interests of others. Ishaan Tharoor tracks how China has capitalized on the crisis by staying out of the fight while studying American military vulnerabilities, building diplomatic credibility with adversarial and non-aligned states, and positioning itself as a responsible stakeholder by contrast. The article frames the Iran war not as a geopolitical win or loss but as an acceleration of the US-China power transition that was already underway.
