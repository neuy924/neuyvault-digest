## Evolution, Palaeontology & Natural History

### [external] [[New Scientist - Fossils Reveal How Ancient Animals Lived - External|How Fossils Are Revealing How Ancient Animals Really Lived]]
*New Scientist* · 2026

Palaeontologist Dave Hone challenges popular images of dinosaurs — as pack hunters, as solitary apex predators, as large-scale cooperators — by applying the standards of modern zoological inference to the fossil record. The interview covers cooperative hunting in theropods (evidence is near-absent), the role of elaborate structures like Triceratops frills (likely social signaling), and why pterosaurs grew far larger than birds. Hone's key corrective is to stop treating dinosaurs as monsters and start treating them as organisms embedded in real ecological systems across 160 million years of evolutionary history.

### [external] [[New Yorker - Kolbert - What Happens When a Whale Is Born - External|What Happens When a Whale Is Born?]]
*The New Yorker* · Elizabeth Kolbert · 2026

Researchers with the Cetacean Translation Initiative accidentally witnessed and filmed a sperm-whale birth near Dominica — only the second ever recorded. The footage showed that group members cooperated to keep the newborn afloat and safe, including individuals not closely related to the mother, evidence of social care that cuts across kinship lines. Kolbert uses the event to reflect on what the coordinated response reveals about the depth and breadth of social bonds within matrilineal sperm-whale societies.

### [external] [[The Economist - Ancient DNA Human Change - External|Study of Ancient DNA Shows Human Change Was Rapid]]
*The Economist* · 2026

Ali Akbari and David Reich's *Nature* study of 15,836 ancient West Eurasian genomes spanning 18,000 years — representing a 14-fold increase in ancient DNA data — reveals that strong directional natural selection (previously considered rare in humans) was in fact a major driver of genetic change over the last 11,000 years. Many of the identified selection sweeps relate to disease resistance, metabolic adaptation, and lifestyle shifts associated with the Neolithic transition to agriculture. The results challenge the assumption that human genetic evolution effectively ceased with modernity, suggesting instead that our species has been continuously and rapidly shaped by environmental pressures far more recently than previously understood.

### [external] [[The Economist - Neanderthal Dental Surgery Siberia - External|Tooth from Siberian Cave Reveals Neanderthal Dental Surgery]]
*The Economist* · 2026-05-16

A 59,000-year-old Neanderthal tooth recovered from a Siberian cave bears marks consistent with deliberate drilling and cleaning — the earliest confirmed evidence of invasive dental intervention in any hominin. The tool marks show precision inconsistent with accidental damage, and the individual's survival of the procedure implies a functional understanding of infection and pain management. The discovery complicates the long-held image of Neanderthals as behaviorally simple, adding dental surgery to a growing list of cognitive and social capacities previously assumed exclusive to modern humans.

## Physics, Environment & Philosophy of Science

### [external] [[New Scientist - Everything Reduced to Quantum Physics - External|Can Everything Be Reduced to Quantum Physics?]]
*New Scientist* · 2026

Karmela Padavic-Callaghan uses two contrasting popular science books — one arguing all reality reduces to quantum fields, the other asserting that conscious experience is the fundamental substrate — as a frame for examining reductionism as the organizing assumption of physics. The piece questions whether the first-person character of experience can coherently be derived from any mathematical description of fields or particles, and what it would mean for scientific practice if it cannot. The article surfaces the philosophical stakes of a debate usually conducted within physics without acknowledgment of its metaphysical assumptions.

### [external] [[The Economist - Chernobyl at 40 - External|Chernobyl at 40]]
*The Economist* · 2026-04-25

Forty years after the world's worst nuclear disaster, Chernobyl continues to function as an active scientific site: workers monitor radiation migration through soil, water, and food chains; engineers pursue the slow decommissioning of the surviving reactors; and ecologists observe the surprising return of wildlife to the exclusion zone. Techniques developed at Chernobyl — filtering radionuclides from water sources, measuring exposure pathways through agricultural produce — have become standard tools for radiological emergency preparedness globally. The piece argues that managing a nuclear disaster's long tail is a multi-generational scientific and engineering project, not a media event.
