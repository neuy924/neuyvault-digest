## Visibility, Masculinity & the Body

### [[Queer Theory - Gay Asian Masculinity - Racial-Sexual Visibility and the Body - Read|The Body as Reply: Gay Asian Men, Masculinity & the Price of Being Seen]]
*Format: READ · Non-Technical collection*

Examines how gay Asian men navigate racial-sexual stereotyping in queer spaces — specifically the double structure where Asian gay men are simultaneously *feminized* (rendered sexually passive, decorative, submissive — the "rice queen" dynamic's complement) and *invisible* as agents of desire (their own desiring subject-position erased). "The body as reply" names the core argument: in the absence of other representational resources, the body itself becomes the speech act — gym-built muscularity, assertive cruising, tattooing, or the deliberate refusal of assigned roles as forms of counter-inscription. The essay likely engages with David Eng and Alice Hom on queer Asian racial formation, Richard Fung's analysis of Asian men in gay porn, and the sociological literature on sexual racism and racial preference in dating apps (Grindr data, OkCupid racial preference studies). It may also draw on Fanon's account of the racialized body as object of the white gaze, adapted to the queer sexual marketplace.

**Key arguments:**
- "Sexual racism" in queer spaces is not merely prejudice applied to sex but a *structural* feature of racially organized desire markets — racial preferences are not neutral personal tastes but learned responses to a racialized representational economy
- The feminization of Asian men in Western gay culture is not universal but specific to a post-WWII geopolitical-aesthetic formation; pre-war and non-Western gay Asian masculinity produces different self-understandings
- "The price of being seen" is double: invisibility in mainstream gay culture has a price (social erasure), but visibility as a racial-sexual type also has a price (hypervisibility on terms set by others) — the essay navigates this double bind

## Butler, Buddhist Ontology & Global South Formations

### [[Queer Theory - Melancholic Subject - Buddhist Ontology and the Global South - Review|Beyond the Melancholic Subject: Queer Theory, Buddhist Ontology, and the Global South]]
*Format: REVIEW (state-of-the-field)*

A state-of-the-field review arguing that the encounter between Anglo-American queer theory, Madhyamaka Buddhist philosophy, and the queer formations of Southeast Asia and the Global South produces theoretical resources unavailable within any single tradition. The article prosecutes three simultaneous "destabilising encounters" with the canonical Butler/Sedgwick/Foucault triad. First, the **queer of colour critique** (Muñoz, Puar, Haritaworn, Snorton, Berlant) demonstrates that the canon's melancholic subject is constituted by an unmarked whiteness and absent imperialism that reproduce racial liberalism even at the framework's most radical iterations — Muñoz's critique of Edelman's anti-futurity as "a luxury afforded only by those who are already in possession of a present"; Berlant's concept of the **impasse** as the normalisation of emergency under neoliberalism. Second, the **Global South queer formations** encounter (Filipino *bakla*, Indonesian *tomboi*, Thai *kathoey*) reveals that the homo/hetero binary central to the canon's architecture is a culturally specific rather than universal formation — across much of the Global South, sexuality is organised around **gender role** (masculine/feminine, active/receptive) rather than **object choice**. Third, **Madhyamaka Buddhist ontology** — *anattā* (non-self), Nāgārjuna's *śūnyatā* (emptiness), the Vimalakīrti Sūtra's teaching on gender emptiness — provides a more radical ontological ground for queer theory than the psychoanalytic-phenomenological frameworks Butler, Sedgwick, and Foucault inherit. The article develops a structural parallel between Butler's **passionate attachment** and the Buddhist concept of **upādāna** (clinging), argues that the two-truths framework resolves the political-dissolution tension that Buddhist ontology generates, and proposes the **bodhisattva ideal** — compassionate political action without attachment to outcome — as a model for queer political engagement adequate to conditions of trans healthcare denial, legal rollback, and necropolitical violence.

**Key arguments:**
- The canon's "melancholic subject" is not a universal subject — it is constituted by an unmarked whiteness (the foreclosed desire it incorporates is racialised) and a specific philosophical genealogy (Hegel's *Anerkennung*, Freud's libido economy, Lacan's Symbolic Order) that presupposes a kind of interiority and subject-formation not universal across cultures
- The homo/hetero binary is a historically specific Western formation (the 19th-century medicalisation of sexuality as a "type of person" rather than "type of act") — the *bakla*, *tomboi*, and *kathoey* formations show that sexuality can be organised entirely differently, around gender-role axes, without mapping onto the closet/disclosure structure Sedgwick's framework assumes
- The Buddhist institutional homophobia of the Global South is substantially a **colonial import** — Buddhist modernism's absorption of Victorian Protestant sexual ethics, presented as ancient doctrine (Cabezón's philological scholarship)
- The structural parallel between Butler's "passionate attachment" and Buddhist *upādāna* is the article's most precise theoretical contribution: both name the mechanism by which the subject clings to the conditions of its own suffering, and both face the political problem that relinquishing the clinging risks a dissolution the subject experiences as annihilation
- The **two-truths framework** (conventional truth / ultimate truth) resolves the political-dissolution tension: Butler's melancholic subject and the political urgency of trans rights operate at the conventional level with full force; Buddhist *anattā* provides the ontological ground at the ultimate level — the bodhisattva acts for justice without the melancholic attachment to outcome that makes political endurance structurally difficult

### [[Queer Theory - Melancholic Subject - Butler, Passionate Attachment, and Buddhist Anatta - Review|Emptying the Melancholic Subject: Butler's Passionate Attachment and the Buddhist Doctrine of Anattā]]
*Format: REVIEW (focused study)*

A focused companion piece to the broader state-of-the-field review above. Where *Beyond the Melancholic Subject* surveys the full encounter between queer theory, Global South formations, and Buddhist ontology, this article concentrates specifically on the **structural parallel between Judith Butler's "passionate attachment"** — the subject's investment in the norms that simultaneously constitute and harm it — **and the Buddhist concept of *upādāna*** (clinging, the third of the twelve links of dependent origination). The parallel is not merely metaphorical: both frameworks identify a mechanism by which the subject sustains the conditions of its own suffering because the attachment is structurally prior to any particular choice; both identify dissolution of the self-structure it produces as simultaneously the path to liberation and the most feared outcome; and both face the political problem of how to act against the norms that harm you without either the melancholic investment Butler describes or the quietism that Buddhist non-attachment is sometimes misread as licensing. The article develops the **Buddhist doctrine of *anattā*** (non-self) as an ontological ground capable of supporting queer political engagement without reproducing the melancholic structure — the self that is harmed by the heterosexual matrix is real at the conventional level and calls for justice, while the Buddhist analysis of its empty, processual, interdependently arising nature provides the ontological space to act without the passionate attachment to outcome that makes Butler's melancholic subject structurally unable to sustain political endurance.

**Key arguments:**
- Butler's passionate attachment and Buddhist *upādāna* name the same structural phenomenon from different theoretical frameworks: the subject's constitutive clinging to what simultaneously forms and harms it
- *Anattā* as an ontological claim is not the same as political nihilism — the two-truths framework distinguishes the conventional reality of political harm and the urgency of trans rights from the ultimate reality of the empty, processual self; both are true on their respective registers
- The Buddhist practice of working with *upādāna* — not through suppression or forced detachment but through clear seeing of its structure — offers a model for queer political engagement that is neither the melancholic attachment Butler describes nor the spiritual bypass of political action
- The Vimalakīrti Sūtra's non-dual teaching on gender emptiness (neither male nor female in ultimate reality, while both remain conventionally real and politically significant) provides a specifically Buddhist resource for trans theory that does not require Western psychoanalytic frameworks

**Key concepts:** passionate attachment · *upādāna* · *anattā* · two-truths doctrine · Butler's *The Psychic Life of Power* · Vimalakīrti Sūtra · gender emptiness · dependent origination · political engagement without attachment · melancholy · queer theory

### [[Queer Theory - Sampat - Sinophone Social-Typing and Reclamation - Read|三八（sampat）與那些看起來不夠體面的重要關係]]
*Format: READ (Chinese-language primary) · Non-Technical collection*

Title translates as "Sampat (*三八*) and the Important Relationships That Don't Look Respectable Enough." 三八 (*sānbā*) is a Chinese/Hokkien-derived social type — applied to a woman (or feminized/gender-nonconforming figure) seen as loud, excessive, boundary-crossing, socially undignified, and transgressive of respectability norms; the term is simultaneously descriptive, pejorative, and (in reclamation) a badge of honor. *Sampat* is the Malay and Singaporean cognate — absorbed from Hokkien into Singapore/Malaysian Chinese and Malay vernacular, where it attaches to a specific figure: the socially-unruly woman who refuses proper feminine decorum. The essay's core move is rehabilitation: the "important relationships" that 三八/sampat figures inhabit — messy friendships, chosen families, non-normative queer bonds, care relationships outside the family — are precisely the relationships that social respectability discourse dismisses as insufficiently serious or stable. By centering these relationships as both important and legitimate, the essay performs a queer critique of respectability politics in the Sinophone context.

**Key arguments:**
- 三八/sampat is not merely a type but a *relational accusation*: it names the transgression of proper social distance — the 三八 figure is too loud, too close, too present, too much in spaces where women should be invisible or decorative
- The "important relationships" in the title are a taxonomic correction: the essay insists that relationships that look like gossip networks, informal care chains, queer platonic partnerships, or "unnecessary" intensities are in fact the social infrastructure that normative kinship discourse fails to recognize
- Cross-cultural Hokkien/Mandarin/Malay circulation of *sampat/三八* reveals how the Sinophone diaspora carries gendered respectability norms across national borders, and how those norms get renegotiated in the diasporic context

### [[Queer Studies - Seoul - Queer Geography and Korean Bear Culture - Reference|Layered Queer Seoul: Selective Visibility, Spatial Duality & Bear Culture]]
*Format: REFERENCE (analytical)*

Urban ethnographic and platform-theoretical survey of Seoul's contemporary gay scene, with particular attention to the Itaewon/Jongno spatial duality and the evidence for Korean bear and bara cultures. The governing concept is **selective visibility**: Seoul's queer life is not simply hidden or fully public, but visible through overlapping layers of venue geography, digital platforms, Korean-language search terms, body-type community tags, and social discretion. Itaewon [이태원] / Homo Hill [호모힐] is characterized as the more international, visitor-readable, club-oriented district — legible through English-language nightlife guides and visible drag and nightlife infrastructure; Jongno 3-ga [종로3가] represents an older, more locally embedded gay ecology of small bars, *pocha* [포장마차], karaoke, and regular-based spaces that operate through social discretion rather than public visibility. The contrast is not merely geographic but structural: Itaewon is legible to foreign gaze; Jongno is legible to those with Korean language, local social codes, and established networks. Bear culture has identifiable Korean-language infrastructure (venue tags, community platforms, body-type search terms: 베어, 근육, 건장), while *bara* appears weaker as a public Korean scene and more likely circulates as imported manga/fandom aesthetics. Three analytical lenses are developed: platform-mediated queer life (apps, Instagram, KakaoTalk, Naver/Kakao maps as hybrid queer infrastructure), foreigner gaze versus local opacity, and body politics (bear identity intersecting with Korean masculinity ideals, muscularity, size, age, and cuteness).

**Key arguments:**
- The Itaewon/Jongno contrast represents two distinct regimes of queer urban life: international visibility vs local discretion; English-facing nightlife vs Korean-coded social intimacy — Western travel guides systematically overrepresent Itaewon and underread Jongno's local opacity
- Privacy is structural infrastructure, not merely personal caution: venue culture, platform use, social entry codes, and visitor etiquette are all organized around managing outing risk — the primary safety concern is not violence but exposure
- Bear culture in Seoul has real Korean-language infrastructure (베어바, 게이 베어 community platforms), but intersects with distinctly Korean body ideals: *geunjang* 건장 (muscular/sturdy), *tongtong* 통통 (chubby/cute), and age — making it non-identical with Western bear aesthetics
- Apps, Naver Maps, Kakao Maps, Instagram, and X create a layered queer geography that exists partially independently of physical venues — the "scene" is distributed across platforms as much as places

### [[Queer Theory - Queer Slang - Authorship Platform Ecology and Erotic Sorting - Reference|Queer Slang as Survival, Style, Desire, and Digital Sorting Across Cultures]]
*Format: REFERENCE*

A sociolinguistic analysis treating gay and queer slang as a complex system rather than a simple vocabulary of identity or humor. The analysis maps key theorists of code-switching, indexicality, accommodation, markedness, linguistic capital, and postcolonial hybridity onto Western and East Asian queer terminology, then identifies three underexamined lenses: authorship and erasure, digital platform ecology, and desire-based exclusion. Across these lenses, queer slang is shown to be produced by specific marginal communities - Black and Latinx ballroom houses, drag performers, trans women, lesbian and sapphic communities, sex workers, and online queer youth - before being absorbed into mainstream gay, pop-cultural, or corporate speech, with original sources often erased. Digital platforms transform queer language into searchable tags, profile shorthand, algorithmic signals, and moderation workarounds. Erotic slang ranks bodies through race, masculinity, body type, age, class, HIV status, and sexual role.

**Key arguments:**
- Queer slang originates overwhelmingly in specific marginal sub-communities but undergoes systematic source erasure as it enters mainstream circulation
- Digital platforms do not merely distribute queer language but transform it - turning identity vocabulary into algorithmic signals, moderation workarounds, and monetizable identity categories
- Erotic slang functions as a sorting mechanism: it encodes hierarchies of racial desirability, body type, masculinity performance, and sexual role that reproduce broader social inequalities within queer communities
- The same term can simultaneously mark belonging, exclude others, perform authenticity, and enable surveillance - these functions are not separable but structural
