**Shared insight:** Language does not simply describe historical change — it *is* historical change. The sediment layers in words are primary evidence. **Shared question:** Is the Western "Buffered Individual" (Taylor's concept, taken up in Mead/Strathern anthropology) a universal model of personhood, or a historically and culturally specific formation? Whether this is liberatory or depersonalizing is the open question.

## 12. Colonial Language, Postcolonial Subjectivity & Diaspora Accent

A thread running through comparative linguistics, postcolonial theory, and diaspora identity: how colonial language hierarchies enter the psyche, shape what counts as legitimate speech, and persist in the body long after migration. **Shared question:** Who gets to speak, in which language, and with whose accent, as a *legitimate* person? The Fanon-Bhabha-Bourdieu nexus (colonial language as psychic prosthesis; mimicry; linguistic capital) provides the theoretical spine. Malaysian English's "transnational repertoire" and the Itaewon/Jongno legibility gap demonstrate that the colonial language hierarchy does not dissolve with migration — it restructures along new axes: native-speaker prestige, class coding, colonial memory, and loss of local intimacy.

**Key tension:** The diaspora speaker is caught between two impossible positions: speaking the colonizer's language "too well" (mimicry — almost the same, but not quite) and speaking the home language "too local" (lack of cultural capital in the receiving society). Neither position is stable. What Bhabha calls the "third space" of enunciation — the hyphen between cultures — is not a comfortable hybrid but an unstable zone of contested social recognition.
