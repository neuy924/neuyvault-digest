# Malaysian English Abroad and the Social Life of Accent Perception

## Abstract in English

This conversation examined how a Malaysian or Singaporean English speaker’s accent may change, persist, or be socially reinterpreted after long residence in the US, UK, or Ireland. The discussion moved from a general “returned accent” scenario to a more precise account of long-term Malaysian diasporic speech: retained Malaysian phonology, reduced colloquial Malaysianisms such as “lah,” and a professional Anglophone register shaped by Irish, American, medical, academic, and institutional environments. The central argument was that accent should not be confused with register, particles, class performance, or cultural loyalty. Using World Englishes, Communication Accommodation Theory, indexicality, linguistic capital, postcolonial theory, and sociophonetics, the conversation reframed the issue as one of transnational repertoire formation rather than accent loss or cultural betrayal. It also showed why Irish/American listeners may hear “Malaysian,” while Malaysian listeners may hear “diasporic,” “professional,” “neutralised,” or “less colloquial.”

## Major Themes

The first major theme was that **accent is relational**. A speaker does not simply “have” an accent in isolation; an accent is heard against the listener’s own categories.

The second theme was that **World Englishes corrects native-speaker bias**. Malaysian English should not be treated as defective American, British, or Irish English.

The third theme was that **absence of “lah” is not absence of Malaysian identity**. “Lah” is a discourse-pragmatic marker, while accent is carried by deeper phonological and prosodic structures.

The fourth theme was that **professional register can be mistaken for cultural distance**. A medical or academic voice may sound less colloquial because it is shaped by institutional clarity and authority.

The fifth theme was that **listeners overread accents**. A listener may infer ethnicity, class, schooling, migration history, loyalty, or arrogance from speech patterns that may simply reflect accommodation, intelligibility pressure, or long-term habit formation.

The sixth theme was that **“dual culture” is too blunt**. Better labels include transnational professional repertoire, Malaysian diasporic English, transnational Malaysian-Anglophone identity, and World Englishes repertoire with long-term accommodation.

## Preamble

The key point: the accent is not just a sound change. In Malaysia/Singapore, English accent indexes education, class, race, colonial history, cosmopolitan mobility, “localness,” and sometimes moral character. The listener’s irritation is often a reaction to what the accent appears to *mean*, not only how it sounds.

## Topic Map

The phenomenon sits at the intersection of:

1. **Phonetic accommodation** — people unconsciously adjust speech to interlocutors and environments.
   Source: Howard Giles, Nikolas Coupland, Justine Coupland, Communication Accommodation Theory; earlier Giles et al., journal article, 1973.

2. **Code-switching and style-shifting** — accent can work like a code, signalling setting, audience, topic, stance, and identity.
   Source: Blom & Gumperz, chapter, 1972; John Gumperz, *Discourse Strategies*, monograph, 1982; Penelope Gardner-Chloros, *Code-switching*, monograph, 2009.

3. **Linguistic capital and symbolic violence** — some accents carry market value; others are made to feel “less proper.”
   Source: Pierre Bourdieu, “The Economics of Linguistic Exchanges,” article, 1977; *Language and Symbolic Power*, monograph, 1991.

4. **Postcolonial mimicry and alienation** — adopting the accent of a former imperial centre can be read as aspiration, insecurity, or strategic survival.
   Source: Frantz Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, monograph, 1952; Homi Bhabha, “Of Mimicry and Man,” article, 1984; *The Location of Culture*, monograph, 1994.

5. **Indexicality** — an accent points beyond phonetics to social meanings: “educated,” “pretentious,” “Westernised,” “elite,” “fake,” “global,” “disloyal,” or “not local enough.”
   Source: Michael Silverstein, “Indexical Order and the Dialectics of Sociolinguistic Life,” article, 2003; Penelope Eckert, “Variation and the Indexical Field,” article, 2008; Kathryn Woolard, *Singular and Plural*, monograph, 2016.

## Deep Analysis

### 1. What is actually changing?

An “American” or “British” accent may involve several layers: vowel quality, rhoticity, intonation, stress timing, pitch movement, lexical choices, discourse markers, pragmatic habits, and even facial/jaw posture. A Singaporean or Malaysian speaker returning from the US/UK may not have acquired a full new accent; they may have acquired selected features.

For example, a returned speaker might keep Singaporean/Malaysian English grammar and rhythm but adopt American rhotic /r/, American intonation, British vowel shifts, “like,” “right,” “mate,” “cheers,” “gonna,” or UK/US hesitation patterns. Listeners often hear these selectively and exaggerate their social meaning.

So the phenomenon is not binary: it ranges from genuine phonetic drift to situational style-shifting to conscious performance.

## Case 1: Years Abroad

After several years in the US or UK, accent change is sociolinguistically unsurprising.

### Giles: accommodation as adaptation

Giles’ Communication Accommodation Theory explains this through **convergence**: speakers adjust toward the speech of people around them to reduce social distance, increase intelligibility, or gain acceptance. Over years, repeated short-term convergence can fossilise into durable accent change.

This does not require vanity or self-hatred. A Malaysian/Singaporean student or professional may change accent because local interlocutors keep misunderstanding them, because workplace norms reward “standard” American/British pronunciation, or because accommodation becomes automatic.

But CAT also allows **divergence**. Some speakers deliberately retain Malaysian/Singaporean English features abroad to mark identity, resist assimilation, or bond with co-nationals.

Source: Giles et al., “Towards a Theory of Interpersonal Accommodation through Language,” journal article, 1973; Giles & Powesland, *Speech Style and Social Evaluation*, monograph, 1975.

### Bourdieu: accent as convertible capital

Bourdieu helps explain why some accents have more value. A British or American accent can function as **linguistic capital** in corporate, academic, diplomatic, or media settings. It may signal international education, elite mobility, and access to high-status institutions.

But this is not neutral. It implies a **linguistic market** in which local Englishes may be treated as less authoritative even when they are fully functional. The returning accent therefore carries class meaning. The listener may not merely hear “different pronunciation”; they hear a person returning with a newly acquired symbolic asset.

Source: Bourdieu, “The Economics of Linguistic Exchanges,” article, 1977; *Language and Symbolic Power*, monograph, 1991.

### Fanon: alienation through the metropolitan voice

Fanon is useful but must be used carefully. In *Black Skin, White Masks*, he examines how colonised subjects may internalise the prestige of the coloniser’s language and voice. Applied to Malaysia/Singapore, a UK/US accent may be read as a desire to occupy a metropolitan or “white-coded” symbolic position.

This does not prove the speaker is alienated. It explains why the listener may *suspect* alienation: “You went away and came back sounding like you think you are above us.” The emotional charge comes from colonial hierarchy being replayed in the mouth.

Source: Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, monograph, 1952.

### Bhabha: mimicry as “almost the same, but not quite”

Bhabha’s concept of **mimicry** fits the ambivalence. A returned speaker may sound “almost British” or “almost American,” but not completely. That partial imitation can be socially unstable. It may be admired, mocked, envied, or resented.

Mimicry is threatening because it reveals that prestige accents are not natural essences; they are repeatable performances. The mimic both confirms and destabilises the authority of the prestige centre.

Source: Bhabha, “Of Mimicry and Man,” article, 1984; *The Location of Culture*, monograph, 1994.

### Gumperz, Blom, Gardner-Chloros: accent as contextual code

Gumperz and Blom show that switching between speech styles is not random. It carries **contextualization cues**: it tells the listener how to interpret the situation. A returned accent may cue “professional mode,” “foreign-educated mode,” “cosmopolitan mode,” or “not-local mode.”

Gardner-Chloros is helpful for treating accent shift as part of a broader multilingual repertoire. In Malaysia/Singapore, speakers already move among English, Malay, Mandarin, Tamil, Cantonese, Hokkien, Singlish, Manglish, and other varieties. A UK/US accent may be another resource in that repertoire, not a total identity replacement.

Source: Blom & Gumperz, “Social Meaning in Linguistic Structures,” chapter, 1972; Gumperz, *Discourse Strategies*, monograph, 1982; Gardner-Chloros, *Code-switching*, monograph, 2009.

## Case 2: Weeks Abroad

A strong accent after only weeks is interpreted differently.

### Phonetic change can happen quickly, but durable accent replacement is less plausible

Short-term accommodation can happen rapidly. People pick up intonation, lexical items, and pronunciation habits from interlocutors, sometimes within a conversation. But a stable, full American/British accent after a few weeks is less likely to be deep phonological restructuring.

So in Case 2, listeners are more likely to interpret the new accent as **performance**, **stylisation**, or **identity claim**, rather than genuine adaptation.

### Bell: audience design

Allan Bell’s **audience design** theory is useful here. Speakers adjust style depending on who they are speaking to, who is overhearing, and what identity they want to project. Someone returning after a short trip may not be “faking” in a simple sense; they may be orienting toward an imagined audience: foreign peers, global professionals, elite English users, or a self-image formed abroad.

Source: Bell, “Language Style as Audience Design,” journal article, 1984.

### Coupland: stylisation and persona

Nikolas Coupland’s work on **style** and **stylisation** helps explain the irritation. If the accent sounds overdone, listeners may hear it as theatrical: “You are performing Americanness/Britishness at us.” The offense comes not only from the foreign accent, but from perceived self-dramatisation.

Source: Coupland, *Style: Language Variation and Identity*, monograph, 2007.

### Rampton: crossing

Ben Rampton’s concept of **crossing** describes speakers using a variety associated with a group to which they do not straightforwardly belong. A Malaysian/Singaporean using an American/British accent after weeks abroad may be heard as “crossing” into a prestige identity without sufficient social legitimacy.

This is not the same as saying they are wrong to do so. It means the performance may be socially contested.

Source: Rampton, *Crossing: Language and Ethnicity among Adolescents*, monograph, 1995.

## Task 2: Why does the listener get annoyed or offended?

The listener’s annoyance is sociologically rich. It usually involves several overlapping reactions.

### 1. Authenticity policing

The listener may feel the speaker is no longer “being real.” In Malaysia/Singapore, where local English varieties are tied to intimacy, humour, informality, and solidarity, a sudden US/UK accent can sound like withdrawal from the local speech community.

The implicit accusation is: “You are refusing to sound like us.”

This is a classic authenticity conflict. Woolard’s distinction between **authenticity** and **anonymity** is useful. Local accents often derive authority from rootedness. Standard American/British accents derive authority from seeming placeless, institutional, and globally legible.

Source: Woolard, *Singular and Plural*, monograph, 2016.

### 2. Class resentment

The accent may be heard as class performance. In Singaporean terms, it may be read as “atas”; in Malaysian terms, as elite, urban, private-school, international-school, expatriate-adjacent, or socially distancing.

The listener may not object to pronunciation itself. They object to the implied hierarchy: “Your new accent says your life-world is more valuable than mine.”

Bourdieu would call this a struggle over legitimate language. The offended listener is resisting the idea that American/British English has more symbolic authority than local English.

### 3. Postcolonial wound

The reaction can be sharpened by colonial memory. British English in particular is not just another accent in Malaysia/Singapore. It is historically tied to administration, education, law, elite employment, and colonial hierarchy.

An American accent may carry a different but related prestige: global media, corporate power, tech, entertainment, and university prestige. The listener may hear not only “you changed,” but “you changed toward the dominant centre.”

This is where Fanon and Bhabha matter. The irritation can be a reaction to perceived mimicry of the powerful.

### 4. Betrayal of local intimacy

Local speech is often not merely communicative; it is affiliative. Singlish, Manglish, Cantonese-inflected English, Malay particles, Hokkien particles, Tamil-influenced English, and other local forms can signal warmth, informality, and shared background.

A returned accent may feel cold because it removes these cues. The listener may experience it as a rejection of shared intimacy.

Gumperz would frame this as a change in contextualization cues. The speaker thinks they are “just speaking normally.” The listener hears a change in footing: formal, superior, foreign, distant.

### 5. Suspicion of inauthenticity

The shorter the time abroad, the more likely the listener is to infer performance. After years abroad, “I picked it up” is plausible. After two weeks, a strong accent invites suspicion: “You are putting this on.”

But this suspicion can be unfair. Some people are highly phonetically suggestible, socially anxious, or trained by repeated correction. Others unconsciously accommodate to dominant speech patterns quickly.

The listener’s irritation may therefore reveal as much about the listener’s language ideology as about the speaker’s intention.

### 6. Threat to group boundaries

Accent change makes mobility audible. It says: this person has left, mixed elsewhere, and returned altered. That can threaten group continuity. The annoyed listener may be defending a boundary: local versus foreign, humble versus pretentious, real versus performed, us versus them.

This is especially strong in small, multilingual, status-conscious societies where speech gives away class, school background, ethnicity, cosmopolitan exposure, and social ambition.

## Red Team Review

**Potential weakness:** This analysis may over-politicise what is sometimes a mundane phonetic adaptation.
**Why it matters:** Not every returned accent is colonial mimicry or class performance. Some accent shift is automatic, practical, or caused by intelligibility pressure.
**How to test it:** Observe whether the speaker changes accent by audience. Do they use the UK/US accent with everyone, or mainly in formal, professional, foreign-facing, or status-sensitive contexts?

**Potential weakness:** The listener’s irritation may be framed too sympathetically.
**Why it matters:** Annoyance can become unfair policing of bilingual or transnational identity. A person who has lived abroad may genuinely sound different.
**How to test it:** Ask whether the listener would react similarly to other changes: clothing, vocabulary, confidence, manners, or partner choice. If yes, the accent may be a proxy for resentment toward mobility.

**Potential weakness:** “Malaysian/Singaporean” is too broad.
**Why it matters:** Malay, Chinese, Indian, Eurasian, international-school, local-school, elite, working-class, urban, and regional speakers are evaluated differently.
**How to test it:** Compare reactions across school background, ethnicity, profession, and whether the accent is British, American, Australian, or “international school” English.

## Known Unknowns

I cannot confirm the speaker’s intention from accent alone. The same accent shift could be unconscious accommodation, strategic professional adaptation, class performance, insecurity, parody, or identity experimentation.

I also cannot confirm whether the listener’s annoyance is justified without knowing the relationship, context, degree of accent change, and whether the speaker uses the accent selectively.

One correction: “index groups” is not a label I would confidently assign to Penelope Gardner-Chloros. The better theoretical pairing is Gardner-Chloros for code-switching, and Silverstein/Eckert/Woolard for indexicality and social meaning.

## Bottom Line

For **years abroad**, the new accent is linguistically plausible and may represent durable accommodation, changed habitus, and acquired linguistic capital.

For **weeks abroad**, a strong new accent is more likely to be heard as marked performance, stylisation, or status display, even if some short-term accommodation is real.

The listener’s annoyance is usually not about phonetics alone. It is about perceived betrayal, class signalling, colonial prestige, authenticity loss, and the fear that the returned speaker is using accent to renegotiate social rank.

# Deep Analysis

## 1. Kachru: Malaysia and Singapore are not peripheral to English

Braj Kachru’s World Englishes model separates English-speaking contexts into **Inner Circle**, **Outer Circle**, and **Expanding Circle**. The important move is that countries such as Malaysia and Singapore are not merely “foreign learners” of English. They belong to postcolonial English ecologies where English has institutional, educational, administrative, and multilingual functions. Kachru’s classic 1985 chapter is usually cited as “Standards, Codification and Sociolinguistic Realism: The English Language in the Outer Circle”; his later work further developed World Englishes as a field. ([Wiley Online Library][1])

This matters because the returned-accent scenario is often misread through an Inner Circle bias:

> “They went to the US/UK and came back with proper English.”

World Englishes reverses that hierarchy:

> “They moved from one legitimate English ecology into another, then returned with features from a different prestige system.”

So the real question is not whether the new accent is “correct.” The question is **which English norm is being activated, and in front of whom?**

### Applied to the scenario

A Malaysian/Singaporean speaker already has access to one or more English repertoires:

* local colloquial English: Singlish, Manglish, bazaar-like contact Englishes, ethnically inflected Englishes;
* standard local English: Singapore Standard English, educated Malaysian English;
* professional/international English;
* school-taught standard English;
* media-influenced American/British/Australian English;
* local elite/international-school English.

A returned American/British accent is therefore not a simple upgrade. It is a **repositioning within a plural English field**.

---

## 2. Schneider: postcolonial Englishes evolve through local history, not imitation alone

Edgar Schneider’s **Dynamic Model of Postcolonial Englishes** is especially useful because it treats postcolonial Englishes as evolving through contact, settlement, identity negotiation, nativisation, and eventual local norm formation. Schneider’s model foregrounds “language ecologies” and the historical relationship between settler/colonial groups and indigenous/local populations. ([pdcrodas.webs.ull.es][2])

His model is useful because Malaysian/Singaporean Englishes are not random mixtures. They emerged through:

* British colonial administration and education;
* multilingual contact among Malay, Chinese varieties, Tamil, English, and other languages;
* post-independence state-building;
* schooling systems;
* class stratification;
* global business and media;
* local identity formation.

[INFERENCE] In this frame, a US/UK accent returning home is not merely “foreign influence.” It is a **new exonormative pressure** entering an already nativised or partially endonormative English ecology.

### Key terms

| Term                          | Meaning                                                               | Relevance                                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Exonormative orientation**  | Norms are judged by external standards, often British/American        | “Your English is good because it sounds British/American.”                            |
| **Endonormative orientation** | Local norms become legitimate standards                               | “Singapore/Malaysian English can be correct in its own system.”                       |
| **Nativisation**              | English adapts to local phonology, grammar, pragmatics, and discourse | Local particles, rhythm, lexical items, discourse patterns become meaningful.         |
| **Norm conflict**             | Competing standards coexist                                           | The returned accent triggers conflict between local authenticity and global prestige. |

## 3. Malaysia: Malaysian English is not one thing

Malaysian English should not be treated as a single accent. Scholarly work on Malaysian English commonly discusses it as a lectal continuum, often distinguishing acrolectal, mesolectal, and basilectal forms. The “Many Faces of Malaysian English” article discusses Malaysian English through models including Kachru, Moag, and Schneider, and notes the acrolect/mesolect/basilect continuum. ([Wiley Online Library][3])

This matters because a returned British/American accent will be interpreted differently depending on the speaker’s original position:

| Speaker background                            | Likely listener interpretation of US/UK accent            |
| --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| International school / elite urban background | “Expected,” “atas,” “already like that”                   |
| Local public-school background                | “Changed,” “putting it on,” “forgot where they came from” |
| Professional corporate sector                 | “Useful,” “global,” “client-facing”                       |
| Close family/friends                          | “Why are you talking like that with us?”                  |
| Multilingual local setting                    | “You are refusing local intimacy cues”                    |
| Academic/legal/medical setting                | May be read as professional polish or foreign prestige    |

Malaysia also has a complex English-policy history, including recurring tensions around Malay-medium nationalism, English for global competitiveness, and English-medium education pressures. Gill’s 2005 article on Malaysian language policy discusses a policy reversal toward English-medium instruction in 2002, framed by pressures from internationalisation and higher education. ([Springer][4])

So in Malaysia, a returned US/UK accent can index not only “foreignness,” but also:

* class mobility;
* private education;
* urban elite identity;
* colonial residue;
* professional advantage;
* possible Malay/Chinese/Indian intra-national difference;
* local versus global aspiration.

## 4. Singapore: the issue is sharpened by Singlish versus “Good English”

Singapore is even more ideologically charged because English is both a working language and a site of state discipline. Singapore’s bilingual policy emphasizes English plus officially assigned “mother tongue” languages — Mandarin, Malay, or Tamil — and English has been central to education and national economic strategy. The National Library Board describes bilingualism as a cornerstone of Singapore language policy since PAP came to power in 1959. ([NLB][5])

The **Speak Good English Movement** was launched in 2000 amid official concern about Singlish and standard English. A 2024 scholarly reflection states that SGEM was launched in 2000 because of official concern about widespread Singlish and its implications; the National Archives record Prime Minister Goh Chok Tong’s launch speech on 29 April 2000. ([Taylor & Francis Online][6])

This creates a split ideology:

| Variety                        | Common official/economic valuation                 | Common identity valuation                  |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Standard Singapore English     | Educated, useful, globally intelligible            | Acceptable, professional                   |
| Singlish                       | Often treated as problematic in official discourse | Local, intimate, humorous, authentic       |
| British/American English       | Global, prestigious, media/corporate-coded         | May sound elite, foreign, performative     |
| “International school” English | High-status, mobile, cosmopolitan                  | May sound socially distant or class-marked |

The returned accent therefore lands in a society already trained to hear English through a double bind:

> Be globally intelligible, but do not sound like you reject local belonging.

That is why the listener’s annoyance can be intense. The speaker may think: “I’m just speaking clear English.”
The listener may hear: “You are choosing global prestige over local solidarity.”

## 5. Why World Englishes changes the moral interpretation

Without World Englishes, the scenario is often interpreted like this:

> Local accent → inferior.
> UK/US accent → superior.
> Returned speaker → improved or pretentious.

With World Englishes, the interpretation changes:

> Local English → legitimate variety with local norms.
> UK/US accent → another prestige-coded variety, not inherently superior.
> Returned speaker → moving between unequal but coexisting English markets.

This distinction matters. It prevents two errors:

1. **Native-speaker worship** — assuming British/American English is the natural endpoint of good English.
2. **Authenticity policing** — assuming any US/UK accent feature means betrayal, fakery, or colonial self-hatred.

The stronger analysis asks: **what social work is the accent doing?**

Braj B. Kachru and Edgar W. Schneider are two of the central architects of World Englishes theory, but they do different jobs.

For Malaysian/Singaporean English, Kachru tells us: **do not treat local English as defective British/American English.** Schneider tells us: **ask where that local English sits in a historical process of contact, identity formation, and norm stabilisation.**

## Brief Biography

**Braj Bihari Kachru** was an Indian-American linguist, born in Srinagar, Kashmir, in 1932 and later based at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. A Springer reference entry published in 2025 describes him as educated at Deccan College, the University of Allahabad, and the University of Edinburgh, and as having held positions at Illinois including Professor of Linguistics, Jubilee Professor of Liberal Arts and Sciences, and Professor at the Center for Advanced Study. The University of Illinois Center for Advanced Study profile states that he “pioneered, shaped, and defined” the scholarly field of World Englishes and was founder and co-editor of the journal *World Englishes*. ([Springer][1])

Kachru’s intellectual position is partly biographical: he was not writing from the centre of native-speaker English authority, but from the perspective of a multilingual South Asian scholar who understood English as a language that had been appropriated, refunctioned, and indigenised by postcolonial societies. That matters because his theory is not simply descriptive; it is a challenge to the hierarchy that treats native-speaker English as the only legitimate model.

# 2. Kachru’s Theory of World Englishes

## 2.1 The Three Circles Model

Kachru’s best-known model divides global English into three broad circles:

| Circle               | Description                                                        | Examples                                         | Norm status                  |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| **Inner Circle**     | Historical native-speaker centres                                  | UK, US, Ireland, Canada, Australia, New Zealand  | Norm-providing               |
| **Outer Circle**     | Postcolonial societies where English has institutional functions   | India, Singapore, Malaysia, Nigeria, Philippines | Norm-developing              |
| **Expanding Circle** | Countries where English is mainly a foreign/international language | China, Japan, Korea, much of Europe              | Traditionally norm-dependent |

The Open University’s educational overview describes Kachru’s three-circle model as influential for understanding the varieties and functions of English worldwide; a World Englishes overview similarly describes it as the most influential model of English’s spread and links the circles to norm-providing, norm-developing, and norm-dependent statuses. ([The Open University][6])

The key point is not geography alone. It is **norm authority**. Kachru’s intervention was to say that Outer Circle Englishes are not merely imperfect learner Englishes. They are institutionalised, socially embedded, and capable of developing their own norms.

For Malaysia and Singapore, this is decisive. They are not simply places where people fail to speak British English. They are Outer Circle contexts in which English has long-standing institutional, educational, administrative, and multilingual functions.

## 2.2 “The Other Tongue”

Kachru’s phrase **“the other tongue”** is conceptually powerful. It contests the idea that English is either a mother tongue or a foreign tongue. In postcolonial contexts, English can be:

* not ancestral, but deeply local;
* not native in the Inner Circle sense, but institutionally embedded;
* not foreign, but also not ethnically primordial;
* not merely learned, but appropriated and transformed.

This is why World Englishes theory is useful for Singaporean and Malaysian English. English may be colonial in origin, but its later forms are not merely colonial leftovers. They become media of local humour, bureaucracy, schooling, literature, class mobility, and peer intimacy.

## 2.3 The “alchemy” of English

The title *The Alchemy of English* captures Kachru’s main claim: English changes when it enters new multilingual ecologies. It is not simply transmitted intact. It is transformed.

That transformation happens across:

* phonology: local accent systems;
* lexis: local words, borrowings, calques;
* syntax: substrate-influenced structures;
* discourse: local particles, pragmatic conventions, politeness norms;
* literature: local idioms and cultural references;
* identity: English as Indian, Singaporean, Malaysian, Nigerian, etc.

Kachru therefore treats bilingual and multilingual users as **creative agents**, not deficient imitators.

## 2.4 Observations and Findings

Kachru’s major findings are not laboratory “findings” in a narrow experimental sense. They are sociolinguistic observations and theoretical claims:

1. **English has plural centres.** British and American English are historically important, but they are not the only legitimate norms.

2. **Outer Circle Englishes are institutionalised.** They are used in law, education, bureaucracy, media, literature, and interethnic communication.

3. **Non-native users are not peripheral.** They may outnumber native speakers and use English in domains that matter socially and politically.

4. **Deviation is not always error.** A local feature may be systematic, meaningful, and norm-governed within its speech community.

5. **English is culturally resemanticised.** It carries local metaphors, idioms, values, and literary traditions.

6. **Native-speakerism is an ideology.** The belief that only native speakers provide valid norms is not a neutral linguistic truth.

## 2.5 Why Kachru matters for returned accents

In your original scenario — Malaysian/Singaporean goes to US/UK and returns with an American/British accent — Kachru changes the frame.

A weak analysis says:

> They went abroad and came back with better or worse English.

A Kachruvian analysis says:

> They moved between English norm-systems: Malaysian/Singaporean English, British/American English, global professional English, and local intimate English.

This means the question is not “Is the new accent correct?” The question is:

> Which English market is the speaker now orienting toward, and what local norm are they distancing from?

# 3. Edgar W. Schneider

## Brief Biography

**Edgar W. Schneider** is an Austrian-born German linguist and Emeritus Professor of English Linguistics at the University of Regensburg. His University of Regensburg profile states that he held the Chair of English Linguistics there from 1993 to March 2020, after appointments at the Free University of Berlin, the University of Georgia, the University of Trier, and the University of Bamberg; it also notes that he taught at the National University of Singapore for three semesters between 2021 and 2023. ([University of Regensburg][7])

Schneider is best known in World Englishes for the **Dynamic Model of Postcolonial Englishes**, first articulated in major form in the 2003 *Language* article “The Dynamics of New Englishes: From Identity Construction to Dialect Birth” and then elaborated in *Postcolonial English: Varieties Around the World* in 2007. ERIC’s abstract for the 2003 article summarises it as proposing five phases: foundation, exonormative stabilisation, nativisation, endonormative stabilisation, and differentiation. ([ERIC][8])

# 4. Schneider’s Dynamic Model

## 4.1 What problem is Schneider solving?

Kachru gives a macro-map of Englishes. Schneider asks a more historical question:

> Why do postcolonial Englishes in very different places show similar developmental patterns?

He argues that New Englishes emerge through repeated sociolinguistic processes: contact, bilingualism, identity negotiation, accommodation, local norm formation, and structural stabilisation.

An Oxford Research Encyclopedia entry on World Englishes describes Schneider’s Dynamic Model as arguing that, despite geographical and historical differences, similarities in the emergence of new English varieties can be identified, grounded in sociolinguistic accommodation and identity transformations. ([OUP Academic][12])

## 4.2 The Five Phases

| Phase | Name                            | Main process                                            | Norm orientation          | Linguistic effect                                                          |
| ----: | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
|     1 | **Foundation**                  | English enters a new territory through colonial contact | External                  | Early borrowing, contact, interpreters, limited bilingualism               |
|     2 | **Exonormative stabilisation**  | English gains institutional role under colonial rule    | External, usually British | Local elites learn English; external norms dominate                        |
|     3 | **Nativisation**                | English adapts to local languages and identities        | Mixed                     | Local phonology, syntax, discourse features emerge                         |
|     4 | **Endonormative stabilisation** | Local English gains acceptance and confidence           | Local                     | Local norms become legitimate; dictionaries, literature, public acceptance |
|     5 | **Differentiation**             | Internal varieties emerge within the local English      | Local but plural          | Regional, ethnic, class, and stylistic differentiation                     |

Cambridge material on Schneider’s *Postcolonial English* states that chapter 3 outlines the Dynamic Model and connects it to language-contact settings, colonisation types, social identity, and linguistic accommodation. ([Cambridge Assets][13])

## 4.3 Key Mechanism: Identity Construction

Schneider’s model is not only linguistic. It is also identity-based.

The central idea is that English changes as the identities of colonisers, settlers, indigenous populations, and later citizens change. A colonial language becomes local when people stop treating it only as the language of outsiders and begin using it to express local belonging.

This is why Schneider’s model is more dynamic than Kachru’s circles. Kachru classifies world English contexts. Schneider models movement through time.

## 4.4 Observations and Findings

Schneider’s core observations:

1. **Postcolonial Englishes are historically patterned.** They do not emerge randomly.

2. **Language change follows social ecology.** Demography, education, colonial type, bilingualism, policy, and identity all matter.

3. **Nativisation is normal.** Local accent, vocabulary, grammar, and discourse features are predictable outcomes of contact.

4. **Complaints about “corruption” are a phase symptom.** During nativisation, local forms are often attacked as errors before they are recognised as norms.

5. **Endonormativity marks confidence.** A society reaches a stronger phase when it accepts local English features as legitimate.

6. **Differentiation follows stabilisation.** Once a local English is established, it can develop internal ethnic, regional, class, and stylistic varieties.

## 4.5 Why Schneider matters for Malaysia/Singapore

For Malaysia and Singapore, Schneider’s model asks:

* How did English enter through colonial administration and schooling?
* When did English become a local institutional resource?
* Which local features became stabilised?
* Which features are still stigmatised as “broken English”?
* Is local English accepted as a norm, or does the society still look to Britain/America?
* Are Singlish/Manglish informal varieties moving toward recognition, or still excluded from formal legitimacy?

Singapore is especially interesting because Standard Singapore English and Colloquial Singapore English/Singlish occupy different symbolic positions. Gupta’s classic 1989 article describes Singapore English as diglossic: proficient users often use Singapore Colloquial English informally and Standard English in writing/formal settings. ([Taylor & Francis Online][14])

Malaysia is differently structured: Malaysian English sits inside a multilingual national ecology shaped by Malay, English, Chinese varieties, Tamil, education policy, ethnicity, and class. A 2012 article titled “The Many Faces of Malaysian English” explicitly treats Malaysian English through World Englishes models and discusses its multiple forms rather than a single homogeneous variety. ([Wiley Online Library][15])

# 6. Application to World Englishes Generally

## What they jointly overturn

Together, Kachru and Schneider attack the older “deficit” view:

> British/American English = correct English.
> Other Englishes = learner error, colonial residue, or imperfect imitation.

They replace it with a plural model:

> Englishes are historically situated, socially functional, and locally norm-governed.

## What they make visible

They make visible several things older models hide:

1. **Institutional function**
   English may be used in courts, universities, parliament, medicine, media, and business outside native-speaker countries.

2. **Local creativity**
   New Englishes generate literature, humour, idioms, particles, metaphors, and discourse styles.

3. **Norm conflict**
   Local users may still judge themselves by British/American standards even when their own English is functional and systematic.

4. **Colonial residue**
   Prestige hierarchies do not disappear after independence; they remain in schooling, hiring, accent prejudice, and class signalling.

5. **Identity ambivalence**
   English may be both colonial and local, elite and intimate, global and domestic.

# 7. Application to Malaysian/Singaporean Returned Accents

## Kachruvian reading

A Malaysian/Singaporean returning with a UK/US accent is not moving from “bad English” to “good English.” They are moving from one English norm-system to another.

The relevant contrast is:

| Local norm                    | External norm                      |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Malaysian/Singaporean English | British/American English           |
| Endonormative identity        | Exonormative prestige              |
| Local intimacy                | Global legibility                  |
| Multilingual pragmatics       | Native-speaker-coded pronunciation |
| Peer belonging                | Institutional/corporate authority  |

The listener’s annoyance may arise because the speaker appears to privilege external prestige over local legitimacy.

## Schneiderian reading

Schneider would frame the returned accent as a sign of tension between **endonormative stabilisation** and **exonormative pull**.

If a society has strong endonormative confidence, local English features can be accepted as legitimate. If exonormative prestige remains strong, speakers may still orient toward British/American accents as higher-value models.

So the returned accent can be read as:

* a practical accommodation to overseas life;
* a prestige performance;
* a retreat from local English norms;
* an unresolved colonial/postcolonial status signal;
* or simply an expanded repertoire.

## Case 1: Years abroad

Kachru: the speaker has acquired another legitimate English variety, not necessarily “better English.”
Schneider: the speaker is moving between norm systems; durable accent change is plausible through long-term exposure and accommodation.

## Case 2: Weeks abroad

Kachru: a sudden UK/US accent may reveal continuing prestige of Inner Circle norms.
Schneider: the speaker may be over-orienting to exonormative authority before any deep phonological shift has stabilised.

# 8. Criticisms and Limits

## Criticisms of Kachru

1. **The circles are too static.** Countries change. Singapore, for example, complicates the Outer Circle category because English is increasingly used as a home language by many speakers.

2. **The model is nation-based.** It underplays transnational migration, internet English, class, ethnicity, and mixed repertoires.

3. **Expanding Circle Englishes have changed.** English in places such as Europe, Korea, China, and Japan may be less simply “norm-dependent” than the original model suggested.

4. **Inner/Outer/Expanding may preserve hierarchy.** Even while critiquing native-speaker dominance, the circles can still make the Inner Circle look central.

## Criticisms of Schneider

1. **The model may be too linear.** Real societies may not pass neatly from one phase to the next.

2. **Settler/indigenous categories do not always fit.** Some postcolonial settings had little permanent settler population but strong institutional English.

3. **Different domains develop differently.** Formal writing, everyday speech, broadcast English, school English, and literary English may sit at different phases.

4. **Globalisation complicates endonormativity.** Even when local norms stabilise, American media, British schooling, international schools, and corporate English keep reintroducing external norms.

# Red Team Review

**Potential weakness:** Kachru and Schneider can make local Englishes sound more stable and codified than they are.
**Why it matters:** Malaysian English, Singapore Standard English, Singlish, Manglish, and elite international English are not equally accepted across schools, workplaces, families, and state institutions.
**How to test:** Separate domains: home, school, court, medicine, media, social media, corporate office.

**Potential weakness:** World Englishes can overcorrect against native-speaker norms.
**Why it matters:** There are real contexts where international intelligibility and accent accommodation matter.
**How to test:** Ask whether the feature improves communication or mainly signals prestige.

**Potential weakness:** Schneider’s model may underplay ongoing global media pressure.
**Why it matters:** A Singaporean/Malaysian speaker may orient to American English not through colonial history but through Netflix, TikTok, YouTube, gaming, university culture, and corporate tech English.
**How to test:** Track the source of the accent feature: school, peers, media, workplace, overseas immersion.

**Kachru** gives the ideological breakthrough: English is plural, locally owned, and not reducible to British/American correctness.

## Preamble

The four conflicts are related but not identical. **Native-speaker prestige** concerns which English is treated as legitimate. **Class mobility** concerns what the accent signals about status. **Colonial memory** concerns historical power and mimicry. **Loss of local intimacy** concerns relational betrayal: the sense that someone no longer wants to sound like “us.”

For Malaysia/Singapore, the strongest frame should remain **World Englishes**: Singaporean and Malaysian Englishes are not failed British/American Englishes; they are postcolonial Englishes with local norms, meanings, and histories. Kachru’s “Outer Circle” model explicitly argues for the sociolinguistic reality of institutionalised postcolonial Englishes, while Schneider’s Dynamic Model explains how such Englishes develop through colonial contact, nativisation, and eventual local norm-formation. Kachru, book chapter, 1985; Schneider, journal article, 2003 and monograph, 2007. ([Science and Education Publishing][1])

## Native-Speaker Prestige

Native-speaker prestige is the conflict over **whose English counts as proper English**. In this frame, the returned American/British accent is not merely a sound; it is an orientation toward the “Inner Circle” prestige of the US/UK. Kachru’s World Englishes theory is the key corrective here: it challenges the assumption that British or American English is the natural standard against which Malaysian or Singaporean English should be judged. Kachru’s 1985 chapter on standards, codification, and the Outer Circle is the classic source for this argument. ([Science and Education Publishing][1])

In the returned-accent scenario, native-speaker prestige becomes visible when the speaker appears to treat US/UK pronunciation as an upgrade. The listener may hear: “You now think their English is better than ours.” This is why the issue is not just phonetic accuracy. A person may genuinely have accommodated abroad, but the local listener may interpret the new accent as submission to external authority.

Schneider sharpens this through the distinction between **exonormative** and **endonormative** orientation. Exonormativity means looking outward to British/American norms; endonormativity means recognising local English as norm-bearing. Schneider’s Dynamic Model describes postcolonial Englishes as moving through phases including foundation, exonormative stabilisation, nativisation, endonormative stabilisation, and differentiation. Schneider, *Language*, journal article, 2003; *Postcolonial English*, monograph, 2007. ([let.rug.nl][2])

So native-speaker prestige is the most important conflict when the argument is about **correctness**, **accent legitimacy**, or whether Singaporean/Malaysian English is “real English.” It is less about personal betrayal and more about language ideology.

## Class Mobility

Class mobility is the conflict over **what the accent says about social rank**. A British or American accent may signal overseas education, elite schooling, professional polish, corporate cosmopolitanism, or access to transnational networks. Here the key theorist is Bourdieu: accent functions as **linguistic capital**, and linguistic capital can be converted into social, educational, and economic advantage. Bourdieu’s *Language and Symbolic Power*, monograph, 1991, develops language as a site of symbolic power rather than merely communication. ([Miguel A. Martínez][3])

In Malaysia/Singapore, this class reading can be especially sharp because English already indexes schooling, ethnicity, profession, urbanity, and global mobility. A returned US/UK accent may not simply sound foreign; it may sound **expensive**. It can index private education, international school culture, foreign university exposure, elite employment, or proximity to expatriate/global corporate worlds.

The listener’s annoyance may therefore be resentment against an implied hierarchy: “You are using your accent to place yourself above us.” This differs from native-speaker prestige. Native-speaker prestige is about **which norm is authoritative**; class mobility is about **who gains status from using that norm**.

Monica Heller is also relevant here. Her work on language and late capitalism treats language as commodified: a resource people use in service economies, tourism, education, corporate life, and global labour markets. Applied to returned accents, the American/British accent can become a form of personal branding. It says, or appears to say: “I am globally employable.”

## Colonial Memory

Colonial memory is the conflict over **history lodged in the voice**. British English in Malaysia/Singapore is not just another accent. It is historically tied to colonial administration, law, schooling, bureaucracy, elite formation, and social hierarchy. American English carries a different global prestige: media, technology, universities, finance, entertainment, and geopolitical power. The two are not identical, but both can be heard as voices of external authority.

Fanon is central here. In *Black Skin, White Masks*, monograph, 1952, Fanon analyses how the colonised subject may internalise the language and cultural authority of the coloniser. His discussion of language is not simply about grammar or pronunciation; it is about psychic and political proximity to whiteness and metropolitan legitimacy. ([Burawoy Lab][4])

Bhabha adds ambivalence. In “Of Mimicry and Man,” article, 1984, colonial mimicry is unstable because it produces a subject who is “almost the same” as the coloniser but not quite. This is why a returned British accent can be admired and mocked at the same time. It can sound prestigious, but also derivative; polished, but also anxious; globally mobile, but also postcolonially exposed. ([University of Warwick][5])

Colonial memory becomes the dominant conflict when the listener’s reaction is not merely “you sound posh,” but “you sound like you are trying to be ang moh / white / colonial / Western.” This is a different moral charge. It is not just class resentment; it is irritation at perceived mimicry of historical power.

## Loss of Local Intimacy

Loss of local intimacy is the conflict over **relationship and belonging**. This is often the most immediate reason friends or family get annoyed. Local Englishes are not just linguistic systems; they carry warmth, humour, teasing, particles, rhythm, pragmatic softness or bluntness, and shared social footing. When someone returns and speaks to old friends in a strong American/British accent, the listener may hear distance.

Gumperz is useful here because of **contextualization cues**. In *Discourse Strategies*, monograph, 1982, Gumperz analyses how prosody, code choice, rhythm, and discourse cues guide interpretation in interaction. A changed accent can therefore alter the perceived frame of the conversation: from peer intimacy to formality, from local belonging to foreign-facing performance. ([Cat Directory][6])

Woolard’s distinction between **authenticity** and **anonymity** is also important. In her 2016 book *Singular and Plural*, she analyses linguistic authority as grounded either in rooted, authentic belonging or in anonymous, socially unmarked authority. A local accent may derive authority from authenticity: “this is our voice.” A US/UK accent may derive authority from anonymity: “this sounds globally standard.” ([OUP Academic][7])

The listener’s complaint — “why are you talking like that with us?” — is usually an intimacy complaint. It does not necessarily mean the listener rejects all accent change. It means the speaker has used the wrong register for the relationship. Giles’ Communication Accommodation Theory is relevant because it explicitly studies how speakers converge or diverge in interaction and how listeners evaluate those adjustments. Giles, communication theory, originally developed from 1970s speech accommodation work and restated in later CAT literature. ([ScienceDirect][8])

## Comparison and Contrast

The four conflicts differ by what the accent is taken to threaten. **Native-speaker prestige** threatens local English legitimacy. **Class mobility** threatens status equality. **Colonial memory** threatens postcolonial dignity. **Loss of local intimacy** threatens relational closeness.

They also differ by social setting. In a workplace, the accent may be interpreted mainly through native-speaker prestige and class mobility: it sounds polished, global, client-facing, or elite. Among old friends, the same accent may trigger intimacy loss: it sounds like the speaker is refusing the shared local register. In politically or historically conscious settings, the same accent may trigger colonial memory: it sounds like mimicry of external power.

They also differ by timescale. After **years abroad**, accent shift is more plausibly explained by accommodation, exposure, and changed communicative habits. Listener irritation may still occur, but the moral charge is weaker unless the speaker appears to look down on local English. After **weeks abroad**, a strong accent is more likely to be heard as stylisation, performance, or premature claim to prestige. That makes class mobility and colonial mimicry more salient.

The most analytically precise answer is: **native-speaker prestige is the structural conflict; class mobility is the social-status conflict; colonial memory is the historical-symbolic conflict; loss of local intimacy is the interpersonal conflict.** In real life, the listener usually experiences them together, but one tends to dominate depending on relationship and context.

For Malaysia/Singapore, I would rank them conditionally. In close friendship or family settings, **loss of local intimacy** is often the proximate trigger. In elite education or workplace settings, **class mobility** and **native-speaker prestige** dominate. In explicitly postcolonial readings, especially around Britishness, **colonial memory** gives the reaction its deeper historical charge.
