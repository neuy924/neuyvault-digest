## Abstract in English

This essay surveys salted fish as a global food technology and historical object, moving from the Atlantic salt-cod economy to East Asian salted and fermented seafood traditions. It argues that salted fish should not be understood merely as preserved food, but as an infrastructure of trade, religion, empire, labour, rice economies, sensory identity, and cultural memory. In the Atlantic world, salt cod connected North Atlantic fisheries, Catholic fasting rules, maritime trade, Caribbean plantation provisioning, slavery, and later national cuisines such as Portuguese bacalhau and Jamaican ackee and saltfish. A critical historiographic frame draws on Harold Innis’s staples theory, Sidney Mintz’s political anthropology of food, Mark Kurlansky’s commodity biography, and Atlantic-world scholarship on slavery, provisioning, and racial capitalism. In East Asia, salted fish follows a different logic: it operates as a rice-compatible flavour technology, fermentation substrate, class marker, diaspora memory, and sometimes health-risk food. Cantonese 鹹魚, Japanese 熟れ鮨（なれずし） and 塩辛（しおから）, Korean 젓갈, kimchi seasonings, and Taiwanese 烏魚子 show how salted seafood mediates between freshness and decay, poverty and luxury, household memory and public smell politics, heritage and carcinogenic risk, visible food and invisible animal-derived seasoning.

## Key themes

The strongest recurring theme was that salted fish is a **boundary object**. It crosses boundaries between fresh and rotten, local and global, poor food and prestige food, preservation and taste, religion and commerce, ration and national dish, memory and risk.

A second major theme was **infrastructure**. In the Atlantic world, salted fish helped sustain ships, Catholic fasting markets, plantation labour, and empire. In East Asia, salted fish sustained rice economies, fermentation systems, household frugality, and invisible umami structures.

A third theme was **coercion versus creativity**. Caribbean saltfish emerged partly through plantation provisioning and slavery, but Caribbean cooks transformed it into national, diasporic, and creole cuisines. Similarly, East Asian salted fish often originated in scarcity or preservation need, but became craft, identity, nostalgia, and connoisseurship.

A fourth theme was **sensory politics**. Salted fish is not only eaten; it smells, lingers, embarrasses, attracts, repels, and marks belonging. Its pungency makes it sociologically visible even when used in small quantities.

A fifth theme was **critical heritage**. Salted fish cannot be treated only as precious tradition. Some forms, especially Chinese-style salted fish, have documented health risks. The conversation therefore resisted both romantic heritage discourse and reductive medical dismissal.

## Topic map

Salted fish is not one cuisine category but several overlapping technologies:

1. **Dry-salted fish** — packed in salt, sometimes dried: salt cod, salt herring, salted mackerel.
2. **Salt-dried fish** — salted then air/sun dried: klippfisk, baccalà, Caribbean saltfish.
3. **Brined or pickled fish** — stored in salt brine, vinegar, or both: herring, anchovies.
4. **Fermented-salted fish** — salt controls spoilage while enzymes/microbes transform flavour: garum, fish sauce, bagoong, prahok, pla ra, jeotgal, shiokara.
5. **Smoked-salted fish** — salt plus smoke/drying: common in West Africa, northern Europe, parts of the Caribbean.
6. **Ritual or prestige cured fish** — not merely preservation but identity food: bacalhau in Portugal, surströmming in Sweden, funa-zushi in Japan, ackee and saltfish in Jamaica.

The core mechanism is simple: **salt lowers water activity, suppresses many spoilage organisms, firms flesh, concentrates flavour, and creates conditions for drying or controlled fermentation**. Before refrigeration, this made fish portable, taxable, tradable, and available inland. FAO’s fish-processing overview lists drying, salting, pickling, smoking, and fermentation as major traditional methods before mechanical freezing and canning became dominant. ([FAOHome][1])

## Big picture

Salted fish appears wherever four conditions overlap: abundant seasonal fish, access to salt, need for storage, and trade routes. It is especially prominent in Atlantic Europe, the Mediterranean, the Caribbean, West Africa, South and Southeast Asia, East Asia, and northern Europe. Its social status is unstable: it can be **poor people’s protein**, **sailors’ ration**, **monastic fasting food**, **plantation food**, **diaspora comfort food**, or **high-end heritage ingredient**.

A useful distinction: in Atlantic and Mediterranean traditions, salted fish often remains visibly “fish” — cod, herring, anchovy. In Southeast and East Asia, salted fish often becomes a **condiment system** — paste, sauce, roe, entrails, or fermented fragments used to season rice, vegetables, soups, and stews.

## 1. Atlantic Europe and the salt-cod world

The most globally consequential salted fish is probably **salt cod**: Portuguese **bacalhau**, Italian **baccalà**, Spanish **bacalao**, Norwegian **klippfisk**, and Caribbean **saltfish**. Dried and salted cod has been produced for centuries in the North Atlantic, especially around Newfoundland, Iceland, the Faroe Islands, and Norway; Norwegian klippfisk literally refers to fish dried on rocks or cliffs. ([Wikipedia][2])

Portugal is the classic case of a cuisine built around imported salt cod. Bacalhau became central partly because cod was durable, protein-rich, suited to long-distance maritime provisioning, and compatible with Catholic abstinence rules. Modern Portuguese food culture treats bacalhau as national-symbolic despite the fish historically coming from northern waters rather than Portuguese seas. History Today describes salt cod as having been central to Atlantic trade for roughly six centuries. ([History Today][3])

Common dishes include:

| Region                 |      Salted fish form | Dishes / uses                                               |
| ---------------------- | --------------------: | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Portugal               |              bacalhau | bacalhau à Brás, bacalhau com natas, bacalhau à Gomes de Sá |
| Spain / Basque Country |               bacalao | bacalao al pil-pil, bacalao a la vizcaína                   |
| Italy                  | baccalà / stoccafisso | baccalà mantecato, baccalà alla vicentina                   |
| Norway                 |             klippfisk | bacalao stew, export product                                |
| France / Provence      |                 morue | brandade de morue                                           |

**Analytical point:** salt cod is a “long-distance fish”: caught in cold northern waters, preserved with salt and drying, then consumed heavily in Catholic southern Europe and later the Caribbean. It is a food of empire, Lent, shipping, and working-class kitchens.

## 2. Mediterranean salted fish, anchovy, tuna, and garum

The Mediterranean produced a different salted-fish ecology: smaller oily fish, anchovies, sardines, tuna, roe, and sauces. Ancient Roman **garum** and related fish sauces were produced in coastal salting installations; recent archaeological and biomolecular work continues to confirm the role of fish preservation in Roman Atlantic and Mediterranean economies. A 2025 *Antiquity* article describes Roman fish-salting plants, or **cetariae**, as part of large-scale preservation and trade. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

Modern descendants are not direct one-to-one continuations, but they occupy a similar flavour niche:

| Product           | Area                         | Culinary role                          |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Salted anchovies  | Italy, Spain, France, Greece | umami, salinity, sauce base, antipasti |
| Bottarga          | Italy, Greece, North Africa  | salted-dried roe, grated or sliced     |
| Colatura di alici | Cetara, Italy                | anchovy fish sauce, seasoning          |
| Tarama            | Greece / Turkey / Balkans    | salted roe paste                       |
| Mojama            | Spain                        | salt-cured tuna loin                   |

Mediterranean salted fish often functions less as a main protein and more as an **umami concentrate**. Anchovies disappear into sauces, dressings, stews, and vegetable dishes. The “fishiness” is transformed into savoury depth.

## 3. Northern Europe and fermented-salted fish

Northern Europe developed strong traditions of herring, cod, trout, shark, and other fish preserved through salting, drying, smoking, and fermentation. A review of northern European fermented fish products identifies **Icelandic hákarl**, **Norwegian rakfisk**, **Swedish surströmming**, and **barrel-salted ripened herring** as major traditional examples. ([ScienceDirect][5])

| Product      | Region                           | Method                                      | Food logic                                                |
| ------------ | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Surströmming | Sweden                           | lightly salted fermented Baltic herring     | intense fermented aroma; seasonal/heritage food           |
| Rakfisk      | Norway                           | salted fermented trout/char                 | eaten with flatbread, potatoes, onion, sour cream         |
| Hákarl       | Iceland                          | fermented and dried Greenland/sleeper shark | detoxification and preservation; ritualized identity food |
| Salt herring | Netherlands, Scandinavia, Baltic | brined/salted herring                       | everyday preserved fish, trade staple                     |
| Lutefisk     | Nordic diaspora                  | dried fish reconstituted with lye           | festive heritage food, though not simply “salted fish”    |

This cluster shows that salted fish is not always about high salt alone. In surströmming or rakfisk, the salt level may be low enough to permit fermentation, while still restraining dangerous spoilage. That makes the product biologically and culturally riskier: success depends on inherited technique.

## 4. West Africa and the smoked-salted-dried fish belt

In West Africa, preservation is often a mixed system: smoking, salting, drying, fermentation, and trade across inland routes. FAO describes smoking, salting, drying, fermentation, and frying as major fish-preservation methods in West Africa, with smoked fish especially important in Ghana. ([FAOHome][6])

Salted and dried fish products circulate as both protein and seasoning. FAO material on African fermented fish notes that artisanal processing remains a major preservation mode, with smoking, sun-drying, salting, and fermentation prominent across regions. ([FAOHome][7])

Examples include:

| Product / type                 | Region                      | Use                                         |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Guedj / gejj                   | Senegal, Gambia             | salted fermented dried fish, soups/stews    |
| Bonga / shad products          | Gambia, West Africa         | smoked, salted-dried, fermented-dried forms |
| Dried anchovy / small pelagics | Ghana, Nigeria, Benin, Togo | soups, sauces, stews                        |
| Salted-dried tilapia/catfish   | inland West Africa          | trade protein and flavouring                |

The important point is that West African salted fish often enters a **sauce-and-stew economy**: it seasons vegetable stews, grains, legumes, and starch staples. The fish may be less a plate-centred fillet and more a flavour-bearing ingredient.

## 5. Caribbean saltfish and plantation Atlantic history

Caribbean **saltfish** is usually salted cod or related white fish, historically tied to Atlantic trade, plantation provisioning, and later national cuisine. Jamaica’s **ackee and saltfish** is the most famous example. History Today notes that salt cod remained important in the Caribbean after the Atlantic slave trade ended, surviving in dishes such as Puerto Rican bacalaitos and Jamaican ackee and saltfish. ([History Today][3])

Caribbean saltfish matters because it joins three histories:

1. **North Atlantic fisheries** — cod caught and salted far away.
2. **Plantation economies** — durable imported protein for enslaved and later working populations.
3. **Creolization** — saltfish becomes local through pairing with ackee, callaloo, breadfruit, plantain, dumplings, rice, peppers, and onions.

Common forms:

| Region             | Dish                                  |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| Jamaica            | ackee and saltfish, saltfish fritters |
| Trinidad & Tobago  | buljol, saltfish with bake            |
| Barbados           | saltfish and cou-cou variants         |
| Puerto Rico        | bacalaitos                            |
| Dominican Republic | bacalao guisado                       |
| Haiti              | morue / salt cod stews                |

**Analytical point:** Caribbean saltfish is not simply “European fish in the tropics.” It is a creole food created by coercive trade, African foodways, island agriculture, and post-emancipation adaptation.

## 6. Southeast Asia: salted fish as paste, sauce, and deep flavour

Southeast Asia has one of the world’s richest salted-fermented fish traditions. Here the key products are often **fish sauce**, **fish paste**, or **fermented fish solids**. A 2023 review in *Trends in Food Science & Technology* states that fish fermentation commonly relies on salting, additives/spices, and anaerobic conditions, selecting microbial communities that produce fermented fish products. ([ScienceDirect][8])

Major examples:

| Product  | Region                       | Form                        | Use                                 |
| -------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Nam pla  | Thailand                     | fish sauce                  | seasoning, dipping sauce, curries   |
| Nuoc mam | Vietnam                      | fish sauce                  | dipping, marinades, soups           |
| Patis    | Philippines                  | fish sauce                  | seasoning                           |
| Bagoong  | Philippines                  | fermented fish/shrimp paste | stews, dips, kare-kare, green mango |
| Prahok   | Cambodia                     | fermented fish paste        | soups, dips, grilled preparations   |
| Pla ra   | Thailand / Laos              | fermented fish              | som tam, soups, stews               |
| Padaek   | Laos                         | fermented fish sauce/paste  | Lao seasoning base                  |
| Ngapi    | Myanmar                      | fish/shrimp paste           | curries, relishes                   |
| Budu     | Malaysia / southern Thailand | fermented anchovy sauce     | rice, herbs, grilled fish           |

ScienceDirect’s food-science overview describes **bagoong** as a Philippine fish paste made from small fish such as anchovies or sardines, mixed with roughly 20–25% salt and fermented in vessels until the liquor is ready. ([ScienceDirect][9]) Prahok is similarly described as a Cambodian salted and fermented fish paste used as seasoning or condiment, originally tied to preserving fish when fresh fish was unavailable. ([Wikipedia][10])

**Mechanism:** Southeast Asian salted fish often converts glutamate-rich fish proteins into sauces and pastes. The product becomes a **background architecture of taste**, not just a preserved fish dish.

## 7. East Asia: salted seafood as rice companion, condiment, and fermentation substrate

East Asian salted fish traditions include whole salted fish, roe, entrails, sauces, and fermented products.

### China and Sinophone cuisines

Chinese salted fish includes **鹹魚 / 咸鱼** (*haam yu* in Cantonese; *xiányú* in Mandarin), often used in small quantities with rice, pork, tofu, eggplant, or steamed meat cakes. Cantonese salted fish is particularly pungent and is commonly used as a seasoning-protein hybrid.

Representative dishes:

| Dish                           | Region                 | Logic                                       |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| 鹹魚雞粒炒飯                         | Cantonese              | salted fish as aromatic umami in fried rice |
| 鹹魚蒸肉餅                          | Cantonese              | salted fish flavours minced pork            |
| 鹹魚茄子煲                          | Cantonese              | salted fish deepens claypot eggplant        |
| Salted fish with rice porridge | South China / diaspora | preserved protein with bland starch         |

### Japan

Japan has several salted-fermented seafood forms: **塩辛（しおから）shiokara**, **魚醤（ぎょしょう）gyoshō**, and older sushi forms such as **熟れ鮨（なれずし）nare-zushi**. Japan’s Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries describes nare-zushi as fish preserved with rice under weight, undergoing lactic fermentation; it identifies funa-zushi as an old form and part of the historical background to later sushi. ([Ministry of Agriculture][11])

Shiokara is made by maturing salted seafood meat and offal; enzymatic breakdown creates free amino acids and umami while salt prevents spoilage. ([sushiuniversity.jp][12])

### Korea

Korean **젓갈 jeotgal** is a whole category of salted seafood: shrimp, oysters, clams, fish, roe, and other marine products. It functions both as a banchan and as a fermentation/seasoning component, especially in kimchi. Public summaries define jeotgal as salted preserved seafood whose texture ranges from solid pieces to liquid-like forms. ([Wikipedia][13])

Examples:

| Product        | Region        | Use                              |
| -------------- | ------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Saeujeot       | Korea         | salted shrimp; kimchi, soups     |
| Myeolchi-jeot  | Korea         | salted anchovy; kimchi seasoning |
| Myeongnan-jeot | Korea / Japan | salted pollock roe               |
| Ojingeo-jeot   | Korea         | salted squid side dish           |

## 8. South Asia and the Indian Ocean

South Asia uses salted, dried, and fermented fish across coastal and riverine regions, especially where monsoon seasonality, inland transport, and rice-based diets make preserved fish valuable.

Examples:

## 11. Salted fish as seasoning versus main protein

A major cross-cultural contrast:

| Mode            | Examples                                                | Culinary logic                         |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Main protein    | bacalhau, baccalà, saltfish, rakfisk                    | soak/desalt, cook as centrepiece       |
| Condiment       | anchovy, jeotgal, shiokara, prahok, bagoong, fish sauce | small quantity transforms dish         |
| Staple-side     | Chinese salted fish, shutki, dried salted fish          | eaten with rice/starch                 |
| Ritual food     | fesikh, funa-zushi, hákarl, surströmming                | identity, seasonality, inherited taste |
| Trade commodity | salt cod, herring, dried bonga                          | durable, portable, marketable          |

This is one reason “salted fish” can feel completely different across cuisines. Portuguese bacalhau is often rehydrated and treated almost like fresh fish. Thai fish sauce or Cambodian prahok may be invisible in the finished dish but structurally essential.

## 12. Why salted fish becomes culturally powerful

Salted fish has recurring historical roles:

**Preservation technology.** It solved the problem of abundance followed by scarcity.

**Trade engine.** Salt cod, herring, anchovies, and dried fish moved across long routes. Salted fish helped connect cold-water fisheries to warm-climate markets.

**Class food.** It often fed sailors, labourers, monks, enslaved people, rural poor, and urban workers.

**Diaspora marker.** Caribbean saltfish, Chinese salted fish, Korean jeotgal, and South Asian dried fish remain strong diaspora taste markers.

**Umami infrastructure.** Fermented salted fish creates glutamates, peptides, and amino acids that intensify savoury flavour.

**Identity food.** Strong-smelling products such as surströmming, hákarl, prahok, and shiokara often become boundary markers: loved by insiders, caricatured by outsiders.

## 13. Safety and health issues

Salted fish is not nutritionally neutral. It may provide protein, minerals, and micronutrients, but high sodium is a real issue. Some traditional salted or fermented fish products also carry food-safety risks when improperly prepared.

There is also a specific cancer-risk issue: **Chinese-style salted fish** has been classified by IARC as carcinogenic to humans, particularly associated with nasopharyngeal carcinoma risk. I’m not expanding this here because it would require a focused source review, but it is a major suppressed angle in any culinary celebration of salted fish. [UNCERTAIN] The risk is unlikely to apply equally to all salted fish worldwide; product type, nitrosamine formation, childhood exposure, fermentation conditions, and dietary context probably matter.

## Synthesis

Salted fish is best understood as a **global preservation complex**, not a single ingredient. Its forms range from clean white salt cod to pungent fermented paste, from Catholic fasting food to Buddhist/monastic-compatible protein, from plantation ration to national dish, from poor food to artisanal delicacy.

The strongest cross-cultural pattern is this: **where fresh fish was abundant but unstable, salt turned local seasonality into durable cuisine**. The second pattern is more subtle: once preservation became unnecessary, salted fish survived because it had become a flavour technology and identity marker. Refrigeration did not eliminate it because no fresh fish tastes like salt cod, prahok, jeotgal, shiokara, anchovy, or bacalhau.

## Preamble

Salt fish is easy to misread as a rustic ingredient. Historically, it was closer to an **infrastructure food**: a portable protein that helped convert distant seas into imperial calories. It fed Catholic fasting tables, sailors, soldiers, enslaved plantation workers, urban poor, and later diaspora cuisines. Its story is not just culinary. It is about preservation, shipping, coercion, markets, religious regulation, and the unequal geography of taste.

The central claim is this: **salt fish became globally important because it solved a logistical problem for empires before refrigeration**. It could be caught in cold northern waters, preserved cheaply, shipped across oceans, sold into Catholic abstinence markets, and offloaded in lower grades to plantation economies.

## Topic map

Salt fish links four historical systems:

| System                  | What salt fish solved                                                  | Historical effect                                                                             |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Catholic fasting        | Meat abstinence created repeated demand for non-meat protein           | Salt cod became embedded in Portuguese, Spanish, Italian, French, and Latin American foodways |
| Maritime trade          | Ships needed durable food and profitable cargo                         | Cod, herring, and other salted fish became Atlantic commodities                               |
| Empire                  | Northern fisheries connected to southern European and colonial markets | Fishing grounds, ports, salt supplies, and shipping lanes became strategic assets             |
| Slavery and plantations | Plantation owners needed cheap durable protein for coerced labourers   | Low-grade salt fish helped provision Caribbean sugar economies                                |

## Deep analysis

### 1. Salt fish as a pre-refrigeration technology of empire

Salt fish mattered because it made fish separable from the coast. Fresh fish is local and perishable. Salted and dried fish is transportable, taxable, storable, and tradable. That technical shift changed fish from a coastal food into an imperial commodity.

The preservation mechanism was brutally practical. Cod was especially suitable because its flesh is lean, white, firm, and low in oil, making it easier to salt and dry without rapid rancidity. Once cured, it could survive long voyages and tropical storage better than many fresh or lightly preserved foods. This made it ideal for the Atlantic world, where northern fisheries, Iberian markets, Caribbean plantations, and imperial navies were joined by ships rather than roads.

Harold Innis’s *The Cod Fisheries* is still the classic framing here: cod was not just a fishery but an “international economy,” linking European imperial rivalry, North American colonial development, Catholic demand, and Atlantic commodity flows. A summary of Innis’s argument notes that English producers could cure fish onshore and sell dried cod into Mediterranean Catholic markets, while the trade also intersected with sugar, tobacco, rum, and slavery. ([Wikipedia][1])

This is the first major correction to a naïve food-history reading: **salt fish was not peripheral to empire; it was one of the small, durable goods that made empire physically workable**.

---

### 2. Catholic fasting created a mass market for preserved fish

Catholic abstinence rules did not invent salt fish, but they created repeated demand. If meat was restricted on Fridays, Lent, vigils, or other penitential days, then durable fish became a practical substitute. Current Catholic norms are much lighter than earlier practice, but even today the US Conference of Catholic Bishops states that Ash Wednesday and Good Friday are obligatory days of fasting and abstinence, and Fridays during Lent are obligatory days of abstinence. ([USCCB][2])

Historically, this mattered because fasting was not an occasional dietary preference. It structured calendars, markets, household provisioning, monastery kitchens, and urban supply chains. Fish could substitute for flesh meat, but fresh fish could not always reach inland cities or rural households. Salt cod, salted herring, anchovies, and other cured fish solved that problem.

Southern Europe is the clearest case. Portugal, Spain, Italy, and parts of France incorporated salt cod into ordinary and festive cooking. Hidden Europe’s account of Lenten cod stresses the practical point: salted cod became prominent in Catholic southern Europe because it did not require fast delivery or cold storage. ([hiddeneurope.eu][3])

Portugal is the emblematic example. **Bacalhau** is a national food built around a fish that largely did not come from Portuguese waters. That paradox is the point. Bacalhau is not a simple local-produce tradition; it is a maritime-import tradition naturalized into national cuisine. Portuguese maritime expansion, northern Atlantic fisheries, Catholic abstinence, and domestic taste fused into one ingredient.

[INFERENCE] In cultural terms, bacalhau became Portuguese not because of ecological origin but because of repeated calendrical use. Repetition naturalized importation. The fish was foreign in origin but domestic in ritual.

---

### 3. Maritime capitalism turned fish into a triangular commodity

Salt fish was part of the same Atlantic system as sugar, rum, molasses, tobacco, timber, and enslaved labour. It did not simply travel along one route. It moved through a multi-directional economy.

A simplified Atlantic pattern:

```text
North Atlantic fisheries
  ↓ salt cod, salt fish
Southern Europe / Catholic markets
  ↓ silver, wine, oil, manufactured goods

North Atlantic fisheries / New England / Newfoundland
  ↓ lower-grade salt fish
Caribbean plantations
  ↓ sugar, molasses, rum
Atlantic merchants / imperial ports
```

The important nuance is quality segmentation. Higher-quality fish could be sold to European markets. Lower-grade or “refuse” fish could be sold into plantation provisioning systems. This made the cod economy more profitable: grades that might not command good prices in Iberia or the Mediterranean still had a market in the Caribbean.

Heritage Newfoundland and Labrador’s public-history account states the point directly: British West Indian plantation owners needed cheap durable protein for enslaved people and later free workers, and Newfoundland salt fish supplied that need. It gives export figures: over 150,000 quintals to the British West Indies in 1810 and 1811, a peak of 176,000 quintals in 1816, and an average of 127,000 quintals annually from 1817 to 1833. ([Heritage Newfoundland and Labrador][4])

Those numbers matter because they show that salt fish was not an incidental flavouring. It was bulk provisioning.

---

### 4. Salt fish and slavery: not just “food of the enslaved,” but food of coercive accounting

The salt fish–slavery link should not be romanticized. Caribbean saltfish cuisines today are rich, adaptive, and culturally creative. But their origins include the violence of plantation provisioning.

Plantation owners needed labourers to survive but had no interest in feeding them well beyond what sustained extraction. Salt fish was useful because it was durable, cheap, rationable, and compatible with monocrop plantation geography. Sugar estates prioritized land for cane rather than diverse subsistence farming. Imported preserved foods helped maintain that plantation logic.

The Massachusetts Secretary of the Commonwealth’s public-history exhibit notes that salted cod was sold to Catholic countries such as Spain and Portugal, while damaged or lower-quality cod that could not sell well in open markets was sold to Caribbean plantations as food for enslaved Africans. ([sec.state.ma.us][5])

This produces a hard historical distinction:

| Modern dish                     | Historical sediment                                                                    |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Jamaican ackee and saltfish     | Creole national dish with roots in forced plantation provisioning                      |
| Puerto Rican bacalaitos         | Iberian-Catholic and Atlantic cod inheritance                                          |
| Trinidadian saltfish buljol     | Salt cod transformed through Caribbean vegetables, peppers, oil, and breakfast culture |
| Barbadian saltfish preparations | Plantation ration remade into local cuisine                                            |

[INFERENCE] The transformation from plantation ration to national dish is not a simple story of “resilience.” It is also a story of constraint becoming cuisine. Enslaved and later free Caribbean populations took imposed or cheap ingredients and reorganized them through African, Indigenous, European, and local island food logics.

This is why “saltfish” carries double meaning in the Caribbean: it is both beloved and historically contaminated. It is comfort food and archive.

---

### 5. The plantation made salt fish cheap, but Caribbean cooks made it meaningful

The food-history mistake is to stop at coercion. Saltfish was indeed tied to slavery, but enslaved and post-emancipation communities did not passively consume it as a fixed colonial object. They transformed it.

Saltfish was soaked, flaked, fried, stewed, mixed with peppers, onions, tomatoes, greens, ground provisions, dumplings, plantains, breadfruit, rice, and eventually ackee. The fish changed function: from ration protein to seasoning-protein; from imposed commodity to creole ingredient.

CARICOM’s statistical/public-history discussion of food and colonial legacies notes how national dishes across the Caribbean still include salt fish, including ackee and saltfish in Jamaica, salt fish and bananas in Saint Lucia, and oil down in Grenada. It also describes salt fish and bakes/dumplings as widespread across the region. ([caricomstats.org][6])

The analytical point: **Caribbean cuisine did not erase the violence of provisioning, but it did not remain reducible to it either**. Saltfish became part of a vernacular culinary intelligence: desalting, stretching, seasoning, and combining with local starches and aromatics.

---

### 6. Empire also ran on sailors’ food

Salt fish was not only plantation food. It fed ships. Long-distance maritime labour required durable calories: hardtack, salted meat, peas, beer or rum, and salted fish. Naval, merchant, and fishing vessels all depended on preserved foods.

This matters because maritime empires were not abstractions. They were floating labour systems. Ships required foods that could endure humidity, time, and uncertain resupply. Salt fish met those requirements. It could be stacked, rationed, and sold. It also doubled as cargo and crew food.

[INFERENCE] Salt fish therefore sits in a liminal category: **provision and commodity at once**. The same fish could feed a sailor, provision a garrison, supply a Catholic fast-day meal, or feed an enslaved worker in the Caribbean. Its flexibility was precisely its imperial value.

---

### 7. Catholic demand and slave provisioning were linked by grade, geography, and price

One of the most important mechanisms is market segmentation.

High-quality dried cod went to demanding European markets, especially Iberian and Mediterranean consumers. Lower-quality fish went to plantation markets. This did not mean two separate worlds. It meant one integrated economy in which the same fishery sorted products by grade and destination.

```text
Best-quality salt cod
  → Iberia, Mediterranean, Catholic urban markets

Middle-quality salt fish
  → general Atlantic consumption, shipping, colonial settlements

Low-grade / damaged / cheaper fish
  → plantation provisioning, poorer consumers, labour rations
```

The moral economy was encoded into quality. The same commodity chain could produce a prestigious Christmas or Lenten dish in one place and a coerced ration in another.

History Today’s 2019 article on salt cod emphasizes that salt cod flourished across Scandinavia, Spain, the Caribbean, Italy, and other regions, and that after abolition it survived in Caribbean foods such as Puerto Rican bacalaitos and Jamaican ackee and saltfish. ([History Today][7])

[INFERENCE] Salt cod’s global spread was not because everyone valued the same fish in the same way. It spread because different groups could be made to value or use different grades under different constraints: piety, poverty, coercion, convenience, or taste.

---

### 8. Salt fish and the geography of whiteness, hunger, and empire

Salt cod is visually pale, dry, almost austere. In European Catholic cuisines, that whiteness could become associated with penitence, fasting, simplicity, and ritual. In plantation economies, the same whiteness was bound to hunger, rationing, and coerced survival. The symbolic field changes by context.

In Portugal, bacalhau can signify family, Christmas Eve, saudade, maritime memory, and Catholic inheritance. In Jamaica, saltfish can signify national identity, breakfast, creolization, and the afterlife of slavery. In Italy or Spain, baccalà/bacalao may carry associations of Lent, poverty, regional tradition, and festive revival. The same preservation system generates different moral atmospheres.

This is not contradiction; it is how commodities work. They hide their routes. A dried fish on a plate can look local, timeless, and traditional while carrying the history of distant fisheries, imperial shipping, labour exploitation, and religious regulation.

---

### 9. Comparison with fermented fish sauces and Asian salt-fish worlds

The Atlantic salt cod story should not be mistaken for the universal history of salted fish. East and Southeast Asian salted-fish systems often developed less around Catholic abstinence and more around rice economies, monsoon seasonality, fish abundance, and fermentation.

Fish sauce, prahok, bagoong, jeotgal, shiokara, and Chinese salted fish often act as flavour infrastructures rather than centre-plate proteins. They make bland starches vivid; they preserve seasonal abundance; they create umami through enzymatic breakdown.

The contrast is useful:

| Atlantic salt cod complex                                 | East/Southeast Asian salt-fermentation complex                                         |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Long-distance imperial commodity                          | Local/regional preservation and condiment systems, though also traded                  |
| Often rehydrated as main protein                          | Often used as seasoning, paste, sauce, side dish                                       |
| Strongly tied to Catholic abstinence and Atlantic slavery | Strongly tied to rice economies, fermentation, monsoon ecology, household preservation |
| Cod/herring/white fish central                            | Anchovy, small fish, shrimp, roe, entrails, mixed seafood central                      |

[INFERENCE] The Atlantic case is unusually bound to empire because cod moved at scale between cold fisheries, Catholic markets, and slave plantations. Asian salt-fish systems certainly intersected with trade and state power, but their historical centre of gravity is often fermentation ecology and staple-grain accompaniment rather than Catholic maritime capitalism.

---

### 10. The afterlife: from necessity to heritage

Refrigeration should have killed salt fish as necessity. It did not. That survival tells us that preservation foods often become taste traditions.

Salt cod no longer needs to exist for purely technical reasons. Yet bacalhau, baccalà, saltfish, bacalao, salted anchovies, and salt herring remain culturally powerful because they offer textures and flavours fresh fish cannot replicate: firm fibres, concentrated savouriness, controlled salinity, slight oxidation, and memory.

The same thing happened in many cuisines: foods born from scarcity become heritage foods. But the danger is nostalgia. Heritage language can erase coercion. Saying “ackee and saltfish is a national dish” is true; saying only that is incomplete. Saying “bacalhau is Portuguese tradition” is true; saying only that hides the imported northern fish and the maritime systems that supplied it.

---

## Synthesis

Salt fish became world-historical because it sat at the intersection of four forces:

1. **Ecology:** cold northern seas produced abundant cod and herring.
2. **Technology:** salt and drying made fish portable before refrigeration.
3. **Religion:** Catholic abstinence created predictable recurring demand.
4. **Coercive economy:** plantations needed cheap durable protein for enslaved labour.

The result was a commodity that could move between piety and brutality. On one table, salt cod marked Lent, Christmas Eve, or national belonging. On another, it was a ration calculated to keep enslaved people alive enough to labour. The same preserved fish helped organize Catholic calendars, maritime trade, imperial rivalries, and plantation survival.

That is the central historical irony: **salt fish is both humble food and imperial technology**. Its afterlife in beloved cuisines does not cancel its violence; nor does its violent history exhaust its culinary meaning. It is precisely the tension between coercion and creativity that makes saltfish such a dense food-historical object.

## Preamble

A critical historiography of salt fish should not simply say “cod changed the world.” That is Kurlansky’s memorable narrative hook, but it risks turning a commodity into a heroic agent. A stronger version asks: **which historical systems made salted fish powerful, and which people bore the costs of that power?**

The best frame is to put **Harold Innis’s staple theory**, **Mark Kurlansky’s popular commodity biography**, **Sidney Mintz’s political anthropology of food**, and **Atlantic-world slavery scholarship** into tension. Innis explains the commodity network. Kurlansky narrates its drama. Mintz explains how consumption and production are joined by power. Atlantic-world scholarship forces the hardest correction: salt fish was not only trade, taste, and maritime ingenuity; it was also provisioning infrastructure for slavery.

## Topic Map

| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| Mark Kurlansky                     | Cod as accessible global narrative; links ecology, exploration, markets, and collapse         | Can over-personify cod and flatten political economy into “the fish that changed the world” |

## Deep Analysis

### 1. Innis: cod as staple, not merely ingredient

Harold Innis’s *The Cod Fisheries: The History of an International Economy* was published in 1940 and belongs to his broader “staples” approach to Canadian and Atlantic economic history. In this model, a staple commodity is not just something exported; it reorganizes settlement, labour, technology, imperial policy, finance, transport, and dependency. Public summaries of Innis’s book describe it as a study of cod fishing across five centuries, with attention to European empires, Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, New England, Mediterranean Catholic markets, the West Indies, sugar, tobacco, rum, and slavery. ([Wikipedia][1])

Innis’s strongest insight is that cod was a **network commodity**. The fish mattered because it joined ecology to empire: cold-water abundance, shore-curing technology, European shipping, Catholic demand, Mediterranean markets, colonial rivalry, and West Indian plantation provisioning. His famous “international economy” framing makes salt fish appear less like a culinary tradition and more like a logistical medium through which imperial systems circulated value. ([Wikipedia][1])

The limitation is also clear. Innis is excellent on structure but weaker on embodied consumption. He shows where fish moved and how markets formed, but less fully how enslaved people, sailors, poor Catholics, Caribbean cooks, or diasporic communities experienced, resisted, transformed, or remembered the food. Innis’s cod is a staple of imperial political economy more than a sensory, racialized, gendered, or domestic food.

**Use Innis, but do not stop with Innis.** His model explains why salt fish moved. It does not fully explain what salt fish meant.

---

### 2. Kurlansky: the commodity biography and its seductions

Mark Kurlansky’s *Cod: A Biography of the Fish That Changed the World* popularized the idea that cod was a world-historical fish. Reviews and bookseller summaries characterize it as a sweeping account spanning Vikings, Basques, European expansion, North Atlantic fisheries, Catholic demand, and ecological depletion. Smithsonian’s 1997 review describes the book as a cod-centred look at European and North American history, including the “1,000-year fishing spree” that ended in collapse for many fishing communities. ([Smithsonian Magazine][2])

Kurlansky is valuable because he gives the fish narrative force. Where Innis is infrastructural, Kurlansky is biographical. He helps readers see that a humble preserved food can connect medieval Basque fishing, Newfoundland, European fast days, modern overfishing, and the collapse of cod stocks.

But this genre has a built-in risk: **commodity biography can become commodity determinism**. “The fish that changed the world” is rhetorically powerful but analytically unstable. Fish do not change worlds by themselves. States, merchants, ships, enslavers, fasting rules, salt makers, credit systems, imperial wars, and labour regimes do. Cod is a material node, not a historical subject.

So Kurlansky’s contribution is best treated as **narrative synthesis**, not final explanation. He makes the story legible; he does not, by himself, supply enough theory of power.

---

### 3. Mintz: sugar as the missing analytic model for salt fish

Sidney Mintz’s *Sweetness and Power* is about sugar, not cod, but it gives the sharpest method for studying salt fish. Mintz’s own description of the project says he situated sugar within Western history because it was an old commodity central to the emergence of a global market, and because its meanings changed as it moved through production, consumption, labour, and class. ([sidneymintz.net][3])

Mintz’s central methodological lesson is that food history must link **production and consumption**. Sugar was not merely grown in the Caribbean and eaten in Britain; it connected enslaved plantation labour to changing metropolitan habits, industrial work rhythms, class formation, and imperial power. Salt fish needs the same treatment.

Applied to salt fish, a Mintzian analysis asks:

| Mintzian question                 | Salt-fish application                                                                       |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Who produced it?                  | Fishers, shore crews, salt workers, merchants, colonial labourers                           |
| Who consumed it?                  | Catholics, sailors, enslaved plantation workers, poor urban families, diasporic communities |
| What made it cheap or valuable?   | Preservation, grading, imperial trade routes, labour exploitation, Catholic demand          |
| How did meaning change?           | From fast-day food, ration, and poor food into heritage cuisine and national dish           |
| What power relations did it hide? | Slavery, colonial dependence, ecological extraction, class hierarchy                        |

This is where salt fish becomes a useful counterpoint to sugar. Sugar was the plantation export that made imperial wealth visible; salt fish was part of the provisioning system that made plantation labour sustainable. Sugar moved outward from Caribbean plantations to metropolitan consumers. Salt fish moved inward toward the plantation, feeding the coerced workers who produced sugar, molasses, rum, and other Atlantic commodities.

[INFERENCE] In Mintzian terms, salt fish was not the sweet taste of empire but the salty ration behind empire’s sweetness.

---

### 4. Atlantic-world scholarship: provisioning as political economy, not background detail

Atlantic-world history adds the essential correction: food supply was not a neutral logistical backdrop to slavery. It was part of plantation power. Heritage Newfoundland and Labrador’s account of nineteenth-century salt fish markets states that British West Indian plantation owners needed cheap, durable protein for enslaved people and later free workers, and that Newfoundland salt fish supplied this demand. It gives concrete export figures: more than 150,000 quintals shipped to the British West Indies in both 1810 and 1811, a peak of 176,000 quintals in 1816, and an annual average of 127,000 quintals from 1817 to 1833. ([Heritage Newfoundland and Labrador][4])

This evidence forces a shift in emphasis. Salt fish was not merely a commodity moving through Atlantic trade. It was part of a **ration regime**. In plantation societies, food was an accounting problem: how cheaply could labour be reproduced? What minimum provisioning kept enslaved workers alive, disciplined, and productive? Salt fish’s durability, portability, and grading made it useful to that system.

Atlantic scholarship on provisioning also widens the frame beyond cod alone. Irish salt beef, dried fish, maize, plantains, provision grounds, imported grain, and local subsistence all belonged to the contested politics of feeding enslaved populations. Work on slave provisioning emphasizes that ration allowances and subsistence were political ground, tied to planter debt, commercial practice, ethics, and imperial governance. ([Dash][5])

The historiographic point is blunt: **if salt fish is narrated only as trade, it is sanitized. If narrated only as cuisine, it is sentimentalized. It must be narrated as provisioning under coercion.**

---

### 5. Catholic fasting: demand engine, but not the whole story

Catholic abstinence rules helped create predictable demand for fish, especially in southern Europe. Current Catholic guidance still preserves a reduced form of this older structure: the US Conference of Catholic Bishops states that Ash Wednesday and Good Friday are obligatory days of fasting and abstinence, and Fridays in Lent are obligatory days of abstinence. ([Masaryk University][6])

Historically, the scale and strictness of abstinence were broader and more socially consequential than current practice. Salted fish solved the problem of feeding inland and urban Catholic populations when fresh fish was unavailable or expensive. This helps explain the embedding of bacalhau, baccalà, bacalao, morue, and related forms into Portuguese, Italian, Spanish, and French Catholic foodways. Summaries of the salt cod trade emphasize that dried cod sold well in Catholic countries where fish consumption was required or encouraged on abstinence days. ([Wikipedia][1])

But Catholic fasting must not be overused as a monocausal explanation. It explains demand in Iberia, Italy, France, and parts of Latin America. It does not explain all salt fish traditions globally, nor does it explain the Caribbean plantation market by itself. The key historical interaction is that Catholic demand helped make the North Atlantic salt-fish industry commercially viable, while plantation demand absorbed cheaper grades and expanded the commodity’s Atlantic reach.

[INFERENCE] Catholic fasting created high-volume legitimate demand; plantation slavery created low-cost coercive demand. The same fishery could serve both.

---

### 6. Quality grading: where class and race enter the commodity chain

One of the most important historiographic mechanisms is **grading**. Salt fish was not a single commodity. It was sorted by quality, size, cure, damage, appearance, and market destination.

A simplified model:

| Grade / market              | Likely destination                                          | Social meaning                                       |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Higher-grade dried cod      | Iberian and Mediterranean Catholic markets                  | fast-day food, commercial value, household tradition |
| Mid-grade fish              | colonial settlements, navies, general Atlantic provisioning | durable protein                                      |
| Lower-grade or damaged fish | West Indian plantation markets, poorer consumers            | cheap ration, coerced subsistence                    |

Public-history accounts note that poorer or lower-quality cod could be sold into Caribbean plantation provisioning systems, where plantation owners needed cheap durable protein for enslaved labourers. ([Heritage Newfoundland and Labrador][4])

This is where a critical historiography improves on celebratory global-food narratives. The same commodity chain distributed quality according to power. Better fish followed money and prestige; inferior fish followed coerced labour and racial hierarchy.

[INFERENCE] Salt fish’s history is therefore not just about movement across the Atlantic. It is about the sorting of bodies and markets: Catholic consumers, sailors, enslaved workers, and poor households were not eating the same “salt cod” in the same social world.

---

### 7. Caribbean food studies: from “slave food” to “poor people’s food” to “traditional food”

Caribbean food scholarship complicates the story by tracing what happened after saltfish entered plantation societies. S. I. Francis’s 2022 Dublin Gastronomy Symposium paper explicitly frames saltfish’s movement to the West Indies as a journey from “slave” food to “poor people” food to “traditional” food, focusing on Trinidad and Tobago and the enslaved African population under colonial rule. ([Arrow][7])

That trajectory matters. It resists two bad interpretations. The first is a colonial interpretation: saltfish as mere ration. The second is a heritage interpretation: saltfish as timeless national cuisine. The historical reality is transitional and layered. Saltfish moved through coercion, poverty, adaptation, creolization, and memory.

In Jamaica, Trinidad, Barbados, Puerto Rico, Saint Lucia, Grenada, the Virgin Islands, and other Caribbean contexts, saltfish became embedded in dishes with ackee, callaloo, bakes, dumplings, plantain, breadfruit, green banana, peppers, onions, and oil. The ingredient was imposed by Atlantic trade and plantation economies, but Caribbean cooks transformed it into local food systems. Regional public-history and food studies sources identify saltfish as a continuing element in Caribbean national and everyday dishes, including ackee and saltfish, saltfish with bakes, and other combinations. ([History Today][8])

The critical formulation should be: **saltfish is a colonial ration that became a creole ingredient**. Either half alone is misleading.

---

### 8. The “Atlantic triangle” needs revision

The familiar triangular-trade diagram—manufactured goods to Africa, enslaved Africans to the Americas, sugar and plantation goods to Europe—is too simple for salt fish. Salt fish reveals a more complicated provisioning triangle.

```text
North Atlantic fisheries
  ↓ salt cod / salt fish
Caribbean plantations
  ↓ sugar / molasses / rum
European and North American markets
  ↑ credit, ships, salt, manufactured goods, finance
```

Salt fish was not always a direct component of the slave trade in the narrow sense of ship-for-slave exchange. It was often part of the broader **Atlantic provisioning economy** that sustained plantation production after enslaved people had been transported and forced into labour.

That distinction matters. It prevents exaggeration while preserving the structural link. Salt fish did not “cause” slavery. It helped provision slave societies. It made plantation monoculture more sustainable by importing durable protein into islands and colonies whose land and labour were organized around export crops.

---

### 9. Ecological history: the absent fourth term

Kurlansky brings ecology into the story more strongly than Innis or Mintz. The collapse of North Atlantic cod stocks makes the commodity biography end not in national dishes but in ecological depletion. Smithsonian’s review of Kurlansky emphasizes that cod once thrived in vast numbers in the North Sea, off Iceland, on the Grand Banks, and on Georges Bank, but became commercially extinct almost everywhere after centuries of exploitation. ([Smithsonian Magazine][2])

A complete historiography should therefore add environmental history:

| Historical phase           | Ecological logic                                           |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Early abundance            | Cod appears inexhaustible to European fishers              |
| Imperial expansion         | Abundance enables maritime provisioning and trade          |
| Industrial intensification | Technology increases catch capacity                        |
| Collapse                   | The “infinite” staple is revealed as finite                |
| Heritage afterlife         | Salt cod survives culturally even where cod stocks decline |

This ecological ending changes the moral shape of the story. Salt fish was not only tied to human exploitation. It was also tied to extractive assumptions about marine abundance.

## Comparative Historiographic Synthesis

### Innis vs Kurlansky

Innis gives the better structure; Kurlansky gives the better story. Innis sees cod as a staple embedded in imperial economy. Kurlansky turns cod into a narrative protagonist. The former risks dryness and abstraction; the latter risks overstatement.

**Best use:** read Kurlansky for narrative sweep, then discipline him with Innis’s political economy.

### Innis vs Mintz

Innis follows the staple outward through trade and empire. Mintz follows the food inward into labour, consumption, habit, and power. Innis asks how cod shaped economies. Mintz would ask how salt fish entered bodies, routines, class relations, and coerced subsistence.

**Best use:** use Innis to map routes; use Mintz to ask who ate what, under what constraints, and with what consequences.

### Mintz vs Caribbean food studies

Mintz helps connect plantation production and metropolitan consumption. Caribbean food studies help recover the afterlife of imposed foods as cuisine, memory, and identity. The former prevents nostalgia; the latter prevents reducing Caribbean cooks to passive recipients.

**Best use:** keep coercion and creativity in the same frame.

### Atlantic-world scholarship vs national cuisine narratives

Portuguese bacalhau, Jamaican ackee and saltfish, Italian baccalà, and Puerto Rican bacalaitos are often narrated as national or regional foods. Atlantic-world scholarship destabilizes that by showing the routes behind the dish: fisheries, ships, salt, fasting, slavery, grades, and markets.

**Best use:** treat national dishes as endpoints of transnational commodity chains, not origins.

## A More Critical Thesis

Salt fish became globally significant not because cod “changed the world,” but because imperial systems made preserved fish useful. It served four linked regimes:

1. **Religious discipline** — Catholic abstinence generated recurring fish demand.
2. **Maritime logistics** — ships and colonies needed durable protein.
3. **Plantation slavery** — coerced labour systems needed cheap rationable food.
4. **Commodity capitalism** — fish could be graded, priced, shipped, and routed across unequal markets.

This makes salt fish a particularly dense historical object. It is at once pious food, poor food, sailor food, slave-ration food, diaspora food, and heritage food. Its meanings do not replace one another; they sediment.

The most critical formulation is:

> Salt fish was not simply a preserved fish. It was a technology for moving protein through empire, distributing quality by class and race, and converting distant marine ecologies into labour power, religious observance, and culinary memory.

## Brief Red Team Review

**Potential weakness: over-Atlantic framing.**
This version privileges cod, Catholic Europe, Newfoundland, New England, and the Caribbean. That is justified by the requested authors, but it underplays West African, South Asian, East Asian, Southeast Asian, Arctic, and Mediterranean salted-fish systems.

**Potential weakness: too much Mintz-by-analogy.**

**Potential weakness: provisioning evidence is uneven.**
We have strong evidence that Newfoundland salt fish supplied the British West Indies in large quantities, but precise colony-by-colony consumption patterns, quality grades, prices, and ration practices require archival work.

# 1. Harold A. Innis

## Brief biography

Harold Adams Innis, 1894–1952, was a Canadian economic historian and political economist, closely associated with the University of Toronto. He is widely identified with the **staples thesis**, the argument that Canadian historical development was shaped by export staples such as fur, fish, timber, wheat, and minerals. University of Toronto Press describes him as a foundational figure in Canadian economic history and notes that the staples thesis dominated Canadian economic-history discourse for decades; the University of Toronto archives similarly describe his work on fur, fisheries, wheat, and lumber as central to his interpretation of Canadian history. ([UTP Publishing][1])

Innis is useful here because he treats cod not as “food culture” but as **infrastructure**: a staple commodity that reorganized transport, capital, imperial rivalry, settlement, labour, and markets.

## Key bibliography

| Work                                                         |                  Date | Relevance                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------: | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| *The Fur Trade in Canada*                                    |                  1930 | Classic staples-thesis work; fur as a continental economic structure |
| *The Cod Fisheries: The History of an International Economy* |                  1940 | Core text for cod, Newfoundland, Atlantic trade, empire, and markets |
| *Political Economy in the Modern State*                      |                  1946 | Broader political-economic essays                                    |
| *Empire and Communications*                                  |                  1950 | Later communications/infrastructure theory                           |
| *The Bias of Communication*                                  |                  1951 | Media, knowledge, empire; less directly food-related                 |
| *Essays in Canadian Economic History*                        | posthumous collection | Useful synthesis of his economic-history arguments                   |

*The Cod Fisheries* is the essential work for this topic. JSTOR lists the University of Toronto Press edition of *The Cod Fisheries: The History of an International Economy* under Innis, and summaries of the work describe it as spanning five centuries of imperial competition over cod fisheries. ([JSTOR][2])

## Major themes and topics

**Staples theory.** Innis’s central move is to ask how export commodities shape whole societies. Cod is not just traded; it produces ports, credit networks, seasonal labour patterns, imperial dependencies, and colonial settlement.

**Empire and dependency.** Staples create external orientation. Colonies organize themselves around export markets and imperial needs, not necessarily local food security or diversified economies.

**Geography and transport.** Innis is unusually alert to rivers, coasts, shipping routes, preservation technologies, and distance. Salt cod becomes viable because preservation makes geography negotiable.

**Commodity networks.** Innis’s cod is a connective object: Newfoundland, New England, Iberia, the Mediterranean, the West Indies, Britain, France, and Catholic Europe become linked through a fishery.

**Weakness for this project.** Innis helps map the commodity chain, but he is less strong on race, enslaved consumption, gendered cooking labour, sensory culture, and the transformation of ration foods into Caribbean cuisines.

## What Innis contributes to salt-fish historiography

Innis lets us say: salt fish was not merely eaten; it was a **staple commodity system**. It moved through grades, ports, imperial rules, credit markets, and religious demand. The cod fishery was not marginal to empire. It was one of the systems that made Atlantic empire provisionable.

# 2. Sidney W. Mintz

## Brief biography

Sidney Wilfred Mintz, 1922–2015, was an American anthropologist best known for Caribbean anthropology, food studies, creolization, slavery, peasantries, and political economy. Johns Hopkins reported that Mintz co-founded its Department of Anthropology and was William L. Straus Jr. Professor Emeritus; biographical summaries identify him as a major figure in food anthropology, Caribbean studies, and the study of global capitalism. ([The Hub][3])

Mintz trained at Columbia and conducted major fieldwork in Puerto Rico, Jamaica, and Haiti. His work is important because it joins ethnography, archival history, Marxian political economy, and food culture. He is not primarily a salt-fish historian, but his method is extremely relevant.

## Key bibliography

| Work                                                        |        Date | Relevance                                                                  |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | ----------: | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| *Worker in the Cane*                                        |        1960 | Life history of a Puerto Rican sugar worker; labour and plantation society |
| *Caribbean Transformations*                                 |        1974 | Caribbean societies, history, culture, plantation structures               |
| *Sweetness and Power: The Place of Sugar in Modern History* |        1985 | Core model for food, capitalism, slavery, consumption, and class           |
| *The Birth of African-American Culture* with Richard Price  | 1976 / 1992 | Creolization, African continuities, cultural formation under slavery       |
| *Tasting Food, Tasting Freedom*                             |        1996 | Essays on food, culture, freedom, and historical anthropology              |
| *Three Ancient Colonies*                                    |        2010 | Comparative Caribbean historical anthropology                              |

Mintz’s own description of *Sweetness and Power* says he situated sugar within Western history because it was an old commodity central to the emergence of a global market, and because his Caribbean fieldwork led him to trace sugar backward through domestication, spread, and industrial-world importance. ([sidneymintz.net][4])

## Major themes and topics

**Production and consumption must be studied together.** This is Mintz’s key lesson. Sugar was not just grown in the Caribbean and consumed in Europe; production and consumption formed one historical system.

**Plantation capitalism.** Mintz treats the Caribbean plantation as an early modern-industrial institution: disciplined labour, monocrop production, export markets, and coerced work.

**Food and class formation.** *Sweetness and Power* follows sugar from luxury to mass working-class calorie and stimulant. The parallel for salt fish is its movement between prestige Catholic food, sailor ration, slave ration, poor people’s food, and heritage cuisine.

**Creolization.** Mintz and Price resist simplistic ideas that enslaved Africans either preserved African cultures intact or lost them entirely. Instead, they argue for cultural formation under constraint: deep structures, recombination, innovation.

**Weakness for this project.** Mintz is not writing directly about salt cod. Applying him to salt fish is an analytical extension. It is legitimate, but it should be marked as method transfer rather than direct textual authority.

## What Mintz contributes to salt-fish historiography

Mintz lets us ask better questions than “where did cod go?” He asks: **who caught it, who cured it, who transported it, who ate it, under what compulsion, and how did its meaning change?**

A Mintzian salt-fish history would not stop at trade routes. It would link North Atlantic fishers, merchants, enslaved Caribbean labourers, Catholic households, British and Iberian consumers, and post-emancipation cooks into one food system.

The sharp formulation: **sugar was the sweet commodity extracted from enslaved labour; salt fish was one of the salty commodities used to reproduce that labour.**

# 3. Mark Kurlansky

## Brief biography

Mark Kurlansky, born 1948, is an American journalist and popular historian known for commodity-focused narrative histories. His official biography describes him as a prolific author and journalist, and notes that *Cod*, *Salt*, *1968*, and *The Food of a Younger Land* were New York Times bestsellers; biographical summaries also note that *Cod: A Biography of the Fish That Changed the World* won the 1998 James Beard Award and became an international bestseller. Kurlansky’s relevance is not that he invented cod history. He made cod history readable for a broad audience. He belongs to the “commodity biography” genre: cod, salt, oysters, salmon, paper, milk, etc.

## Major themes and topics

**Commodity biography.** Kurlansky’s signature method is to follow one material object through global history.

**Ecological decline.** Kurlansky foregrounds overfishing and the collapse of cod stocks more strongly than many older economic histories. **Risk of overclaim.** The subtitle “the fish that changed the world” is rhetorically effective but analytically dangerous. Cod did not act alone. States, merchants, enslavers, Catholic fasting rules, salt supplies, credit markets, fishing labour, and imperial violence made cod powerful.

## What Kurlansky contributes to salt-fish historiography

Kurlansky gives the story momentum. He is especially useful for introducing readers to cod as a global object. But he should be corrected by Innis for structure, by Mintz for labour/consumption, and by Atlantic-world scholarship for slavery and racial capitalism.

# 5. Deep Dive on Atlantic-World Scholarship

## 5.1 What is “Atlantic-world” scholarship?

Atlantic-world history treats the Atlantic Ocean and its bordering regions — Europe, Africa, the Americas, and the Caribbean — as a connected historical field. The Oxford Research Encyclopedia describes Atlantic history as an approach that analyzes the Atlantic Ocean and adjoining continents as a single unit of historical analysis, emphasizing connections, circulations, and lives transformed by location within this region. ([OUP Academic][7])

This matters because salt fish does not make sense inside a single national frame. Portuguese bacalhau, Jamaican saltfish, Newfoundland cod, Basque fishing, Catholic Lent, Caribbean slavery, and British imperial shipping are not separate stories. They are different sites within one Atlantic system.

## 5.2 Core historiographic move

Older national histories might ask:

“What role did cod play in Newfoundland?”
“What is the origin of bacalhau in Portugal?”
“How did ackee and saltfish become Jamaican?”

Atlantic-world scholarship asks instead:

**How did preserved fish circulate through an oceanic system of empire, religion, slavery, migration, capital, ecology, and consumption?**

That changes the unit of analysis from **nation** to **circulation**.

## 5.3 Major Atlantic-world frames

### A. Circum-Atlantic circulation

This frame follows movement: fish, salt, sugar, rum, ships, enslaved people, credit, disease, recipes, and religious rules. Alison Games, writing in the *American Historical Review* in 2006, emphasizes that Atlantic approaches reveal connections previously obscured, while also warning that a fully Braudelian Atlantic history is difficult because the Atlantic was internally uneven and methodologically fragmented. ([OUP Academic][8])

For salt fish, this means the object is not “cod in Portugal” or “saltfish in Jamaica.” The object is the circuit:

```text
North Atlantic fishery
→ salting / drying / grading
→ Iberian and Mediterranean Catholic markets
→ West Indian plantation provisioning
→ sugar / molasses / rum exports
→ metropolitan consumption and capital
→ reinvestment in ships, fisheries, plantations
```

### B. Plantation political economy

This frame treats Caribbean plantations not as agricultural sites alone but as engines of racial capitalism. Salt fish enters as a provisioning commodity. It helped sustain plantation labour regimes where land was prioritized for sugar rather than diversified food production.

Heritage Newfoundland and Labrador’s public-history account states that British West Indian plantation owners needed cheap durable protein for enslaved and later free workers, and that Newfoundland salt fish supplied that demand; it reports shipments above 150,000 quintals to the British West Indies in both 1810 and 1811, peaking at 176,000 quintals in 1816. ([Arrow at TU Dublin][9])

The critical point is that food supply was not incidental. Provisioning was part of domination: how to reproduce coerced labour at minimum cost.

### C. Commodity grading and unequal markets

Salt fish was not one undifferentiated good. It was graded. Better fish could go to European markets; lower-grade or damaged fish could be sold to plantation markets. This is where class and race enter the commodity chain materially.

The historiographic implication: **inequality was not only in who owned plantations; it was also embedded in who ate which quality of fish.**

### D. Catholic abstinence and religious demand

Catholic fasting and abstinence created recurring demand for preserved fish. That demand helped make the cod trade viable and predictable. But Atlantic-world scholarship prevents over-crediting religion alone. Catholic demand explains much of Iberian and Mediterranean consumption; plantation slavery explains another major demand zone.

A tighter formulation:

**Catholic abstinence made salt fish a respectable fasting commodity. Plantation slavery made it a ration commodity. Maritime capitalism linked both.**

### E. Provision grounds and enslaved agency

The provisioning story should not reduce enslaved people to passive recipients of imported salt fish. Caribbean foodways also included provision grounds, markets, hunting, fishing, gathering, Indigenous crops, African botanical knowledge, and creole culinary invention.

Elizabeth DeLoughrey’s work on provision grounds notes that slave gardens were important sites of creolization and that enslaved people grew diverse crops, while Jamaican markets became substantial spaces of exchange. ([Department of English UCLA][10])

This complicates the saltfish story. Salt fish was imposed through plantation provisioning, but it entered food systems where enslaved and later free people actively combined it with local starches, greens, peppers, oils, dumplings, plantains, breadfruit, and ackee.

### F. Foodways and afterlives

S. I. Francis’s 2022 Dublin Gastronomy Symposium paper explicitly frames saltfish’s Caribbean trajectory as a movement from “slave” food to “poor people” food to “traditional” food, focusing on Trinidad and Tobago. ([Arrow at TU Dublin][11])

That trajectory is crucial. Saltfish is not frozen in its plantation meaning. It passes through several regimes:

```text
Imported ration
→ enslaved food
→ poor people’s food
→ everyday creole food
→ national / diasporic heritage food
→ restaurant / nostalgic / identity food
```

This does not erase coercion. It shows historical sedimentation.

# 6. Major Atlantic-world scholars and how they help

## Bernard Bailyn

Bailyn helped institutionalize Atlantic history in American historiography, especially through Harvard’s Atlantic History seminar. He is important less for salt fish specifically than for the methodological shift from national colonial histories to Atlantic interconnection. A 2012 historiographic article identifies Bailyn as a major contributor to the emergence of Atlantic history through his Harvard seminar. ([SHS Cairn.info][12])

**Use for salt fish:** he legitimizes the Atlantic as a historical unit, allowing cod, Catholic Europe, West Africa, Caribbean plantations, and North American fisheries to be studied together.

## David Armitage

Armitage is important for typology. His “three concepts of Atlantic history” are often summarized as **circum-Atlantic**, **trans-Atlantic**, and **cis-Atlantic** approaches. Oxford Bibliographies identifies Armitage’s essay as a key account of Atlantic history’s conceptual forms. ([oxfordbibliographies.com][13])

Applied to salt fish:

| Armitage-type lens | Salt-fish application                                                        |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Circum-Atlantic    | The whole oceanic system of cod, salt, sugar, slavery, Catholic markets      |
| Trans-Atlantic     | Specific flows: Newfoundland → West Indies; Basque fishery → Iberian markets |
| Cis-Atlantic       | How one place, e.g. Jamaica or Portugal, was shaped by Atlantic connections  |

## Alison Games

Games is useful because she warns against simplistic Atlantic totality. Her 2006 *AHR* essay notes that writing a broad Atlantic history is methodologically difficult because of disjunctions, disciplinary divisions, and vantage-point problems. ([OUP Academic][8])

**Use for salt fish:** her caution prevents an over-smooth diagram. The Atlantic was not a single machine. Newfoundland fishers, Portuguese consumers, enslaved Jamaicans, Basque merchants, British planters, and Catholic clergy were connected but not equivalent.

## Philip D. Morgan, Jack P. Greene, J. H. Elliott, David Eltis, Robin Blackburn, Hilary Beckles

These scholars matter for slavery, empire, colonial governance, and the Atlantic system. They do not all write on salt fish directly, but they provide the necessary background for understanding slavery, sugar, labour regimes, demography, and imperial competition.

**Use for salt fish:** they prevent a food-history account from becoming merely culinary. Salt fish belongs inside histories of plantation labour, shipping, slavery, and imperial rivalry.

## B. W. Higman and Caribbean foodways scholarship

Higman and later Caribbean food scholars are important for the internal Caribbean story: food production, provision grounds, markets, plantation diets, domestic labour, and post-emancipation culinary formation.

**Use for salt fish:** they shift the question from “what did planters import?” to “how did Caribbean people cook, combine, reinterpret, and remember these ingredients?”

# 7. How Atlantic-world scholarship changes the salt-fish argument

## From commodity to system

A simple food history says: “Salt cod was preserved and traded.”

An Atlantic-world history says: “Salt cod linked marine extraction, preservation technology, Catholic abstinence, imperial shipping, plantation provisioning, coerced labour, and post-slavery foodways.”

## From national dish to transnational residue

Portuguese bacalhau is not simply Portuguese. Jamaican saltfish is not simply Jamaican. Italian baccalà is not simply Italian. Each is a local endpoint of an Atlantic commodity system.

## From taste to power

Saltfish dishes are delicious, but their historical formation is not innocent. Taste emerges from constraint: rationing, poverty, religious discipline, preservation need, migration, and adaptation.

## From slavery as background to slavery as infrastructure

In weak food history, slavery appears as a tragic context. In stronger Atlantic history, slavery is infrastructure. It shapes demand, provisioning, land use, labour reproduction, commodity grades, and trade routes.

## From preservation to ecological extraction

The Atlantic cod story is also an environmental history. Cold-water abundance was treated as inexhaustible. Kurlansky’s ecological narrative is useful here: cod history ends not only in cuisine but in depletion and collapse.

# 9. Brief Red Team Review

**Potential weakness: Innis can over-structure the argument.**
He explains routes and staples well, but his framework can underplay enslaved agency, cooking labour, gender, and sensory life.

The Atlantic was not one smooth machine. It was fractured by empire, language, religion, ecology, local markets, war, and resistance.

## Preamble

East Asian salted fish is not the same historical object as Atlantic salt cod. It is less about Catholic fasting and oceanic empire, and more about **rice economies, coastal preservation, fermentation, urban poverty, household frugality, market trade, sensory intensity, diaspora memory, and health risk**. The central pattern is this: salted fish turns perishable marine abundance into **rice-compatible flavour**. Sometimes it is a main protein, but more often it is a **condiment-protein**: a small amount of fish transforms a large amount of rice, porridge, vegetables, tofu, pork, or pickled greens.

# 1. The Core Difference from Atlantic Salt Cod

Atlantic salt cod often travelled as a large commodity: cod caught in cold northern waters, dried, salted, shipped to Catholic Europe, navies, and Caribbean plantations. East Asian salted fish is usually a **smaller-scale sensory technology**: anchovies, mackerel, squid, roe, entrails, small fish, river fish, mullet roe, shrimp, oysters, and mixed seafood are salted, dried, fermented, or turned into paste/sauce.

A key scholarly point from a 2013 study on East Asian fermented fish is that salted fermented fish foods in the region can be classified into forms such as **jeot, jeotgal, paste, sauce, and sikhae**, and that **salt and rice** are central ingredients in these fermentation cultures. The author explicitly links fermented fish foods with rice-staple societies. ([Global Vision Press][1])

That rice connection is crucial. East Asian salted fish often answers this question:

> How do you make plain rice, congee, vegetables, tofu, or winter-stored foods more savoury with a small amount of preserved marine protein?

This is why salted fish in East Asia frequently appears in tiny quantities but has enormous culinary force.

---

# 2. Preservation Ecology: Salt, Rice, Fermentation, and Climate

Salted fish in East Asia sits at the intersection of four ecological pressures.

First, fish is seasonal and perishable. Coastal and riverine communities may have abundant fish at certain moments, but hot and humid climates make spoilage rapid.

Second, rice is filling but bland. Salted fish gives rice savour, protein, fat, aroma, and appetite stimulation.

Third, salt controls microbial activity. In some products, high salt suppresses spoilage almost completely. In others, moderate salt allows controlled fermentation.

Fourth, fermentation creates flavour. Proteins break down into peptides and amino acids, especially glutamate-rich savouriness. This is why fermented fish can function like a proto-MSG system: not merely salty, but deep, meaty, marine, and aromatic.

[INFERENCE] East Asian salted fish is therefore best understood as a **rice-amplification technology**. A small fish product stretches starch, turns scarcity into flavour, and stores seasonality for later use.

---

# 3. South China and the Cantonese Salted Fish World

## 3.1 鹹魚 / 咸鱼 as poverty food and flavour weapon

Cantonese salted fish — 鹹魚 / 咸鱼, Cantonese *haam4 jyu2*, Mandarin *xiányú* — is one of the clearest examples of salted fish as both food and social memory. Public summaries describe Cantonese salted fish as a traditional Guangdong food, historically associated with ordinary rice-based dinners and sometimes nicknamed “poor man’s food” because its saltiness could make simple meals more varied. ([Wikipedia][2])

Its classic uses are structurally revealing:

| Dish                                | Chinese         | Function                                   |
| ----------------------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Steamed pork patty with salted fish | 鹹魚蒸肉餅 / 咸鱼蒸肉饼   | salted fish perfumes pork fat and rice     |
| Chicken and salted fish fried rice  | 鹹魚雞粒炒飯 / 咸鱼鸡粒炒饭 | tiny fish fragments season the whole wok   |
| Salted fish with eggplant claypot   | 鹹魚茄子煲 / 咸鱼茄子煲   | fish gives depth to oil-absorbing eggplant |
| Salted fish with congee or rice     | 鹹魚配粥 / 咸鱼配粥     | minimal protein, maximal rice appetite     |

Cantonese home-cooking sources still present steamed pork patty with salted fish as a recognisable older-generation dish, strongly associated with family cooking and restaurant/home overlap. ([The Woks of Life][3])

## 3.2 下飯菜: “food that sends rice down”

A useful Chinese category is **下飯菜 / 下饭菜** *xiàfàncài*: foods that make rice go down easily. Salted fish is almost the perfect 下飯菜. It is not meant to be eaten in large pieces like a steak. Its job is to **activate appetite**.

This creates a sociology of scarcity. A small amount of intense preserved fish lets a household consume more rice, porridge, or cheap vegetables with satisfaction. In this sense, salted fish is not simply an ingredient. It is a technique of stretching food budgets.

[INFERENCE] The older Cantonese love of 鹹魚 / 咸鱼 is partly a memory of frugality: a cuisine where aroma, salt, pork fat, steam, and rice do more work than expensive meat.

## 3.3 Hong Kong dried seafood trade and urban memory

Hong Kong’s salted and dried seafood trade makes the urban dimension clearer. Zolima CityMag’s history of Hong Kong dried seafood notes that the salted fish trade began in Sai Ying Pun, where seaside location made it a natural base for the seafood industry; early businesses clustered around Mui Fong Street, with fish drying on rooftops and production taking place near living spaces. ([Zolima City Magazine][4])

This matters anthropologically. Salted fish was not hidden industrial food. It was visible, smelled in streets, dried above tenements, sold in shops, and embedded into neighbourhood economies. The scent of salted fish became part of urban sensory memory.

## 3.4 Health shadow: nasopharyngeal carcinoma

Any serious discussion of Chinese-style salted fish must include the health issue. IARC’s monograph on Chinese-style salted fish concluded that there is sufficient evidence in humans for carcinogenicity, that Chinese-style salted fish causes nasopharyngeal cancer, and that Chinese-style salted fish is carcinogenic to humans, Group 1. ([NCBI][5])

This does not mean all salted fish worldwide carries the same risk. The IARC classification is specifically for **Chinese-style salted fish**, and risk likely depends on preparation, fermentation, nitroso compounds, age at exposure, Epstein–Barr virus context, consumption frequency, and population background. ScienceDirect’s overview similarly notes proposed mechanisms including N-nitroso compounds and Epstein–Barr virus activation. ([ScienceDirect][6])

The cultural tension is sharp: Cantonese salted fish is both a memory food and a carcinogenic-risk food. This is not a contradiction; it is exactly how heritage foods often work. The same object can be affectively precious and medically problematic.

# 4. Japan: Salted Seafood, Sushi Before Sushi, and Drinking Foods

Japanese salted fish culture is not one thing. It includes fermented fish, salted entrails, preserved roe, regional sushi ancestors, and delicacies.

Because you prefer furigana for Japanese, I’ll give Japanese terms with furigana first, then plain forms.

## 4.1 塩辛（しおから） / 塩辛: salted-fermented seafood

**塩辛（しおから） / 塩辛** is made by salting raw seafood, often including viscera, and allowing enzymes and microorganisms to transform it. Nippon.com’s 2025 article describes shiokara as a fermented food made by salting raw seafood and letting it ferment naturally through enzymatic and microbial action. ([Nippon][7])

A broader seafood-culture article notes that shiokara is prepared by salting the entrails and muscles of fresh fish; squid shiokara is found across Japan, while skipjack tuna entrail versions appear as **酒盗（しゅとう） / 酒盗** in Kōchi and Nishiizu, and as **ワタガラス / watagarasu** in Okinawa. ([obayashi.co.jp][8])

The name 酒盗（しゅとう）literally suggests “sake thief” — something so salty and savoury that it makes one drink more. This points to a different social setting from Cantonese rice accompaniment: **酒肴（さかな） / 酒肴**, small foods eaten with alcohol.

## 4.2 熟れ鮨（なれずし） / 熟れ鮨: sushi as preservation before delicacy

Modern sushi is often imagined as fresh fish over vinegared rice. But older sushi forms were preservation systems. Japan’s Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries describes **熟れ鮨（なれずし） / 熟れ鮨** as fish preserved with rice under weight for a long time so lactic acid fermentation occurs; it identifies **鮒鮨（ふなずし） / 鮒鮨** as an old form and part of the background to later sushi. ([Ministry of Agriculture][9])

A MAFF regional cuisine page on Wakayama narezushi says there are several origin theories, but one account links it to salted mackerel stuffed with rice and wrapped in leaves; the rice fermented and combined with the fish. ([Ministry of Agriculture][10])

This changes the meaning of sushi. Sushi began not as pure freshness but as **fermented preservation**, where rice was originally part of the preservation environment. Modern sushi’s emphasis on freshness is historically late.

## 4.3 からすみ / 唐墨: salted roe as prestige

**からすみ / 唐墨** is salted and dried mullet roe, comparable to Mediterranean bottarga and Taiwanese 烏魚子. Public food-history summaries describe karasumi as made by salting mullet roe and drying it in sunlight, and as a high-priced Japanese delicacy eaten with sake. ([Wikipedia][11])

This is an important social contrast. Cantonese 鹹魚 often carries poverty or frugality associations. Japanese からすみ can carry elite, gift, or connoisseur associations. Salted fish is not automatically poor food; cured roe can become luxury.

| Funa-zushi | 鮒鮨（ふなずし）               | 鮒鮨             | intensely fermented lake-fish sushi; identity food |
| Karasumi   | 唐墨（からすみ）               | 唐墨             | salted roe delicacy                                |
| Shuto      | 酒盗（しゅとう）               | 酒盗             | salted bonito entrails; “sake thief”               |

[INFERENCE] Japanese salted fish culture has a stronger artisanal and regional-heritage register than Cantonese salted fish. It often moves from preservation into **craft**, **giftability**, **drinking culture**, and **regional pride**.

# 5. Korea: Jeotgal, Kimchi, and Fermentation as National Infrastructure

Korean salted seafood is most visible through **젓갈 / jeotgal** and **액젓 / aekjeot**. Jeotgal refers to salted fermented seafood, including fish, shrimp, roe, shellfish, and entrails. Hansik’s 2023 overview describes jeotgal as fermented salted fish flesh, roe, or offal, and links Korea’s broad jeotgal spectrum to the peninsula’s marine ecologies. ([hansik.or.kr][12])

## 5.1 Jeotgal as banchan and fermentation agent

Jeotgal can be eaten as a side dish, but it is also a fermentation ingredient. Korean Cultural Centre material describes kimchi as combining salted vegetables with condiments including salt, jeotgal, red pepper, green onion, garlic, ginger, and mustard greens; it emphasizes that jeotgal gives kimchi deeper taste. ([korean-culture.org][13])

A 2023 narrative review on kimchi history states that ancient Koreans found salted and seasoned vegetables, beans, seafood, and other foods could remain edible and develop distinctive flavour when stored in earthenware jars, **onggi**. It contrasts Korean reliance on fermentation with Chinese preservation patterns such as pickling and frying, while noting limited cooking-oil availability in Korea as one factor encouraging fermentation. ([Springer][14])

## 5.2 Kimchi changes the role of salted fish

In Korean cuisine, salted fish is often not visible as “fish.” It is absorbed into kimchi’s seasoning paste or brine. This creates a layered taste system:

```text
salted seafood
→ amino acids / savoury depth
→ kimchi fermentation
→ vegetable preservation
→ winter food security
→ national cuisine
```

This differs from Cantonese salted fish, where the fish remains recognisable in pork patty or fried rice. Korean jeotgal often disappears into a larger fermented system.

## 5.3 Korean social anthropology

Jeotgal sits in several social worlds:

| Context                  | Meaning                                                              |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Household kimchi-making  | domestic labour, seasonal preparation, intergenerational skill       |
| Regional seafood markets | coastal identity, local specialization                               |
| Banchan table            | small salty side dish that structures rice eating                    |
| Kimjang culture          | collective winter preparation                                        |
| Diaspora cooking         | authenticity marker; substitution problem for vegetarians or Muslims |

[INFERENCE] Jeotgal is a good example of salted fish as **invisible infrastructure**. Even when diners do not see seafood pieces, the taste of kimchi may depend on salted shrimp, anchovy extract, or fish sauce.

# 6. Taiwan and the Prestige of Salted Roe

Taiwan’s best-known salted seafood product is **烏魚子** *wūyúzǐ*, salted and dried mullet roe, comparable to Japanese からすみ and Mediterranean bottarga. Public summaries describe bottarga-like products across the Mediterranean and East Asia, including Japanese karasumi and Taiwanese wūyúzǐ, usually made from salted cured roe pouches of grey mullet or related fish. ([Wikipedia][15])

烏魚子 is important because it shows that salted fish preservation can become luxury. It is commonly sliced thin, warmed, paired with garlic sprout, daikon, or alcohol, and given as a festive or premium food.

# 7. East Asian Salted Fish as Class Marker

Salted fish moves between poverty and prestige.

## Poverty / frugality pole

Cantonese 鹹魚 with rice, congee, steamed pork, or eggplant belongs strongly to the frugality pole. Its saltiness allows a little protein to season much starch. It is associated with older generations, working-class households, preserved-food economies, and practical home cooking.

## Prestige / connoisseur pole

Japanese からすみ, Taiwanese 烏魚子, Korean premium roe-based jeotgal, and certain regional fermented products belong to the prestige pole. They may be expensive, seasonal, giftable, and artisanal.

## Boundary / outsider pole

Strongly fermented products — shiokara, funa-zushi, certain jeotgal, old salted fish — can become identity markers. Insiders read them as deep, mature, nostalgic, or refined. Outsiders read them as too salty, rotten, fishy, or difficult.

This produces a common anthropology of strong foods:

```text
scarcity technology
→ acquired taste
→ insider identity
→ outsider disgust
→ heritage pride
```

# 8. Smell, Disgust, and Cultural Boundary-Making

Salted fish has a smell problem — or rather, a smell power. It is volatile, persistent, and socially legible. The smell of frying or steaming salted fish can mark a household, a tenement, a market street, or a diaspora apartment.

Anthropologically, strong-smelling foods often become ethnic boundary markers. They are beloved in private but policed in shared public space. Salted fish, kimchi, dried seafood, fermented seafood, and fish sauce can all become targets of outsider disgust.

[INFERENCE] In diaspora contexts, salted fish often becomes a **smell of belonging under pressure**. It carries home memory, but it can also trigger shame or concealment when dominant food norms code it as “stinky.”

This is especially relevant to Chinese, Korean, Japanese, and Southeast Asian diasporas in Western cities, where dried seafood shops, kimchi refrigerators, fish sauce, and salted fish dishes often become sensory signs of immigrant foodways.

---

# 9. Gender, Household Labour, and Embodied Skill

Salted fish is not only a product; it is a domestic technique. Someone has to know how much to use, how long to soak, when to steam, how to balance salt with pork fat, rice, ginger, scallion, or vinegar, and how to prevent a dish from becoming unbearable.

In Cantonese cooking, the skill is often in **dosage**: too little and the dish is flat; too much and it becomes acrid and over-salted. In Japanese shiokara and narezushi, skill lies in curing, fermentation timing, texture, and smell control. In Korean kimchi-making, skill lies in the salt cure, seasoning paste, jeotgal selection, storage vessel, and fermentation temperature.

[INFERENCE] Because much of this knowledge historically lived in households, markets, and small producers, salted fish history is partly a history of **unwritten technique**. Archival sources often under-record the labour of women, elders, market vendors, fish processors, and home cooks.

# 10. Salted Fish and Diaspora

East Asian salted fish is intensely diasporic because it travels well. Dried seafood shops, jars of jeotgal, vacuum-packed karasumi, salted fish fried rice, kimchi with saeujeot, and shiokara in Japanese groceries all preserve not only fish but culinary memory.

Diaspora transformations include:

| Diaspora issue               | Effect                                                                                        |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Apartment smell restrictions | salted fish becomes occasional, hidden, or restaurant-only                                    |
| Health awareness             | reduced frequency, smaller amounts, nostalgia with caution                                    |
| Ingredient substitution      | fish sauce, anchovy paste, dried shrimp, miso, soy sauce, or MSG replace traditional products |
| Generational change          | elders preserve taste; younger generations may reinterpret it as heritage                     |
| Restaurant translation       | strong flavours softened for broader markets                                                  |

[INFERENCE] In diaspora, salted fish often shifts from everyday frugality to **occasional identity performance**. What was once a cheap rice companion becomes a memory dish.

# 11. Health, Modernity, and Moral Ambivalence

Salted fish faces three modern pressures.

First, refrigeration reduces its necessity. Once fresh fish and frozen seafood are widely available, salted fish survives only where taste and identity remain strong.

Second, sodium consciousness makes it suspect. Many salted fish products are extremely high in salt.

Third, specific carcinogenic concerns affect Chinese-style salted fish. IARC’s Group 1 classification is the strongest example and should not be minimized. ([NCBI][5])

This generates moral ambivalence: elders may view salted fish as heritage and thrift; public health may view it as risk; younger cooks may treat it as a rare flavouring rather than daily staple.

A balanced view is not “salted fish is bad” or “salted fish is heritage.” It is:

> Salted fish is a historically important preservation food whose culinary power is real, but some forms — especially Chinese-style salted fish eaten frequently or in childhood — carry documented health risks.

## 13.1 Salted fish as anti-freshness

Modern East Asian culinary branding often emphasizes freshness: sushi, sashimi, live seafood, market freshness, delicate fish. Salted fish complicates this. It is not fresh. # 14. A Strong Thesis

East Asian salted fish should be understood as a **rice-world preservation and flavour system**. It preserved marine abundance, stretched household starch, generated umami before industrial flavouring, and marked class, region, and diaspora identity. Unlike Atlantic salt cod, whose global history is dominated by Catholic fasting, maritime trade, and plantation provisioning, East Asian salted fish is more strongly organized around **household fermentation, rice accompaniment, coastal markets, sensory intensity, and regional memory**.

Its cultural power comes from contradiction: it is humble and luxurious, disgusting and delicious, dangerous and beloved, archaic and modern, invisible and overpowering. It is not merely preserved fish. It is a condensed social technology: salt, fish, rice, smell, labour, memory, and risk.

# 1. Salted Fish as Anti-Freshness

## 1.1 The false modern equation: East Asian seafood equals freshness

A common external image of East Asian seafood is freshness: sushi, sashimi, live tanks, delicate steamed fish, market-to-table fish. This is partly true, but it is not the whole system. East Asian fish cultures also value **ageing, drying, salting, fermenting, enzyme breakdown, and controlled microbial transformation**.

Japanese food history makes this especially clear. Japan’s Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries describes **熟れ鮨（なれずし）/ 熟れ鮨** as a traditional sushi made from salt-pickled fish and rice fermented through lactic acid, originally developed to preserve fish over long periods; the rice that supported fermentation was initially discarded. This directly contradicts the simplified modern equation of sushi with fresh raw fish. Source: MAFF, Japanese government food heritage page, current public institutional source. ([Ministry of Agriculture][1])

The same ministry’s fishery-fermented-food page describes **熟れ鮨（なれずし）/ 熟れ鮨** as fish stuffed with rice and pressed for long fermentation, identifies **鮒鮨（ふなずし）/ 鮒鮨** as one of the oldest forms, and explains that later vinegar-seasoned **早鮨（はやずし）/ 早鮨** eliminated the need for long fermentation, leading toward modern nigiri. Source: MAFF, Japanese government food heritage page. ([Ministry of Agriculture][2])

## 1.2 Fermentation as a rival aesthetic to freshness

In this lens, salted fish is not failed freshness. It is another aesthetic: **matured fish**. The desired qualities are not translucency, raw sweetness, or delicate texture, but sourness, saltiness, depth, aroma, amino-acid savouriness, and sometimes pungency.

Japanese examples:

| Product    | Japanese with furigana | Plain form | Anti-freshness logic                                  |
| ---------- | ---------------------- | ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Nare-zushi | 熟れ鮨（なれずし）              | 熟れ鮨        | fish preserved through rice-aided lactic fermentation |
| Funa-zushi | 鮒鮨（ふなずし）               | 鮒鮨         | intense fermented lake-fish heritage food             |
| Shiokara   | 塩辛（しおから）               | 塩辛         | salted seafood flesh/viscera transformed by enzymes   |
| Shuto      | 酒盗（しゅとう）               | 酒盗         | salted bonito entrails, strong alcohol companion      |

A 2021 review of fermented fish products in South and Southeast Asian cuisines also places Japanese **narezushi** and **shiokara** in broader fermented fish traditions, describing narezushi as fish fermented with additives such as salt, vegetables, or steamed rice. Source: Narzary et al., peer-reviewed review article, 2021. ([PMC][3])

## 1.3 Korean jeotgal as anti-freshness infrastructure

Korean **젓갈 / jeotgal** similarly challenges freshness ideology. Hansik, a Korean food culture institution, describes jeotgal as fermented salted fish flesh, roe, or offal, rooted in Korea’s marine geography and the variety of fish available around the peninsula. Source: Hansik magazine, Korean food culture institution, 2023. ([Hansik][4])

Jeotgal is not just a side dish; it is an engine of kimchi flavour. Scientific work on kimchi fermentation notes that jeotgal provides animal proteins and amino acids, improves savouriness, and affects fermentation dynamics. Source: Lee et al., food science study, 2019. ([ScienceDirect][5])

[INFERENCE] The more accurate aesthetic map is therefore not “fresh versus spoiled,” but:

```text
Fresh fish
  → sweetness, delicacy, clarity

Salted-dried fish
  → concentration, chew, salt, storage

Salted-fermented fish
  → amino acids, aroma, sourness, depth, controlled decay

Roe/offal ferments
  → intensity, connoisseurship, boundary taste
```

## 1.4 Western scholarship contribution

Western food anthropology helps name this as a problem of **modern sensory hierarchy**. Modern urban consumers often code freshness as purity, safety, and refinement, while fermentation and pungency are coded as backward, excessive, or risky. East Asian salted fish exposes the historical shallowness of that hierarchy. Before refrigeration, “freshness” was only one axis of value; durability, digestibility, salt balance, rice compatibility, and flavour concentration mattered as much.

**Critical point:** salted fish is not a degraded version of fresh fish. It is a different sensory technology.

# 2. Salted Fish as “Poor Umami”

## 2.1 The rice-economy problem

Rice is filling but mild. Salted fish solves the rice problem: it makes bland starch satisfying. This is why East Asian salted fish is often eaten in very small amounts. The point is not protein volume; the point is **flavour leverage**.

In Chinese terms, salted fish belongs to the logic of **下飯菜 / 下饭菜**: food that “sends rice down.” Cantonese 鹹魚 / 咸鱼 does this almost perfectly. A small piece flavours a whole bowl of rice, a pork patty, a claypot of eggplant, or a wok of fried rice.

[INFERENCE] This makes salted fish a **poor umami technology**: a cheap, durable, intensely savoury substance that stretches rice, porridge, vegetables, tofu, and small amounts of meat.

## 2.2 Cantonese 鹹魚 / 咸鱼 and frugality

Cantonese salted fish is often remembered as humble, working-class, old-generation food. It concentrates salt, marine protein, fat-soluble aroma, and putrefactive-fermented depth. In dishes like **鹹魚蒸肉餅 / 咸鱼蒸肉饼** or **鹹魚茄子煲 / 咸鱼茄子煲**, it is not merely added; it organizes the dish.

The sociology is important: this is not luxury umami like dried scallop or premium roe. It is harsh, economical, and forceful. It belongs to a kitchen where small ingredients must do large work.

## 2.3 Korean jeotgal and kimchi as distributed umami

Korean jeotgal shows the same logic at a national scale. It does not always appear as visible fish. It disappears into kimchi, seasoning paste, brines, and side dishes. A study on kimchi made with jeotgal alternatives states that jeotgal is rich in free amino acids, contributes to kimchi’s savoury umami and flavour, and is also responsible for fishy smell and high salt content. Source: Lee et al., *Foods*, peer-reviewed food-science article, 2021. ([MDPI][6])

This gives us a more precise formulation: jeotgal is not just “fish in kimchi.” It is a **fermentation substrate and umami donor**.

## 2.4 Asian scholarship contribution

Asian food-science scholarship tends to specify the biochemical mechanism: salting, amino acids, microbial communities, lactic acid bacteria, aroma compounds, storage stability. A 2024 review on salt-fermented fish notes that Korean jeotgal is prepared by adding roughly 5–30% salt to raw shrimp, shellfish, fish, or other seafood materials; it places jeotgal among global salt-fermented fish products. Source: Cha, peer-reviewed review article, 2024. ([Springer][7])

A 2016 global review of fermented foods emphasizes that modern molecular methods have clarified the microbial diversity of fermented foods and beverages, showing that fermentation traditions are not vague “rotting” but structured microbial ecologies. Source: Tamang et al., *Frontiers in Microbiology*, peer-reviewed review, 2016. ([Frontiers][8])

## 2.5 Western scholarship contribution

Mintz’s food-power approach is useful here even though he wrote about sugar rather than salted fish. The question is not simply what people liked; it is how labouring households made cheap calories palatable. In a rice society, salted fish plays a role parallel to molasses, tea, sugar, pickles, or sauces elsewhere: it makes staple consumption repetitive but tolerable.

[INFERENCE] Salted fish turns scarcity into flavour. That does not romanticize poverty; it shows culinary intelligence under constraint.

# 3. Salted Fish as Smell-Politics

## 3.1 Smell is social power

Salted fish is not only tasted. It announces itself. It travels through markets, stairwells, tenement corridors, kitchens, lunchrooms, and apartment ventilation. This makes it a social object before it is eaten.

Smell is politically charged because it crosses boundaries without permission. A dried seafood shop can be beloved by insiders and treated as offensive by outsiders. A pot of kimchi jjigae, fried salted fish, or steamed 鹹魚 may become an ethnic marker in shared housing or workplaces.

## 3.2 Western scholarship on race, food, and smell

Derya Karaosmanoğlu’s 2020 article on studying ethnic food through senses and power explicitly notes, drawing on Ben Highmore and Uma Narayan, that taste and smell are central both to everyday racism and to intercultural conviviality. Source: Karaosmanoğlu, peer-reviewed article, 2020. ([Springer][9])

Rachel Slocum’s 2011 article “Race in the study of food” argues that food scholarship needs explicit engagement with race because racism shapes food systems and food can help theorize race itself. Source: Slocum, peer-reviewed geography article, 2011. ([Sage Journals][10])

Applied to salted fish, this means smell is not trivial. It is one way racial and class boundaries are enforced: “your food smells” often means “your body, household, and culture are out of place.”

## 3.3 Diaspora and the policing of kitchen air

In diaspora, salted fish often becomes divided between private memory and public embarrassment. It may be eaten at home but avoided in shared flats. It may be cooked only when windows can open. It may be displaced to restaurants. It may be replaced by less pungent ingredients.

The point is not only racism. There is also class. Strong fish smells can be associated with older immigrant apartments, dense housing, small kitchens, market streets, or lower-income neighbourhoods. In Hong Kong, the dried seafood trade historically shaped neighbourhood sensory landscapes. A history of Hong Kong dried seafood notes that Sai Ying Pun became a base for salted fish trading because of its seaside location, with early businesses around Mui Fong Street and fish drying on rooftops. Source: Zolima CityMag, public history / urban culture article, 2020. ([doingsts.com][11])

[INFERENCE] Salted fish smell therefore does two opposite things: it creates belonging for those who recognize it, and it creates social vulnerability when outsiders pathologize it.

## 3.4 Asian internal smell politics

It is not only Western racism. East Asian societies also rank smells internally. Salted fish can be seen as old-fashioned, coarse, rural, working-class, overly southern, overly Korean, overly Japanese-regional, or unsuitable for refined dining. Strong foods often mark local identity precisely because they are not universally liked.

Examples:

| Smell object           | Insider reading                                 | Outsider or elite reading         |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Cantonese 鹹魚 / 咸鱼      | home, old taste, rice appetite, wok memory      | stinky, poor, unhealthy           |
| Korean 젓갈              | depth, proper kimchi, regional seafood identity | fishy, too strong, non-vegetarian |
| Japanese 塩辛（しおから） / 塩辛 | sake food, mature taste, adult palate           | slimy, too salty, offal-like      |
| 熟れ鮨（なれずし） / 熟れ鮨        | regional heritage, ancestral sushi              | rotten, difficult, archaic        |

## 3.5 Critical synthesis

Smell is not decorative. It is how salted fish becomes social. It marks who belongs in the kitchen, who can define cleanliness, whose food counts as refined, and whose food must be hidden.

# 4. Salted Fish as Carcinogenic Heritage

## 4.1 The hard case: Chinese-style salted fish

This is the most uncomfortable angle. Chinese-style salted fish is not just “high sodium.” It has a specific cancer-risk literature. IARC’s monograph states that there is sufficient evidence in humans for the carcinogenicity of Chinese-style salted fish, that it causes nasopharyngeal cancer, and that Chinese-style salted fish is carcinogenic to humans, Group 1. Source: IARC monograph via NCBI Bookshelf, authoritative carcinogen evaluation, 2012 online version of earlier monograph material. ([NCBI][12])

The older IARC summary is even more useful for avoiding overgeneralization: it classifies **Chinese-style salted fish** as Group 1, while “other salted fish” is not classifiable as to carcinogenicity in humans, Group 3. Source: IARC summary via INCHEM, 1997 web summary of Volume 56. ([InChem][13])

So the correct claim is not “salted fish causes cancer.” The accurate claim is narrower: **Chinese-style salted fish is classified as carcinogenic to humans, with strongest concern around nasopharyngeal carcinoma.**

## 4.2 Childhood exposure and dose

The risk literature particularly emphasizes early-life exposure. A classic Hong Kong case-control study by Yu et al. reported that consumption of Cantonese-style salted fish was significantly associated with nasopharyngeal carcinoma in young Hong Kong Chinese, with especially strong associations during childhood. Source: Yu et al., peer-reviewed epidemiological study, 1986. ([PubMed][14])

A 2019 study summary states that Chinese-style salted fish intake in early life is considered an established risk factor for nasopharyngeal carcinoma, while adult intake results are more inconsistent. Source: Barrett et al., peer-reviewed nutrition/cancer epidemiology article, 2019. ([ScienceDirect][15])

Hong Kong clinical-public health material reports dose-response associations with heavy weekly or daily consumption and notes that prolonged exposure, especially since childhood, increases risk. Source: *Hong Kong Practitioner*, medical article, 2004. ([hkcfp.org.hk][16])

## 4.3 Mechanisms: nitroso compounds, fermentation, EBV context

The mechanism is not simply “salt is bad.” Candidate mechanisms include N-nitroso compounds formed during production and cooking, volatile nitrosamines, and interactions with Epstein–Barr virus biology in nasopharyngeal carcinoma risk. Hong Kong’s Centre for Food Safety states that Chinese-style salted fish is known to cause nasopharyngeal cancer and notes that N-nitroso compounds can form during production and steaming. Source: Hong Kong Centre for Food Safety, government food safety article, 2018. ([Food Hygiene Dept][17])

A 1981 epidemiological article found that incidence data and experimental evidence were most consistent with Cantonese salted fish consumption as a major etiologic factor in southern Chinese nasopharyngeal carcinoma patterns. Source: Mimi et al., peer-reviewed cancer epidemiology article, 1981. ([ScienceDirect][18])

## 4.4 The anthropology of “dangerous heritage”

Here the issue becomes anthropological rather than only medical. What happens when a beloved memory food is also a documented carcinogenic-risk food?

There are several possible community responses:

| Response               | Meaning                                                     |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Denial                 | “Our grandparents ate this; it is fine”                     |
| Rarity                 | Eat occasionally as heritage rather than staple             |
| Substitution           | Use dried shrimp, anchovy, fish sauce, miso, MSG, mushrooms |
| Medicalization         | Treat it as a risk food, especially for children            |
| Nostalgia with caution | Preserve recipes but reduce frequency and portion           |
| Heritage conflict      | Public health messaging feels like an attack on identity    |

[INFERENCE] Chinese-style salted fish is a model case of **carcinogenic heritage**: a food whose cultural value is real, but whose risk cannot be dismissed as Western food prejudice.

## 4.5 Avoiding two bad arguments

Bad argument one: “It is traditional, therefore safe.”
This is false. Tradition does not nullify carcinogenicity.

Bad argument two: “It is carcinogenic, therefore culturally worthless.”
Also false. Risk does not erase memory, labour, flavour, poverty history, or diaspora meaning.

The better formulation:

> Chinese-style salted fish should be treated as a historically important Cantonese/South Chinese food with documented nasopharyngeal carcinoma risk, especially with frequent or childhood exposure. Its cultural interpretation and health-risk management must be held together.

# 5. Salted Fish as Invisible Animal Presence

## 5.1 The problem: “vegetable” food that is not vegetarian

Salted fish often disappears. It becomes sauce, extract, paste, broth, powder, or seasoning. This matters for Buddhism, vegetarianism, veganism, halal observance, allergy, and diaspora food labelling.

Korean kimchi is the clearest case. Many diners think of kimchi as fermented vegetables. But many kimchi styles use **젓갈 / jeotgal**, **새우젓 / saeujeot** salted shrimp, **멸치액젓 / myeolchi-aekjeot** anchovy fish sauce, or related seafood seasonings.

Food-science work explicitly notes that jeotgal provides animal proteins and amino acids in kimchi, improves savoury taste, and varies by region and preference. Source: Lee et al., food-science article, 2019. ([ScienceDirect][5])

A Korean food culture source similarly defines jeotgal as fermented salted fish flesh, roe, or offal, and describes its variety across Korean marine ecologies. Source: Hansik, 2023. ([Hansik][4])

## 5.2 Buddhist cuisine as a counter-system

Korean Buddhist temple cuisine makes the invisible animal problem explicit by excluding it. A 2019 ACS Symposium Series chapter on Buddhist temple foods states that temple kimchi does not use **jeotgal**, salted and fermented fish, or the pungent five vegetables. Source: ACS Symposium Series chapter, scholarly food chemistry/culture volume, 2019. ([ACS Publications][19])

The official Korean Temple Food site presents temple food as a distinct Buddhist culinary tradition maintained by Korean Buddhist institutions. Source: Korean Temple Food official site, Cultural Corps of Korean Buddhism. ([한국사찰음식 Korea Temple Food][20])

This is not a minor recipe adjustment. It produces a different umami architecture:

```text
Lay kimchi:
vegetable + salt + chili + garlic/scallion + jeotgal/fish sauce
→ animal amino acids + lactic fermentation + pungency

Temple kimchi:
vegetable + salt + plant/mineral/seaweed/mushroom/perilla-based savouriness
→ plant-based fermentation + Buddhist discipline
```

## 5.3 Japanese and Chinese parallels

In Japan, fish-based seasonings such as dashi, bonito flakes, salted entrails, and fermented fish products can similarly make apparently vegetable dishes non-vegetarian. In Chinese cuisines, dried shrimp, dried scallop, oyster sauce, fish sauce, salted fish, and dried seafood can enter vegetable dishes as background savouriness.

[INFERENCE] East Asian cuisines often do not separate “meat dish” and “vegetable dish” using modern vegetarian categories. They often distinguish instead by **main visible ingredient**, while animal-derived seasonings remain structurally present.

## 5.4 Why this matters anthropologically

Invisible animality exposes a mismatch between food taxonomies:

| Traditional culinary taxonomy                                        | Modern dietary taxonomy                                |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Is the main ingredient vegetable, rice, tofu, fish, pork?            | Does it contain any animal-derived ingredient?         |
| Is it appropriate for rice, alcohol, winter storage, ritual, guests? | Is it vegan, vegetarian, halal, kosher, allergen-safe? |
| Does it taste balanced?                                              | What hidden ingredients are in the seasoning base?     |

This matters especially for Buddhism because East Asian Buddhist food practices vary by region, period, and institution. It also matters for modern veganism and halal food because seafood-derived condiments can be invisible in translation.

## 5.5 Critical synthesis

Salted fish is often culturally powerful precisely when it is not visible. It operates beneath the surface: in kimchi, sauces, rice dishes, broths, pastes, and condiments. This makes it a hidden architecture of taste — but also a hidden ethical and dietary problem.

> East Asian salted fish is a boundary technology. It mediates between fresh and rotten, poor and refined, rice and protein, home and public space, heritage and disease risk, vegetable dishes and animal-derived flavour, insider memory and outsider disgust.

Cantonese 鹹魚, Korean 젓갈, Japanese 熟れ鮨（なれずし）/ 熟れ鮨, and Taiwanese 烏魚子 are not one tradition. This analysis compares them as analogous salted-fish systems, not as a single lineage.

The general scholarship on smell, race, and ethnic food is strong, but I did not find a large body of salted-fish-specific diaspora ethnography. The application to salted fish is plausible but should be marked [INFERENCE].

**Potential weakness: health-risk discussion may dominate Cantonese salted fish.**
That risk is real and strongly evidenced, but overemphasis can erase poverty history, taste memory, and household labour.

**Potential weakness: Buddhist/vegetarian discussion is clearer for Korean temple kimchi than for all East Asian Buddhist cuisines.**
Japan, China, Taiwan, and Korean lay practice differ substantially.
