## Abstract in English

This discussion develops a comparative taxonomy of Chinese heat-based cooking terms, beginning with the contrast between **煨** *(wēi)* and **㸆** *(kào)* and expanding into a broader system including **燉、燜、燒、炙、烹、炒、爆、炸、煎、熘、蒸、煮、汆、燴、扒、熏**. The analysis shows that Chinese culinary verbs often encode not only heat transfer, but also vessel logic, sequence, liquid level, sauce reduction, texture, and regional kitchen practice. When mapped against French culinary terminology, the strongest correspondences occur where the medium is physically clear, such as **炸** with *frire*, **蒸** with *cuire à la vapeur*, and **熏** with *fumer*. Weaker correspondences arise for terms such as **燒、㸆、熘、燴、扒、烹**, where Chinese words compress multi-step processes that French would usually distribute across verbs such as *braiser, réduire, napper, lier,* and *glacer*.

The comparison was then extended to Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai, Malay-Indonesian, and other culinary systems. These analogues show that French is useful for general heat-transfer and sauce-action analysis, but East and Southeast Asian systems often provide closer structural matches for certain Chinese techniques. Korean **조림** *(jorim)*, Vietnamese **kho**, and late-stage Malay-Indonesian **rendang**, for example, illuminate the reduction-braise logic of **㸆** more effectively than a single French term. Finally, Cantonese terms such as **炆、焗、灼、煲** were examined as regional technical words with distinct semantic histories: **炆** as low-fire sauce braising, **焗** as enclosed heat, **灼** as precise scald-cooking, and **煲** as vessel-led slow simmering. Taken together, the discussion argues that Chinese culinary vocabulary is best understood as a layered technical language in which heat, medium, vessel, time, sauce, and regional history are fused rather than neatly separated.

## Topic map

**煨** and **㸆** both belong to the Chinese vocabulary of *slow heat*, but they are not equivalent.

**煨** is broader and older: buried-in-ashes roasting, low-fire simmering, stewing, preserving internal moisture.
**㸆** is narrower and more regional: Jiangnan / Zhejiang-style slow cooking that reduces liquid until flavour adheres tightly to the ingredient.

## 3. Culinary philosophy

### Established knowledge

**煨** belongs to a philosophy of *enclosure and patience*. Food is protected from direct violence of flame: buried in ash, wrapped in clay, enclosed in a pot, or held below boiling. The result is moisture retention, internal softening, and flavour integration. Modern descriptions define 煨 as either ash-cooking or long, gentle simmering, especially where moisture and original flavour are preserved. ([食力 foodNEXT][3])

**㸆** belongs to a philosophy of *concentration and adhesion*. The liquid is not merely a cooking medium; it is progressively reduced until it becomes coating, glaze, and flavour architecture. A Ningbo food account defines 㸆 as low-fire slow stewing that reduces the cooking liquid, allowing the ingredient to absorb flavour and become more concentrated and firm. ([Sina News][4])

### Interpretation

The contrast is almost metaphysical:

| Axis                   | 煨                             | 㸆                                     |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Heat logic             | Concealed, gentle, enveloping | Persistent, reductive, adhesive       |
| Moisture logic         | Preserve internal juice       | Reduce external sauce                 |
| Texture                | Soft, yielding, protected     | Dense, glazed, savoury-sweet, compact |
| Philosophical metaphor | **nurture by enclosure**      | **intensification by reduction**      |
| Best examples          | 煨番薯, 煨湯, 黃泥煨雞                 | 㸆菜, 㸆肉, 苔條㸆五花肉, 鯗㸆肉                   |

煨 treats heat as an environment.
㸆 treats heat as a discipline.

## 4. Historiography

### 煨: older, wider, more archaeologically plausible

**煨** plausibly belongs to a very old stratum of cooking: fire, ash, earth, embers, enclosed heat. Sources describing early Chinese cooking link pre-vessel “fire cooking” with placing food directly in fire or burying it in ash to roast / 煨. ([Sohu][5])

Lexically, 煨 is also well-attested in dictionary traditions. Wiktionary’s entry lists classical references and gives the culinary meaning as slow cooking over low fire; it also preserves a textual example involving cooking food with improvised fuel in snowy conditions. ([Wiktionary][6])

**Historiographic implication:** 煨 sits at the boundary between primitive fire-cooking and refined slow-cooking. It can mean both “roast in ashes” and “simmer slowly,” because both share the same principle: indirect, moderated heat.

### 㸆: regional, specialist, probably recipe-register rather than canonical-register

**㸆** is different. It is not a major classical culinary verb in the way 煨, 燒, 炙, 烹, 煮, 蒸, 煎, 炒 are. It appears much more as a regional culinary graph associated with Jiangnan / Zhejiang / Ningbo / Hangzhou-style cooking. Modern food writing explicitly identifies dishes such as **苔條㸆五花肉** as Ningbo-style, and explains 㸆 as slow cooking and reducing the sauce. ([Sina News][4])

The fact that Taiwan’s official Mandarin dictionary returns no standard entry for **㸆** supports the view that it is not fully integrated into high-standard national lexicography. ([Revised Mandarin Dictionary][2])

**Historiographic implication:** 㸆 is probably best treated as a *regional technical term that entered written culinary Chinese from practice*, not as a pan-Chinese classical category.

## Bottom line

**煨** = slow, enclosed, moist, patient heat; historically deep and semantically broad.
**㸆** = Jiangnan slow braise-reduction; regionally marked, sauce-concentrating, less lexicographically canonical.

**Best compressed distinction:**
**煨 preserves essence by protecting it from harsh fire. 㸆 intensifies essence by slowly reducing everything around it.**

**Confidence:** Moderate-high for 煨; moderate for 㸆. The main uncertainty is early documentary history of 㸆.

## Topic map

These seven terms are best mapped along **five axes**:

1. **Fire contact** — direct flame, radiant heat, oil, water, steam, enclosed pot.
2. **Liquid level** — dry, moist, submerged, sauce-reduction.
3. **Enclosure** — open fire, covered vessel, sealed vessel, buried heat.
4. **Temporal logic** — quick finishing vs long transformation.
5. **Culinary philosophy** — softening, sealing, concentrating, charring, harmonising.

The taxonomy is not perfectly stable across regions. **燒** and **烹** are especially broad; **㸆** is regionally marked; **煨** has an older ash-roasting sense and a later slow-stewing sense.

## 3. Individual technique profiles

### 煨 *(wēi)*

**Core meaning:** slow, indirect cooking. Older usage emphasizes **hot ash / embers**; later culinary usage extends to **low-fire slow cooking**.

**Technique:** Food is buried, wrapped, sealed, or slowly heated until the inside softens without violent boiling or direct scorching.

**Examples:**
**煨番薯** *(wēi fānshǔ)* — ash-roasted sweet potato
**煨栗子** *(wēi lìzi)* — roasted chestnuts in embers
**煨牛肉** *(wēi niúròu)* — slow-simmered beef
**瓦罐煨湯** *(wǎguàn wēi tāng)* — crock slow-simmered soup

**Philosophy:** **protection by enclosure**. 煨 does not attack the ingredient; it surrounds it.

**Historiographic note:** This is likely one of the older cooking logics in the set because ash, embers, clay, and buried heat precede complex vessel-based cuisine. The dictionary evidence preserves that older “hot ash” layer clearly. ([dict.revised.moe.edu.tw][1])

---

### 燉 *(dùn)*

**Core meaning:** long, moist cooking in liquid.

**Technique:** Ingredient is cooked in water, broth, or stock over low heat. In modern Chinese cuisine, 燉 may mean ordinary stewing or more refined **隔水燉** *(géshuǐ dùn)*, double-boiling, where the food vessel is indirectly heated in water.

**Examples:**
**燉雞湯** *(dùn jī tāng)* — stewed chicken soup
**清燉牛腩** *(qīngdùn niúnǎn)* — clear-stewed beef brisket
**燉補** *(dùnbǔ)* — tonic stewing

**Philosophy:** **extraction and harmonisation**. The ingredient yields essence into the broth, while the broth returns flavour into the ingredient.

**Difference from 煨:** 燉 is more liquid-forward. 煨 may be ash-based, pot-based, or low-fire braising; 燉 normally implies a broth or soup environment.

---

### 燜 *(mèn)*

**Core meaning:** covered cooking by retained heat, vapour, and moisture.

**Technique:** Food, seasoning, and some liquid are placed in a pot; the lid is closed tightly; the food cooks through trapped steam and internal heat. The Education Ministry definition explicitly stresses a tight lid and cooking or softening food through the pot’s internal steam and temperature. ([dict.revised.moe.edu.tw][2])

**Examples:**
**燜肉** *(mèn ròu)* — covered-braised meat
**油燜筍** *(yóumèn sǔn)* — oil-braised bamboo shoots
**黃燜雞** *(huángmèn jī)* — yellow-braised chicken

**Philosophy:** **retention**. 燜 traps aroma, liquid, and heat. It is less about extracting into soup than forcing flavour to circulate inside a closed system.

**Difference from 燉:** 燉 is liquid-dominant; 燜 is enclosure-dominant.

**Difference from 㸆:** 燜 may retain sauce; 㸆 drives sauce toward reduction.

---

### 㸆 *(kào)*

**Core meaning:** regional Jiangnan / Ningbo-style slow cooking where sauce becomes thick, absorbed, or nearly dry.

**Technique:** Ingredients are first fried, sautéed, or seared; seasonings and liquid are added; the dish is moved to lower heat; the sauce gradually thickens, reduces, or dries down. Ningbo culinary sources describe 㸆 as close to **燒** but lower in heat, longer in time, and marked by the gathering-in of the braising liquid. ([nb.ifeng.com][3])

**Examples:**
**寧波㸆菜** *(Níngbō kào cài)* — Ningbo reduction-braised greens
**㸆肉** *(kào ròu)* — reduction-braised pork
**苔條㸆五花肉** *(táitiáo kào wǔhuāròu)* — pork belly reduction-braised with seaweed strips
**蔥㸆河鯽魚** *(cōng kào héjìyú)* — crucian carp reduction-braised with scallion

**Philosophy:** **intensification by reduction**. 㸆 is not merely cooking until done; it cooks until flavour clings.

**Difference from 烤 *(kǎo)*:** despite graphic and menu confusion, **㸆 *(kào)*** is not ordinary dry roasting. Ningbo sources explicitly distinguish local “火靠 / 㸆” from air-heat roasting such as roast duck or char siu. ([nb.ifeng.com][3])

**Historiographic note:** 㸆 is much less canonical than 煨, 燉, 燜, 燒, 炙, 烹. It looks like a regional kitchen term that entered writing from practice, especially Jiangnan / Ningbo cookery.

---

### 燒 *(shāo)*

**Core meaning:** to burn, heat, cook; in cuisine, often to braise, roast, or cook with sauce.

**Technique:** Extremely broad. In older and general usage, 燒 can mean burning or roasting. In culinary usage, it often refers to **braising after preliminary frying/searing**, especially in compounds such as **紅燒** *(hóngshāo)*.

**Examples:**
**紅燒肉** *(hóngshāo ròu)* — red-braised pork
**乾燒魚** *(gānshāo yú)* — dry-braised fish
**叉燒** *(chāshāo)* — Cantonese roast pork
**鐵板燒** *(tiěbǎnshāo)* — teppan-style hot-plate cooking

**Philosophy:** **transformation by fire into savoury depth**. 燒 is the middle term: it can point upward toward roasting or downward toward braising.

**Difference from 㸆:** 㸆 is narrower and more reduction-focused. 燒 may stop at braising; 㸆 pushes further toward thickened, absorbed sauce.

**Difference from 炙:** 炙 is more specifically direct roasting/grilling; 燒 is broader.

---

### 炙 *(zhì)*

**Core meaning:** roast, grill, scorch by direct heat.

**Technique:** Meat or other food is exposed to radiant heat, flame, or hot surfaces. The Ministry of Education dictionary defines 炙 as roasting/scorching and also gives it as a noun for roasted meat. ([dict.revised.moe.edu.tw][4])

**Examples:**
**炙肉** *(zhì ròu)* — roasted meat
**膾炙人口** *(kuàizhì rénkǒu)* — literally “sliced raw fish and roasted meat are in people’s mouths”; figuratively, widely praised
**炙手可熱** *(zhìshǒu kěrè)* — so hot it burns the hand; figuratively, powerful/influential

**Philosophy:** **exposure**. Unlike 煨 or 燜, 炙 does not protect the food from fire. It subjects the surface to direct heat, producing browning, aroma, and sometimes char.

**Historiographic note:** 炙 belongs to an old stratum of meat cookery and ritual vocabulary. Its presence in classical textual examples makes it more archaic and literarily visible than many later regional kitchen terms. ([dict.revised.moe.edu.tw][4])

---

### 烹 *(pēng)*

**Core meaning:** to cook; also a specific technique involving oil-heating followed by seasoning and brief boiling.

**Technique:** In the broadest sense, 烹 is the umbrella word behind **烹飪** *(pēngrèn)*, cooking. In a narrower technical sense, the dictionary defines it as putting food into hot oil until semi-fried or cooked, then adding seasonings and briefly boiling. ([dict.revised.moe.edu.tw][5])

**Examples:**
**烹調** *(pēngtiáo)* — cooking / cuisine preparation
**烹飪** *(pēngrèn)* — cookery
**烹魚** *(pēng yú)* — cook fish
**烹茗** *(pēng míng)* — prepare tea

**Philosophy:** **culinary transformation itself**. 烹 is less a single heat technique than the act of converting raw material into edible culture.

**Difference from the others:** 烹 is both narrower and broader. Narrowly, it is a quick oil-and-seasoning technique. Broadly, it names the whole civilizational act of cooking.

### A. Dry-fire family

| Term  |   Pinyin | Position                                                      |
| ----- | -------: | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **炙** |  **zhì** | Direct roasting/grilling; close to flame and surface browning |
| **燒** | **shāo** | Can mean roast/burn, but also overlaps with braising          |
| **煨** |  **wēi** | Indirect dry-moist heat, often ash/ember/burial logic         |

**Internal contrast:**
**炙** exposes.
**燒** transforms.
**煨** shelters.

---

### B. Moist slow-cooking family

| Term  |   Pinyin | Position                                       |
| ----- | -------: | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **燉** |  **dùn** | Liquid-based long cooking                      |
| **燜** |  **mèn** | Covered moist cooking using trapped vapour     |
| **煨** |  **wēi** | Low-fire softening; may overlap with slow stew |
| **燒** | **shāo** | Sauce-braise, especially in modern compounds   |

**Internal contrast:**
**燉** lets the ingredient enter the broth.
**燜** keeps vapour and aroma inside the vessel.
**煨** uses low heat to soften without violence.
**燒** often seasons, colours, and finishes.

---

### C. Reduction / concentration family

| Term   |       Pinyin | Position                                                    |
| ------ | -----------: | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **㸆**  |      **kào** | Strongest reduction logic: sauce thickens, clings, or dries |
| **乾燒** |  **gānshāo** | Dry-braise; related logic but not identical                 |
| **紅燒** | **hóngshāo** | Soy-sugar braise; may remain saucier than 㸆                 |
| **燜**  |      **mèn** | Can reduce, but reduction is not the defining feature       |

**Internal contrast:**
**㸆** is not just “braise.” It is **braise-to-adhesion**.

---

## 5. Technique-by-technique comparison

| Pair                         | Key difference                                                                              |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **煨 *(wēi)* vs 燉 *(dùn)***   | 煨 emphasizes low, indirect, protective heat; 燉 emphasizes liquid-based long cooking.        |
| **燉 *(dùn)* vs 燜 *(mèn)***   | 燉 is broth/submersion logic; 燜 is sealed-pot vapour logic.                                  |
| **燜 *(mèn)* vs 㸆 *(kào)***   | 燜 traps moisture; 㸆 reduces moisture until sauce concentrates.                              |
| **㸆 *(kào)* vs 燒 *(shāo)***  | 燒 is broad braise/roast/cook; 㸆 is narrower slow reduction-braise.                          |
| **燒 *(shāo)* vs 炙 *(zhì)***  | 燒 may be roasting or braising; 炙 is more specifically direct dry heat / grilling.           |
| **炙 *(zhì)* vs 煨 *(wēi)***   | 炙 exposes food to fire; 煨 protects food within ash, vessel, or low heat.                    |
| **烹 *(pēng)* vs all others** | 烹 is both an umbrella term for cooking and a particular quick oil-plus-seasoning technique. |

## 6. Philosophical map

| Culinary value          | Technique      | Logic                                                |
| ----------------------- | -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Preserve essence**    | **煨** *(wēi)*  | Keep flavour inside by shielding from harsh heat     |
| **Extract essence**     | **燉** *(dùn)*  | Draw flavour into broth through time                 |
| **Retain essence**      | **燜** *(mèn)*  | Prevent aroma and vapour from escaping               |
| **Concentrate essence** | **㸆** *(kào)*  | Reduce sauce until flavour adheres                   |
| **Transform essence**   | **燒** *(shāo)* | Use fire, sauce, colour, and time to alter character |
| **Expose essence**      | **炙** *(zhì)*  | Bring surface directly into contact with heat        |
| **Civilise rawness**    | **烹** *(pēng)* | Convert raw material into cuisine                    |

## 7. Historiographic layering

| Historical layer                     | Likely terms                  | Why                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Pre-vessel / early fire cookery**  | **炙** *(zhì)*, **煨** *(wēi)*  | Direct roasting and ash/ember cooking are technologically basic and textually old. |
| **Vessel-based boiling and stewing** | **烹** *(pēng)*, **燉** *(dùn)* | Requires pot, liquid, fuel management, and seasoning systems.                      |
| **Refined covered-pot cuisine**      | **燜** *(mèn)*                 | Depends on vessel control, lid use, and managed steam.                             |
| **Sauce-braising cuisine**           | **燒** *(shāo)*                | Associated with mature seasoning systems: soy, sugar, wine, aromatics.             |
| **Regional reduction cuisine**       | **㸆** *(kào)*                 | Strongly associated with Jiangnan / Ningbo usage; less nationally canonical.       |

This is not a strict chronological sequence. It is a **technological-cultural layering**: fire → vessel → enclosure → sauce → regional refinement.

## 9. Compressed taxonomy

**煨 *(wēi)*:** slow protected heat.
**燉 *(dùn)*:** slow liquid extraction.
**燜 *(mèn)*:** covered vapour retention.
**㸆 *(kào)*:** slow sauce reduction.
**燒 *(shāo)*:** broad fire/braise transformation.
**炙 *(zhì)*:** direct roasting exposure.
**烹 *(pēng)*:** cooking as transformation, sometimes quick oil-water finishing.

## Topic map

The expanded system can be organised into **six families**:

1. **油火快熟** — 炒、爆
2. **油火成形** — 炸、煎
3. **水火速熟** — 汆、煮
4. **汽火封熟** — 蒸、燉、燜、煨
5. **汁火成味** — 燒、㸆、熘、燴、扒、烹
6. **煙火賦香** — 炙、熏

This is not a rigid taxonomy. Chinese culinary verbs are historically layered: some are **ancient fire words** such as 炙 *(zhì)* and 烹 *(pēng)*; some are **vessel-and-liquid techniques** such as 煮 *(zhǔ)*, 蒸 *(zhēng)*, 燉 *(dùn)*; some are **restaurant-kitchen technical verbs** such as 熘 *(liū)*, 燴 *(huì)*, 扒 *(pá)*; and some are **regional / specialist terms** such as 㸆 *(kào)*. Modern lists of Chinese cooking techniques commonly include these terms together, but their semantic depth is uneven. One culinary list, for example, places 炸、爆、燒、炒、煮、汆、蒸、燉、煨、燜、燴、扒、熏 among a wider repertoire of more than thirty common methods. ([sohu.com][1])

# 3. Oil-fire family

## 炒 *(chǎo)*

**Core mechanism:** small-cut ingredients are rapidly turned in a hot wok with a modest amount of oil. A modern cooking-method source defines 炒 as cutting food into small pieces and rapidly stirring it in a hot wok with seasonings. ([sohu.com][1])

**Technical logic:** surface heat plus motion. 炒 depends on **cut size**, **wok heat**, **sequencing**, and **timing**. It is not merely “frying”; it is a kinetic technique.

| Subtype |        Pinyin | Meaning                                   |
| ------- | ------------: | ----------------------------------------- |
| **生炒**  | **shēngchǎo** | raw ingredients stir-fried directly       |
| **熟炒**  |   **shúchǎo** | pre-cooked ingredients stir-fried         |
| **滑炒**  |   **huáchǎo** | velveting / starch-coated tender stir-fry |
| **乾炒**  |   **gānchǎo** | drier stir-fry, less sauce                |

**Philosophy:** preserve freshness under violent heat. 炒 is paradoxical: high heat, short time, minimal damage.

---

## 爆 *(bào)*

**Core mechanism:** extremely rapid cooking over intense heat, often after the ingredient has already been cut, marinated, blanched, oiled, or otherwise pretreated. One source describes 爆 as using hot wok and hot oil, adding sauce or stock so steam rises sharply and small ingredients cook quickly with wok aroma; another describes 爆 as rapid high-heat cooking in oil or stock and often the final stage after earlier preparation. ([sohu.com][1])

| Subtype |       Pinyin | Meaning                           |
| ------- | -----------: | --------------------------------- |
| **油爆**  |   **yóubào** | flash-cooked in oil               |
| **湯爆**  |  **tāngbào** | flash-cooked in boiling stock     |
| **醬爆**  | **jiàngbào** | flash-finished with sauce / paste |
| **蔥爆**  |  **cōngbào** | flash-fried with scallion aroma   |

**Difference from 炒 *(chǎo)*:** 炒 is fast; 爆 is abrupt. 炒 manages heat through tossing; 爆 weaponises heat as a finishing event.

**Philosophy:** controlled explosion.

---

# 4. Oil-shaping family

## 炸 *(zhá)*

**Core mechanism:** food is submerged in hot oil until cooked, crisped, puffed, or sealed. Culinary lists define 炸 as cooking food in a large amount of hot oil until done and crisp. ([sohu.com][1])

| Subtype |      Pinyin | Meaning                              |
| ------- | ----------: | ------------------------------------ |
| **清炸**  | **qīngzhá** | plain deep-fry without heavy coating |
| **乾炸**  |  **gānzhá** | dry-coated deep-fry                  |
| **軟炸**  | **ruǎnzhá** | tender, softer deep-fry              |
| **酥炸**  |   **sūzhá** | flaky / crisp deep-fry               |
| **脆炸**  |  **cuìzhá** | crisp deep-fry                       |

**Technical logic:** oil surrounds the ingredient, transfers heat rapidly, evaporates surface moisture, and builds crust.

**Philosophy:** separation by crust. 炸 creates an outer shell that protects or contrasts the interior.

---

## 煎 *(jiān)*

**Core mechanism:** food lies flat against a heated pan or wok with a small amount of oil. It browns by contact. One culinary source defines 煎 as placing food flat against a heated pan with little oil and using low heat to make the surface golden and cooked; another contrasts it with 炒 and 爆 by noting that it uses less oil than deep-frying and lower heat than stir-frying. ([sohu.com][1])

| Subtype |        Pinyin | Meaning                                                          |
| ------- | ------------: | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **乾煎**  |   **gānjiān** | dry pan-fry, minimal sauce                                       |
| **香煎**  | **xiāngjiān** | aromatic pan-fry                                                 |
| **煎封**  |  **jiānfēng** | pan-fry then seal / finish with sauce, common in Cantonese usage |
| **貼**   |       **tiē** | related one-sided pan-fry, e.g. potstickers                      |

**Difference from 炸 *(zhá)*:** 炸 surrounds; 煎 contacts.
**Difference from 炒 *(chǎo)*:** 炒 moves; 煎 waits.

**Philosophy:** patience of surface.

---

# 5. Water-fire family

## 煮 *(zhǔ)*

**Core mechanism:** food cooked in water, stock, or soup. A cooking-method source defines 煮 as using boiling water or broth with seasoning to cook food. ([sohu.com][1])

| Subtype |      Pinyin | Meaning                                               |
| ------- | ----------: | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **白煮**  |  **báizhǔ** | plain boiling                                         |
| **水煮**  | **shuǐzhǔ** | water-boiling; in Sichuan also oil-red spicy poaching |
| **滾煮**  |  **gǔnzhǔ** | rolling boil                                          |
| **慢煮**  |  **mànzhǔ** | slow simmer                                           |

**Technical logic:** liquid transfers heat evenly. Depending on timing, 煮 can either clarify or vulgarise flavour: too short, underdone; too long, leached.

**Philosophy:** elemental cooking by water.

---

## 汆 *(cuān)*

**Core mechanism:** thin, small, or tender ingredients are rapidly cooked in boiling water or clear broth, often then served with broth. A cooking-method source defines quick-boiling in broth, **cuan**, as cooking thinly sliced ingredients quickly in boiling clear broth or water. ([chinesefoodmigration.dreamhosters.com][2])

**Variant note:** **汆** and **氽** are sometimes confused. In many practical cookery contexts, **汆 *(cuān)*** means quick boiling / blanch-cooking in water; **氽 *(tǔn)*** may mean floating, and in some culinary usage also appears in “oil-floating” or frying contexts. For this taxonomy, use **汆 *(cuān)*** for quick water / broth cooking.

| Example |            Pinyin | Meaning                     |
| ------- | ----------------: | --------------------------- |
| **汆丸子** |    **cuān wánzi** | quick-boiled meatballs      |
| **汆魚片** |   **cuān yúpiàn** | quick-boiled fish slices    |
| **清湯汆** | **qīngtāng cuān** | quick-cooked in clear broth |

**Difference from 煮 *(zhǔ)*:** 煮 is broad and can be prolonged; 汆 is brief and precision-timed.

**Philosophy:** minimal intervention.

---

# 6. Steam / enclosure family

## 蒸 *(zhēng)*

**Core mechanism:** steam cooks food without immersion. One source defines 蒸 as using the heat of steam to cook food. ([sohu.com][1])

| Subtype |           Pinyin | Meaning                             |
| ------- | ---------------: | ----------------------------------- |
| **清蒸**  |    **qīngzhēng** | plain steaming, often fish          |
| **粉蒸**  |     **fěnzhēng** | steamed with rice powder            |
| **扣蒸**  |     **kòuzhēng** | arranged in bowl, steamed, inverted |
| **隔水蒸** | **géshuǐ zhēng** | steamed indirectly over water       |

**Technical logic:** steam gives even, moist heat while preserving shape and surface. It favours fish, eggs, custards, rice products, and delicate meats.

**Philosophy:** cooking without invasion. 蒸 touches without submerging.

---

## 燉 *(dùn)*

**Core mechanism:** slow cooking in liquid or sealed indirect heat. In some taxonomies, 燉 is described as food plus water/stock in a covered container, cooked by steam heat to produce soup; in northern usage it can overlap with 煨 as slow cooking in much liquid. ([sohu.com][1])

| Subtype |         Pinyin | Meaning                |
| ------- | -------------: | ---------------------- |
| **清燉**  |    **qīngdùn** | clear slow-stew        |
| **紅燉**  |    **hóngdùn** | soy-coloured slow-stew |
| **隔水燉** | **géshuǐ dùn** | double-boiling         |
| **燉補**  |      **dùnbǔ** | tonic stewing          |

**Difference from 蒸 *(zhēng)*:** 蒸 uses steam to cook the food; 燉 usually uses liquid inside the vessel as the flavour matrix.
**Difference from 煮 *(zhǔ)*:** 煮 can be ordinary boiling; 燉 implies duration, gentleness, and extraction.

**Philosophy:** time extracts essence.

---

## 燜 *(mèn)*

**Core mechanism:** covered pot, modest liquid, low-to-medium heat. The lid traps vapour and forces flavours to circulate. One list defines 燜 as adding liquid, covering, and using gentle heat to soften and cook tougher ingredients. ([sohu.com][1])

| Subtype |       Pinyin | Meaning                                  |
| ------- | -----------: | ---------------------------------------- |
| **油燜**  |   **yóumèn** | oil-braise, e.g. bamboo shoots           |
| **紅燜**  |  **hóngmèn** | soy-coloured covered braise              |
| **黃燜**  | **huángmèn** | yellow-braise, often with ginger / sauce |
| **醬燜**  | **jiàngmèn** | sauce-covered braise                     |

**Difference from 燉 *(dùn)*:** 燉 is liquid-extractive; 燜 is enclosure-retentive.
**Difference from 燒 *(shāo)*:** 燒 often emphasises colour and sauce transformation; 燜 emphasises lidded softening.

**Philosophy:** retention under cover.

---

## 煨 *(wēi)*

**Core mechanism:** gentle indirect heat: historically ash / embers; later low-fire vessel cooking. A modern culinary list preserves both the older meaning of burying food in charcoal ash and the later meaning of cooking with ginger, scallion, and broth to remove off-flavours and infuse flavour. ([sohu.com][1])

| Subtype |         Pinyin | Meaning              |
| ------- | -------------: | -------------------- |
| **灰煨**  |     **huīwēi** | ash-roasting         |
| **瓦罐煨** | **wǎguàn wēi** | crock slow-simmering |
| **煨湯**  |   **wēi tāng** | slow-simmered soup   |
| **煨肉**  |    **wēi ròu** | slow-simmered meat   |

**Difference from 燜 *(mèn)*:** 燜 depends on covered-vessel vapour; 煨 is broader and older, including ash / ember cooking.
**Difference from 燉 *(dùn)*:** 燉 is liquid-forward; 煨 is heat-environment-forward.

**Philosophy:** protection from harsh fire.

---

# 7. Sauce-construction family

## 燒 *(shāo)*

**Core mechanism:** broad fire-cooking term; in restaurant cuisine, often fry/sear first, then add liquid or sauce and reduce. Ken Lo’s taxonomy, quoted in one cooking-method summary, distinguishes 燒 from 燜 by saying both involve frying followed by simmering, but 燒 achieves more reduction and leaves less gravy. ([✮ The Food Dictator ✮][3])

| Subtype |       Pinyin | Meaning                         |
| ------- | -----------: | ------------------------------- |
| **紅燒**  | **hóngshāo** | soy-sugar red braise            |
| **白燒**  |  **báishāo** | pale / non-soy braise           |
| **乾燒**  |  **gānshāo** | dry-braise, little final liquid |
| **蔥燒**  | **cōngshāo** | scallion-braise                 |

**Difference from 㸆 *(kào)*:** 燒 is broader; 㸆 is a more forcefully reductive regional sauce technique.

**Philosophy:** fire plus sauce produces depth.

---

## 㸆 *(kào)*

**Core mechanism:** slow infusion and reduction. In one culinary-method list, 㸆 is described as using concentrated, flavourful liquid and gentle heat over a long period to transfer flavour into a comparatively bland main ingredient. ([sohu.com][1])

| Example |        Pinyin | Meaning                                           |
| ------- | ------------: | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **㸆菜**  |   **kào cài** | reduction-braised greens, especially Ningbo-style |
| **㸆肉**  |   **kào ròu** | reduction-braised pork                            |
| **㸆大蝦** | **kào dàxiā** | reduction-braised prawns                          |

**Difference from 燒 *(shāo)*:** 燒 may leave sauce; 㸆 makes sauce cling, penetrate, or nearly disappear.
**Difference from 燴 *(huì)*:** 燴 thickens a collective broth; 㸆 concentrates flavour into a main ingredient.

**Philosophy:** flavour by reduction and adhesion.

---

## 熘 / 溜 *(liū)*

**Core mechanism:** ingredient is usually first cooked by frying, steaming, boiling, or oil-slipping; then a glossy sauce is poured over or tossed around it. A cooking-method source describes **liu** as two processes: the ingredient is deep-fried and then covered with a cornstarch-based sauce, producing a slippery texture. ([chinesefoodmigration.dreamhosters.com][2])

| Subtype |      Pinyin | Meaning                      |
| ------- | ----------: | ---------------------------- |
| **炸熘**  |  **zháliū** | deep-fry then sauce          |
| **滑熘**  |  **huáliū** | velvet / oil-slip then sauce |
| **焦熘**  | **jiāoliū** | crisp-fry then sauce         |
| **醋熘**  |   **cùliū** | vinegar-gloss sauce finish   |

**Difference from 燴 *(huì)*:** 熘 usually highlights a main ingredient with sauce; 燴 assembles multiple ingredients in a thickened broth.
**Difference from 㸆 *(kào)*:** 熘 adds / coats sauce at the end; 㸆 reduces sauce through the cooking process.

**Philosophy:** gloss as finish.

---

## 燴 *(huì)*

**Core mechanism:** multiple pre-cooked or semi-cooked ingredients are brought together in broth or sauce and often thickened. One source defines 燴 as cooking several meat and vegetable ingredients together with a suitable amount of soup; another defines **hui** as precooked ingredients placed together and slow-simmered, with thickening at the end. ([sohu.com][1])

| Example |                        Pinyin | Meaning                               |
| ------- | ----------------------------: | ------------------------------------- |
| **燴飯**  |                    **huìfàn** | rice with thickened stew topping      |
| **燴麵**  |                   **huìmiàn** | noodles in thickened broth            |
| **雜燴**  |                     **záhuì** | mixed stew / assorted braise          |
| **燴三鮮** | **huì sāoxiān / huì sānxiān** | assembled “three delicacies” in sauce |

**Difference from 煮 *(zhǔ)*:** 煮 may simply cook one ingredient in water; 燴 is compositional and often thickened.
**Difference from 扒 *(pá)*:** 燴 is mixed; 扒 is more arranged and presentation-oriented.

**Philosophy:** assembly into coherence.

---

## 扒 *(pá)*

**Core mechanism:** a main ingredient is arranged, gently braised or steamed, then finished with a thickened sauce. In English-language Chinese technique lists, **pa** is glossed as stewing and adding thickening, similar to other braises but finished with starch thickening rather than reduction. ([chinesefoodmigration.dreamhosters.com][2])

| Example |         Pinyin | Meaning                                  |
| ------- | -------------: | ---------------------------------------- |
| **扒白菜** |  **pá báicài** | braised cabbage with sauce               |
| **扒海參** | **pá hǎishēn** | braised sea cucumber with sauce          |
| **扒雞**  |      **pá jī** | braised chicken, often arranged / sauced |
| **扒肘子** |  **pá zhǒuzi** | braised pork knuckle with sauce          |

**Difference from 燴 *(huì)*:** 燴 mixes; 扒 composes.
**Difference from 燒 *(shāo)*:** 燒 transforms by braising and reduction; 扒 often preserves form and finishes with sauce.

**Philosophy:** softness plus presentation.

---

## 烹 *(pēng)*

**Core mechanism:** broad “cook” term; in narrower technique lists, often means brown/fry first, then add liquid or seasonings and reduce. Ken Lo’s list describes 烹 as frying food on both sides until brown, then adding water/stock and supplementary ingredients, continuing on low heat until liquid reduces to dryness. ([✮ The Food Dictator ✮][3])

| Use    |       Pinyin | Meaning                        |
| ------ | -----------: | ------------------------------ |
| **烹飪** |  **pēngrèn** | cookery in general             |
| **烹調** | **pēngtiáo** | cooking / culinary preparation |
| **烹魚** |  **pēng yú** | cook fish                      |
| **煎烹** | **jiānpēng** | pan-fry then season-cook       |

**Difference from all others:** 烹 is both a **general civilisational verb** and a **specific technical finish**. That makes it semantically unstable but historically important.

**Philosophy:** rawness transformed into cuisine.

---

# 8. Fire, smoke, and aroma family

## 炙 *(zhì)*

**Core mechanism:** direct fire, radiant heat, grilling, roasting. This is one of the most archaic-feeling terms in the set.

| Example  |             Pinyin | Meaning                                                                                    |
| -------- | -----------------: | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **炙肉**   |        **zhì ròu** | grilled / roasted meat                                                                     |
| **膾炙人口** | **kuàizhì rénkǒu** | literally raw-sliced fish and roasted meat in people’s mouths; figuratively widely praised |
| **炙烤**   |         **zhìkǎo** | grill / roast                                                                              |

**Difference from 燒 *(shāo)*:** 炙 is more specifically direct exposure to fire; 燒 is broader and can become braising.
**Difference from 煨 *(wēi)*:** 炙 exposes; 煨 shelters.

**Philosophy:** surface meets fire.

---

## 熏 *(xūn)*

**Core mechanism:** smoke flavours, dries, colours, and may preserve food. Modern lists include 熏 among common Chinese cooking techniques, usually alongside roasting, braising, frying, steaming, and curing methods. ([sohu.com][1])

| Subtype |           Pinyin | Meaning              |
| ------- | ---------------: | -------------------- |
| **煙熏**  |       **yānxūn** | smoke-cured / smoked |
| **茶熏**  |       **cháxūn** | tea-smoked           |
| **樟茶熏** | **zhāngchá xūn** | camphor-tea smoked   |
| **糖熏**  |      **tángxūn** | sugar-smoked         |

**Difference from 炙 *(zhì)*:** 炙 uses heat directly; 熏 uses smoke as an aromatic medium.
**Difference from 烤 *(kǎo)*:** 烤 cooks by dry heat; 熏 may cook, preserve, colour, or perfume, depending on duration and prior preparation.

**Philosophy:** flavour without direct touch.

# 11. Historiographic layering

## Established

The older technological base is probably **direct fire**, **boiling**, **steaming**, and **ash / ember cooking**: 炙 *(zhì)*, 煮 *(zhǔ)*, 蒸 *(zhēng)*, 煨 *(wēi)*. These depend on basic fire, vessels, steam, or embers. Restaurant-kitchen systems later multiplied fine distinctions around wok heat, oil temperature, sauce reduction, starch thickening, and presentation.

## Interpretation

A plausible cultural sequence is:

| Layer                        | Techniques                                            | Cultural implication                                           |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Fire age**                 | 炙 *(zhì)*, 煨 *(wēi)*                                  | meat, embers, ash, direct heat                                 |
| **Pot age**                  | 煮 *(zhǔ)*, 蒸 *(zhēng)*, 燉 *(dùn)*                     | grain, broth, medicine-food continuum                          |
| **Wok-oil age**              | 炒 *(chǎo)*, 爆 *(bào)*, 炸 *(zhá)*, 煎 *(jiān)*          | high heat, urban fuel economy, cut-size precision              |
| **Sauce-restaurant age**     | 燒 *(shāo)*, 熘 *(liū)*, 燴 *(huì)*, 扒 *(pá)*, 㸆 *(kào)* | starch, stock, soy, sugar, presentation, regional codification |
| **Preservation-aroma layer** | 熏 *(xūn)*                                             | storage, ritual, regional aromatics                            |

## Uncertainty

This should not be read as strict chronology. Many techniques coexisted early; what changes is not only invention but **lexical codification**. A household may have used a method long before elite recipe books stabilised the name.

# Red team review

**Weak point 1 — regional instability.**
Terms such as 燴 *(huì)*, 扒 *(pá)*, 熘 *(liū)*, 㸆 *(kào)*, 炆 *(wén)*, 焗 *(jú)*, and 煲 *(bāo)* shift across northern, Cantonese, Jiangnan, and restaurant-school usage. A single “Chinese” taxonomy smooths over real regional divergence.

**Weak point 2 — homograph / variant confusion.**
熘 *(liū)* and 溜 *(liū)* are often interchangeable in culinary writing. 汆 *(cuān)* and 氽 *(tǔn / cuān in some culinary contexts)* are often confused. 㸆 *(kào)* is graphically uncommon and can be replaced by 火靠 or explained through 燒.

**Weak point 3 — technique versus dish-name drift.**
Some words survive more strongly in dish names than as actively understood household techniques. Example: 扒海參 *(pá hǎishēn)* may be recognised as a dish format even when the precise technique is not verbally analysed by diners.

---

# Bottom line

The larger taxonomy is best understood as a movement from **heat as force** to **heat as managed environment**:

**炙、炸、煎** expose or shape the surface.
**炒、爆** use speed and motion to preserve freshness.
**汆、煮、蒸、燉** use water or steam to cook gently or extract.
**燜、煨** enclose and protect.
**燒、㸆、熘、燴、扒** build sauce architecture.
**熏** adds smoke as an aromatic medium.
**烹** sits above the system as both a specific technique and the general act of culinary transformation.

# 2. Best-equivalence clusters

## A. Near-direct equivalence

| Chinese |    Pinyin | French                | English            | Cross-cultural equivalent                                                                         |

| **燜**   |  **mèn** | **étuver / braiser à couvert**   | covered braise   | French equivalent depends on liquid amount and lid logic.      |
| **㸆**   |  **kào** | **braiser à réduction**          | reduction-braise | French describes the process but lacks one single common verb. |

**Interpretation:** These require **compound translation**: not just “sauté,” but “high-heat wok sauté”; not just “braise,” but “covered braise” or “reduction-braise.”

| 煎 / 炙 / 燒      | **焼き物** *(yakimono)*, **炙り** *(aburi)* | Good for dry heat; weak for sauce-braised 燒.                       |

**Key point:** Japanese maps well by **dish family**, less well by Chinese-style wok sequencing.

## Korean

Korean has excellent analogues for stir-fry, braise-reduction, grill, steam/braise, and deep-fry: **볶음** *(bokkeum)* for stir-fried dishes, **조림** *(jorim)* for sauce-reduced simmering, **찜** *(jjim)* for steamed/braised dishes, **구이** *(gui)* for grilled items, and **튀김** *(twigim)* for fried items. **Key point:** Korean **조림** is one of the best analogues for **㸆**, **乾燒**, and some **紅燒**.

# 6. Interpretive findings

## Established knowledge

The highest-confidence cross-cultural mappings follow **physical medium**: steam, smoke, deep oil, direct fire. These are not culture-specific abstractions; they are shared heat-transfer situations. French and English terms map well here, and East/Southeast Asian analogues are clear.

## Interpretation

The most interesting comparisons arise not at the level of “boil/fry/steam,” but at the level of **sauce temporality**:

* **㸆 *(kào)*** ≈ Korean **조림 *(jorim)*** ≈ Vietnamese **kho** ≈ late-stage **rendang** logic.
* **燜 *(mèn)*** ≈ French **étuver / à l’étouffée** ≈ Indian **dum** ≈ Korean **찜 *(jjim)***.
* **熘 *(liū)*** ≈ French **napper + lier**, but the Chinese term encodes the whole sequence of pre-cooking plus glossy sauce.
* **炒 *(chǎo)*** ≈ Thai **phat**, Vietnamese **xào**, Korean **볶음**, Japanese **炒め物**, but French **sauter** only partially captures wok logic.

## Uncertainty

Some mappings are cuisine-internal compromises. **燒 *(shāo)*** is unstable because Chinese itself uses it broadly. **烹 *(pēng)*** is nearly impossible to map unless one specifies whether it means “to cook” generally or a narrow technical method. **扒 *(pá)*** is not just heat; it is technique plus arrangement plus sauce presentation.

# Bottom line

The best three-way mappings are not French-centered. French is excellent for **heat-transfer and sauce verbs**; Japanese and Korean are better for **East Asian dish-family analogues**; Vietnamese and Malay-Indonesian are often better for **reduction-braise logic**.

# 2. 炆 man1 / wén

## Modern culinary meaning

**炆** means slow cooking over low fire, usually with sauce, aromatics, and covered heat. It is a Cantonese braising word. Michelin’s Cantonese terminology article describes 炆 as requiring low heat, a lid, often two to three hours of cooking, with ingredients moving from firm to soft and the sauce reducing from more to less. It also notes common Cantonese flavour bases such as **柱侯醬**, **南乳**, **麵豉醬**, ginger, garlic, dried shallot, and spices. ([Michelin Guide][2])

## Compared with other terms

| Compared term | Difference                                                                                                               |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **炆 vs 燜**    | Very close. 燜 stresses covered-vessel trapping of vapour; 炆 in Cantonese stresses low-fire sauce braising and softening. |
| **炆 vs 燉**    | 燉 is often clearer, soupier, or double-boiled; 炆 is darker, saucier, and more strongly seasoned.                         |
| **炆 vs 燒**    | 燒 is broader and may include red-braising or roasting; 炆 is more specifically slow, covered, sauce-braise.               |
| **炆 vs 㸆**    | 㸆 drives sauce toward adhesion/reduction; 炆 may reduce but usually remains braise-like rather than near-dry.             |
| **炆 vs 煲**    | 炆 is method; 煲 is often vessel plus long simmering. A dish can be both: **羊腩煲** may involve 炆-like logic inside a 煲.     |

## Evolutionary philology

**Established:** Lexicographically, **炆** is a fire-radical graph. Zdic gives two core meanings: **“沒有火焰的微火”** and, as a dialect verb, **“用微火燉食物或熬菜.”** It also records the Cantonese reading **man1** and cites **《集韻》: 炆，熅也**. ([Zdic][3])

Taiwan’s Ministry of Education variant dictionary states that **炆** is **not recorded in Shuowen** and gives the meaning **熅也**, citing **《集韻・平聲・文韻》**. ([Taiwan Variant Dictionary][4])

**Interpretation:** The graph’s history is probably not “ancient named Cantonese braise → modern cooking word.” It is more likely:

1. **Fire semantic field**: low, flameless, banked heat.
2. **Lexicographic survival**: 熅 / warm smouldering heat.
3. **Cantonese culinary specialization**: low-fire sauce braising.
4. **Restaurant codification**: 炆 becomes a visible menu and kitchen term, especially for tough meats, offal, mushrooms, sea cucumber, goose web, lamb brisket.

**Uncertainty:** The exact point when 炆 became a stable Cantonese culinary technical term is harder to date than its older lexicographic sense.

# 3. 焗 guk6 / jú

## Modern culinary meaning

**焗** means cooking in a closed environment: sealed pot, oven, salt, hot sand, or trapped heat. Zdic defines it as a dialect cooking method using vapour in a closed vessel and gives examples such as **全焗雞**. Its 國語辭典 section specifically describes heating salt or clean sand in a pot, wrapping the food, burying it in hot salt/sand, covering the lid, and cooking slowly, as in **鹽焗雞**. ([Zdic][5])

## Compared with other terms

| Compared term | Difference                                                                                                            |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **焗 vs 烤**    | 烤 is dry radiant/oven heat; 焗 implies enclosed heat, often with trapped vapour or salt/sand medium.                   |
| **焗 vs 燜**    | 燜 uses covered moist braise; 焗 can be drier, oven-like, salt-sealed, or steam-sealed.                                 |
| **焗 vs 煨**    | 煨 is slow indirect heat, often ash/embers; 焗 is sealed-container heat, often Cantonese and modern-restaurant visible. |
| **焗 vs 蒸**    | 蒸 uses open steam from water; 焗 uses enclosed heat, sometimes with no direct water-steam system.                      |
| **焗 vs 炆**    | 炆 is low-fire sauce braise; 焗 is enclosed heat cooking, often less sauce-forward.                                     |

## Evolutionary philology

**Established:** Zdic lists **焗** as a dialect word meaning to cook in a tightly covered pot and notes Cantonese-related English glosses “to roast, bake; to suffocate, stuffy.” ([Zdic][5]) A separate dictionary page gives **guk6** as the Cantonese reading and defines it as cooking in a closed vessel, also giving the non-culinary sense of stuffiness from poor air circulation. ([shidianguji.com][6])

**Graph logic:** **焗** is 火 + 局. The **火** radical marks heat; **局** evokes constraint/confinement at the semantic level, although formally it is the phonetic/structural component. The modern culinary sense fits the felt image: heat trapped in a limited space.

**Interpretation:** Semantic evolution likely runs:

1. **Confinement / stuffiness sense**: hot, airless, closed atmosphere.
2. **Enclosed cooking**: food cooked by trapped heat/vapour.
3. **Cantonese technique codification**: **鹽焗雞**, **焗魚腸**, **芝士焗飯**, **焗豬扒飯**.
4. **Modern extension**: oven-baking in Hong Kong Cantonese, especially Western-influenced café cuisine.

**Historiographic point:** 焗 is a good example of Cantonese absorbing modern oven/bake semantics without simply adopting 烤. It keeps the older Cantonese feeling of **sealed, enclosed, slightly pressurised heat**.

# 4. 灼 zoek3 / zhuó

## Modern culinary meaning

In Cantonese cooking, especially **白灼**, **灼** means rapidly cooking raw food in boiling water or stock, often to preserve freshness, tenderness, crunch, and natural flavour. A description of 白灼 defines 灼 as using boiling water or soup to scald raw food until cooked; it also stresses that 白灼 is not merely “boiling in plain water,” because timing, seasoning of the water, freshness, and final sauce matter. ([plem.com][7])

Another cooking note divides 白灼 into types, including **原質灼法**, which preserves original flavour, and a processed form for texture-sensitive ingredients such as goose intestine and pork kidney; it again defines 灼 as cooking food in boiling water or broth. ([meishichina.com][8])

## Compared with other terms

| Compared term | Difference                                                                                                               |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **灼 vs 汆**    | Very close. 汆 is quick boiling/blanch-cooking; 灼 in Cantonese is more dish-format and freshness-oriented, especially 白灼. |
| **灼 vs 焯**    | 焯 often means blanching as pre-treatment; 灼 often means final cooking for service.                                       |
| **灼 vs 煮**    | 煮 is broad and may be long; 灼 is brief, precise, and “just cooked.”                                                      |
| **灼 vs 涮**    | 涮 is swishing in hotpot; 灼 is kitchen-executed scalding/blanching, often plated with sauce.                              |
| **灼 vs 浸**    | 浸 may use residual heat or gentle soaking; 灼 usually implies hotter, more active scalding.                               |

## Evolutionary philology

**Established:** In classical lexicography, **灼** is old. Zdic gives it as a phono-semantic character, **火** radical plus **勺** phonetic, with the original meaning “burn/scorch/cauterize,” citing **《說文》: 灼，炙也**. It also records meanings such as scalding, illuminating, and brightness. ([Zdic][9]) CUHK’s multifunctional character database similarly notes meanings including burn/scald, illumination, and clarity/brightness. ([humanum.arts.cuhk.edu.hk][10])

**Interpretation:** The culinary development is semantic softening:

1. **灼 = burn/scorch/cauterize**: direct thermal injury.
2. **灼 = scald**: heat applied through hot water or contact.
3. **白灼 = controlled scalding**: not burning, but precise heat just sufficient to cook.
4. **Cantonese aesthetic specialization**: the technique becomes a test of freshness and timing.

**Why this matters:** 灼 is etymologically closer to “scorch/scald” than to “boil.” Cantonese cuisine retools a harsh heat word into a refined technique of minimal intervention.

# 5. 煲 bou1 / bāo

## Modern culinary meaning

**煲** is both a **vessel** and a **method**. It means a steep-walled pot, especially claypot/sandpot, and by extension cooking slowly in such a vessel: **煲湯**, **煲粥**, **煲仔飯**. Zdic gives both meanings: a steep-walled pot such as **沙煲、電飯煲**, and cooking/boiling in such a pot, such as **煲飯、煲粥**. ([Zdic][11])

Taiwan’s Ministry of Education dictionary defines **煲** as using slow fire to cook food, with examples **煲粥、煲湯、雞煲飯**. ([Ministry of Education Revised Dictionary][12]) The MOE variant dictionary adds that **煲** is not recorded in Shuowen and cites **《彙音寶鑑》: 以緩火煮物曰煲也**, while also giving the vessel meaning “壁較陡直之鍋.” ([Taiwan Variant Dictionary][13])

## Compared with other terms

| Compared term | Difference                                                                                                                          |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **煲 vs 燉**    | 燉 often implies double-boiling or clear slow extraction; 煲 is more open simmering in pot, often richer and more everyday Cantonese. |
| **煲 vs 熬**    | 熬 stresses prolonged boiling down/extraction; 煲 stresses pot and Cantonese soup/congee/rice practice.                               |
| **煲 vs 煮**    | 煮 is generic boiling/cooking; 煲 is vessel-specific and long-duration.                                                               |
| **煲 vs 燜**    | 燜 is covered braise with limited liquid; 煲 often has more liquid, soup, rice, or claypot format.                                    |
| **煲 vs 炆**    | 炆 produces saucy softness; 煲 may produce soup, congee, rice crust, or claypot integration.                                          |

## Evolutionary philology

**Established:** The modern graph **煲** is not in Shuowen according to MOE variant data. It is a later/vernacular graph built around the fire semantic field and associated with pot-cooking and slow simmering. ([Taiwan Variant Dictionary][13]) Zdic’s traditional page points to older **缹** in Kangxi, noting that Kangxi did not list **煲** as a separate headword and that **缹** has old glosses including **火熟也** and variants such as **炰**. ([Zdic][14])

**Interpretation:** Its likely development:

1. **Older vessel/fire-cooking field**: 缹 / 炰 / pot-cooked by fire.
2. **Southern/Cantonese vernacular specialization**: 煲 as high-sided pot and slow pot-cooking.
3. **Menu and household stabilization**: **煲湯**, **煲粥**, **煲仔飯**, **砂鍋煲**.
4. **Modern appliance extension**: **電飯煲** shows the vessel term moving into modern technology.

**Cultural implication:** 煲 is not merely “boil.” It encodes a Cantonese household technology: pot, time, soup-body, rice texture, and heat retention.

# 8. Historiographic synthesis

## Established knowledge

* **炆** is lexicographically tied to **flameless low fire** and dialectal slow cooking. ([Zdic][3])
* **焗** is a dialectal enclosed-heat cooking term, including salt/sand and lidded-vapour cooking. ([Zdic][5])
* **灼** is an older classical graph meaning burn/scorch/scald, later refined in Cantonese culinary practice as rapid scald-cooking. ([Zdic][9])
* **煲** is a later/vernacular graph not recorded in Shuowen, with meanings of slow cooking and steep-walled pot. ([Taiwan Variant Dictionary][13])

## Interpretation

These four words show four different routes into Cantonese culinary vocabulary:

| Route                                                           | Word | Pattern                                         |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **Old thermal word → refined cooking method**                   | 灼    | burn/scald → precise blanching                  |
| **Old/lexical low-fire word → Cantonese braise**                | 炆    | banked heat → slow sauce braise                 |
| **Dialectal enclosed-heat word → Cantonese + modern oven term** | 焗    | stuffy/enclosed heat → salt-bake / bake         |
| **Vessel word → method word → appliance word**                  | 煲    | pot → slow simmer → rice cooker/claypot cuisine |

## Uncertainty

The exact chronology of **Cantonese culinary codification** is uneven. 灼 has deep classical roots as a graph; 炆 has medieval lexicographic attestation through 集韻; 煲 is later/vernacular in its current graph; 焗 is strongly dialectal and modern culinary-visible. The cooking practices may be older than their written menu forms.

# Bottom line

**炆、焗、灼、煲** are not just Cantonese synonyms for standard Mandarin cooking verbs.

* **炆** is Cantonese **low-fire sauce braising**.
* **焗** is **enclosed heat**, ranging from salt-bake to oven-bake.
* **灼** is **precise quick scalding**, especially for freshness.
* **煲** is **pot-based slow cooking**, especially soup, congee, rice, and claypot formats.

Their philological interest lies in how Cantonese cuisine converts older heat words, dialectal graphs, and vessel terms into a practical culinary taxonomy.

# Major topics covered

## 1. 煨 and 㸆 as the starting contrast

The discussion began by comparing **煨** *(wēi)* and **㸆** *(kào)*.

**煨** was treated as a broader and older term. It can refer to ash-roasting, ember cooking, low-fire simmering, or slow protected heat. Its core logic is **indirect, gentle, enclosed transformation**.

**㸆** was treated as narrower and more regional, especially associated with Jiangnan / Ningbo-style cooking. Its logic is **slow reduction**, where sauce thickens, clings, and concentrates around the ingredient.

The compressed distinction:

| Term          | Core logic                    | Culinary philosophy                |
| ------------- | ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **煨** *(wēi)* | low, protected, indirect heat | preserve essence by enclosure      |
| **㸆** *(kào)* | slow sauce reduction          | intensify essence by concentration |
