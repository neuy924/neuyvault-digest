## Current Trending Themes in Queer Theory, 2025–2026

The field in early 2026 is shaped by two converging pressures: an acute political backlash (legislative, juridical, biopolitical) that has galvanised theoretical production, and a longer-running internal expansion as queer theory absorbs crip studies, decolonial thought, and AI critique into its conceptual architecture.

### 1\. Queer Necropolitics — Intensified and Globalised

The framework pioneered by Haritaworn, Kuntsman, and Posocco (2014) has become one of the most active theoretical sites in contemporary scholarship. Queer necropolitics — the hegemonic power to determine who is allowed to suffer, survive, or thrive — has been applied to China's sustained re-enforcement of traditional gender norms, examining how queer communities rendered legally invisible are placed under hegemonic governance techniques of suppression. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13548565241288466) The framework is simultaneously being applied to Global North contexts: death is being refashioned as a source of queer and trans of colour agency, providing a springboard from which artists conceive radical aesthetic critique, indicating new approaches to how queer necropolitics is theorised as more than pure victimhood. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/14647001221085944) The concept now anchors a whole sub-literature on **trans necropolitics**, **carceral queerness**, and the racialised differential distribution of vitality and precarity.

### 2\. Trans-Crip Theory — The Most Productive New Synthesis

## The Butler–Sedgwick–Foucault Axis: A Foundational Primer

Think of these three thinkers as building a house together — in a specific order. Foucault pours the foundations. Sedgwick frames the walls and windows. Butler furnishes and rebuilds the interior. You cannot understand the later rooms without knowing what the ground is made of.

---

## FLOOR ONE: Michel Foucault

### The History of Sexuality, Volume 1 (1976 / English trans. 1978)

Foucault is the **origin point**. But his contribution is counterintuitive — he doesn't say "sexuality was repressed and now we're free." He says something stranger and more disturbing.

### The Repressive Hypothesis — and why it's wrong

Most people assume: *Victorian society repressed sex → modernity liberated it.*

Foucault says: **No. Victorian society produced an explosion of discourse about sex.** It classified, named, and catalogued sexuality obsessively — through medicine, psychiatry, law, confession, demography.

> *"What is peculiar to modern societies... is not that they consigned sex to a shadow existence, but that they dedicated themselves to speaking of it ad infinitum, while exploiting it as the secret."* — *History of Sexuality*, Vol. 1

The point: **power doesn't just repress. Power produces.** It produces categories, identities, knowledges, subjects.

### The Homosexual as Invention

The most famous and important claim:

> *"The sodomite had been a temporary aberration; the homosexual was now a species."* — *History of Sexuality*, Vol. 1

Before the 19th century, "sodomy" was a *sin* — an act anyone might commit. From the 1870s onward, medicine invented *"the homosexual"* — a **type of person**, a psychological species with a nature, an aetiology, a symptomatology.

**What this means:** Gay identity as we know it is not a liberation of something that was always there. It is a *product* of the same power-knowledge apparatus that also pathologised it.

This is deeply uncomfortable — and deeply generative for queer theory.

### Biopower and the Regulation of Life

Foucault introduces *biopower* — the way modern states govern not by killing but by **administering life**: managing populations, reproduction, health, normality.

> *"For the first time in history, no doubt, biological existence was reflected in political existence."*

Sexuality becomes one of the key sites where biopower operates. The "normal" body, the reproductive couple, the healthy citizen — these are not neutral descriptions. They are effects of power.

**Key concepts to memorise:**

- **Discourse** — not just language, but systems of knowledge that define what counts as true, normal, pathological
- **Power/knowledge** — power and knowledge are not separate; knowing something is already a power-relation
- **Biopolitics** — the governance of populations through the administration of life and bodies
- **The Repressive Hypothesis** — the wrong assumption that power only says "no"

## FLOOR TWO: Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick

### Epistemology of the Closet (1990)

If Foucault shows that sexuality is *constructed by discourse*, Sedgwick asks: **what is the structure of that construction in modern Western culture?** Her answer is: **the closet.**

### The Axioms

The book opens with seven "Axioms" — theoretical propositions stated as starting points. They are worth reading slowly.

> *"Axiom 1: People are different from each other."*

This seems obvious — but Sedgwick is making a methodological point. Queer theory must resist the urge to flatten differences into a single explanatory theory.

> *"Axiom 2: The study of sexuality is not coextensive with the study of gender."*

This is a **separation** many people miss. Feminism deals primarily with gender (man/woman). Queer theory deals primarily with sexuality (desire, its objects, its regulation). These are related but not the same axis of power.

The most important axiom:

> *"Axiom 1 \[on ignorance\]: Many of the major nodes of thought and knowledge in twentieth-century Western culture as a whole are structured — indeed, fractured — by a chronic, now endemic crisis of homo/heterosexual definition."*

**The closet is not just a private hiding place.** It is a structure that organises *all* of modern culture — the relationship between the known and the unknown, the said and the unsaid, the public and the private.

### The Closet as Epistemological Structure

> *"The closet is the defining structure for gay oppression in this century."*

But more than this:

> *"Coming out... is never a once-and-for-all event but a series of new closets."*

And crucially:

> *"The epistemology of the closet... is not a discrete thing that can be known and then left behind."*

The closet is a **knowledge structure** — it governs who knows what about whom, under what conditions, with what consequences. The person in the closet is always in an impossible double bind: not coming out protects them but also means living inauthentically; coming out risks exposure but also reveals how much of "normalcy" was built on their silence.

### Minoritising vs. Universalising

Sedgwick identifies two competing logics in how homosexuality is defined:

- **Minoritising view**: Homosexuality concerns a small, distinct minority of people — gay people — who have a specific, stable identity
- **Universalising view**: Homosexuality is a *potential* in everyone; its regulation shapes all sexuality, not just gay sexuality

Queer theory takes the universalising view as its theoretical lever: the closet, the heterosexual/homosexual binary — these structure *everyone's* sexuality and culture, not just gay people's lives.

### Between Men (1985) — Homosocial Desire

In an earlier book, Sedgwick develops the concept of **homosocial desire** using René Girard's triangular desire:

In literature and culture, male bonds (rivalry, mentorship, friendship) are structured by intense but disavowed desire between men — mediated by a woman as the object of exchange.

> *"'Homosocial' is a word occasionally used in history and the social sciences, where it describes social bonds between persons of the same sex... it is applied to such activities as 'male bonding'... In fact, it is applied to the space of difference and displacement between the compulsorily homophobic and the merely homosocial."*

The point: **homophobia is not the opposite of male bonding — it is its regulating mechanism.** Men bond intensely but must patrol that bond against its tipping into desire.

**Key concepts:**

- **The Closet** — not just personal but structural, epistemological
- **Homo/heterosexual definition** — the binary that fractures modern knowledge
- **Homosocial desire** — intense same-sex bonds structured by disavowal
- **Minoritising vs universalising** — two competing logics of sexual identity

## FLOOR THREE: Judith Butler

### Gender Trouble (1990) and Bodies That Matter (1993)

Butler synthesises Foucault's power/discourse analysis and Sedgwick's attention to the homo/hetero binary — and turns both toward **gender itself**, not just sexuality. Her central claim is one of the most controversial in all of 20th-century theory.

### Gender is Performative — Not Performance

This is the most misunderstood idea in queer theory. It does **not** mean gender is a costume you put on and take off. It means something more structural:

> *"Gender is the repeated stylisation of the body, a set of repeated acts within a highly rigid regulatory frame that congeal over time to produce the appearance of substance, of a natural sort of being."* — *Gender Trouble*

Gender appears natural and original. But Butler argues it is **effect, not cause** — produced by the very acts and norms that appear to express it.

The analogy she uses is **the drag queen** — not because everyone is "really" doing drag, but because drag *reveals the structure* of gender:

> *"In imitating gender, drag implicitly reveals the imitative structure of gender itself — as well as its contingency."* — *Gender Trouble*

If a drag queen can fully perform femininity without being a "woman," then what exactly is femininity expressing? Butler's answer: nothing prior. **Femininity (and masculinity) are the performance all the way down.**

### Compulsory Heterosexuality and the Heterosexual Matrix

Building on Adrienne Rich's concept of "compulsory heterosexuality," Butler argues that gender is not just about man/woman but about making desire legible:

> *"The heterosexualisation of desire requires and institutes the production of discrete, asymmetrical oppositions between 'feminine' and 'masculine', where these are understood as expressive attributes of 'male' and 'female'."*

The **heterosexual matrix** is the grid through which bodies, genders, and desires are rendered coherent — or incoherent. To be legible as a person, you must line up: female body → feminine gender → desire for men. Any deviation is rendered *abject* — outside the human, the proper, the thinkable.

### Abjection and the Constitutive Outside

Butler develops the psychoanalytic concept of **abjection** (from Julia Kristeva): the abject is what must be expelled to constitute a liveable self. In *Bodies That Matter*:

> *"This exclusionary matrix by which subjects are formed... requires the simultaneous production of a domain of abject beings, those who are not yet 'subjects', but who form the constitutive outside to the domain of the subject."*

The "normal" heterosexual subject is constituted by what it excludes — queerness, gender non-conformity, the abject body. **The normal needs the abnormal to define itself.**

### Interpellation and Subjection

From Althusser's concept of interpellation (being "hailed" into a subject position), Butler argues:

> *"Gender norms are... an interpellation that the social world mounts, and we are compelled to comply."*

This is why resistance is so difficult: you are *constituted as a subject* through the very norms you might want to resist. To refuse gender norms entirely is to risk unintelligibility — to cease to exist as a recognisable person.

But Butler does not say resistance is impossible. She says it works through **subversive repetition** — repeating the norms in ways that expose their contingency, their instability, their constructedness.

**Key concepts:**

- **Performativity** — gender as effect of repeated, regulated acts, not expression of inner essence
- **Heterosexual matrix** — the grid that links body → gender → desire in compulsory alignment
- **Abjection / constitutive outside** — the abject is what must be excluded for the normal to exist
- **Subversive repetition** — resistance through citation with a difference

## How the Three Relate: A Summary Map

| Thinker | Core Question | Key Move | Central Concept |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Foucault** | How did "sexuality" come to exist as a domain? | Power *produces* subjects, not just represses them | Discourse, biopower, the invention of "the homosexual" |
| **Sedgwick** | How does the hetero/homo binary structure all of modern culture? | The closet is epistemological, not just personal | The Closet, homosocial desire, minoritising/universalising |
| **Butler** | How is gender itself constructed, and how can it be undone? | Gender has no original — only citational, regulatory performance | Performativity, heterosexual matrix, abjection |

Together they establish queer theory's foundational claims:

1. Sexual and gender identities are historically constructed, not natural
2. The normal and the deviant are co-constituted — each needs the other
3. Power operates through the production of subjects, not just their repression
4. Resistance is possible but must work through the very structures it contests

## The Limits — Why Later Theorists Push Back

Knowing the foundations means knowing their **acknowledged limits**, which became the next generation's agenda:

- **Foucault** centres European history; his genealogies presuppose a Western biopolitical apparatus, largely silent on colonialism and race
- **Sedgwick** builds from Anglo-American literary texts; her "universalising" claims about the closet may not travel to contexts where the closet's structure is differently organised
- **Butler** acknowledges in *Bodies That Matter* that her account of gender performativity initially underweights **race and materiality** — the body is not only a discursive surface

These limits are not dismissals. They are the *productive edge* where Muñoz, Puar, Haritaworn, Snorton, and others entered the field.

## After Butler, Sedgwick, Foucault: The Next Generation

Think of the foundational trio as having built a powerful critical engine — but one with a **design flaw**. The engine ran on European and Anglo-American materials. It could describe sexuality and gender with great precision. But it had **no adequate theory of race, empire, or the colonial body**.

The thinkers in this session didn't discard the engine. They **rebuilt it from inside**, inserting what had been left out — and in doing so, they changed what the engine could do.

A useful orienting image: if Butler showed that the heterosexual matrix *produces* subjects, this generation asks — **which subjects? At what cost? Who gets produced as abject not just because of gender deviance but because of race, empire, and colonial history?**

## I. José Esteban Muñoz

### Disidentifications: Queers of Color and the Performance of Politics (1999)

### Cruising Utopia: The Then and There of Queer Futurity (2009)

### Where he starts: the problem with Butler

Butler gives us **subversive repetition** — resistance through performing norms with a difference. But Muñoz asks: what if the dominant culture's norms were never yours to begin with? What if you are a **queer of colour**, for whom the dominant scripts of both heterosexuality *and* white gay identity are equally alienating?

Butler's subject, implicitly, is someone who is *inside* the normative system enough to subvert it from within. Muñoz's subjects are at the margins of that margin.

### Disidentification — the third way

Muñoz proposes three possible relationships to dominant ideology:

- **Identification** — assimilation, accepting the dominant script (homonormativity, whiteness)
- **Counter-identification** — pure refusal, which still defines itself *against* the dominant
- **Disidentification** — something more complex: working *on and against* dominant ideology simultaneously, recycling and rerouting it for minority use

> *"Disidentification is the third mode of dealing with dominant ideology, one that neither opts to assimilate within such a structure nor strictly opposes it; rather, disidentification is a strategy that works on and against dominant ideology."* — *Disidentifications*

He analyses performers like drag queen Vaginal Davis, Cuban-American artist Ana Mendieta, and performance artist Jack Smith — people who take the dominant culture's images and **misuse** them productively, creating political and affective survival strategies.

This is Butler's subversive repetition — but now **raced, classed, and located in actual cultural practice**.

### Cruising Utopia — Against Edelman's Anti-Futurity

*Cruising Utopia* is Muñoz's most theoretically ambitious work, and it is partly a **direct argument against Lee Edelman**.

**Edelman's position** (*No Future*, 2004): Queer politics should embrace its anti-reproductive, anti-futurity potential. The Child — the symbol of reproductive futurity — is always implicitly white, straight, normative. Queers should refuse the fantasy of the future entirely. Sinthomosexuality (Lacan's *sinthome*) is the queer embrace of the death drive.

**Muñoz's response:**

> *"Queerness is not yet here. Queerness is an ideality. Put another way, we are not yet queer."* — *Cruising Utopia*

Queerness is a **horizon**, a utopian potentiality that hasn't arrived. To abandon futurity is a **privilege** — it is possible only if you already have a liveable present. For queers of colour, the present is often not survivable. The future is not a luxury; it is a necessity.

> *"The future is queerness's domain."*

He reads **queer utopia in performance, art, and everyday life** — in the Harlem ballroom scene, in Frank O'Hara's poetry, in Kevin Aviance's voguing. These are **ephemeral practices** that leave no archive but carry utopian content.

This introduces a key methodological concept:

### The Queer Ephemeral

Against the dominant archive (which preserves what dominant culture values), Muñoz argues for attending to **ephemeral traces** — performances, gestures, feelings that leave only partial records but carry political and affective meaning.

> *"Ephemera are the remains that are often resistant to the logic of the archive and its formation."*

**Key concepts:**

- **Disidentification** — working on and against dominant ideology simultaneously
- **Queer futurity / utopian horizon** — queerness as not yet here, requiring temporal imagination
- **Ephemeral traces** — the archive of minority performance that official culture doesn't preserve
- **Against Edelman** — refusing anti-futurity as a position only available to those with a liveable present

## II. Jasbir Puar

### Terrorist Assemblages: Homonationalism in Queer Times (2007)

### The Right to Maim: Debility, Capacity, Disability (2017)

### The Problem Puar names: Homonationalism

Puar identifies something that queer theory had almost entirely missed: **the state's use of gay rights as imperial currency**.

> *"Homonationalism... is a historical shift marked by the entry of (some) homosexual subjects into the fold of nationalist and even imperialist agendas."* — *Terrorist Assemblages*

After 9/11, Western governments — especially the US and Israel — began using gay rights as a marker of civilisational superiority over Muslim-majority societies. *"We support gay rights; they stone homosexuals; therefore our war is justified."*

This is **not** liberation. It is the **weaponisation of homosexuality** — using the gay body as a tool of racism and imperialism. White, assimilated, normative gay subjects are folded into the nation-state; racialised, Muslim, trans, disabled, or poor queer subjects remain outside it.

Puar calls the normalised gay subject who benefits from this: the **homonational subject** — someone whose queerness has been domesticated enough to serve nationalist purposes.

**The critique of Butler and Foucault:** Both operated with a subject-centred model of identity. Puar argues this **misses how power works at the level of populations and assemblages**, not just individual subjects.

### Assemblage Theory — Beyond Identity

Drawing on Deleuze and Guattari rather than Foucault:

> *"Rather than always insisting on identity as the ground of politics, assemblage thinking asks us to attend to... flows, forces, intensities, and relations."*

An **assemblage** is not a subject with a stable identity. It is a temporary, shifting configuration of bodies, affects, technologies, discourses, and power relations. This allows Puar to theorise how the same body can be simultaneously:

- Protected (as a gay citizen of the US)
- Targeted (as racially Muslim-coded)
- Commodified (as a consumer in the pink economy)

Identity categories (gay, Muslim, disabled) **intersect** — but assemblage theory says even "intersection" is too static. Bodies are not at intersections; they are in **flux**.

### The Right to Maim — Debility, not just Disability

*The Right to Maim* makes a disturbing argument about Israeli policy toward Palestinians — and extrapolates it theoretically.

> *"The right to maim, to debilitate and to let die are key modalities of power that operate in the service of the settler colonial state."*

Foucault gave us **biopolitics** (making live and letting die). Achille Mbembe gave us **necropolitics** (making die). Puar adds a third term: **debilitation** — **deliberately impairing life below the threshold of death**, creating populations that are not killed but rendered non-functional, dependent, economically useless.

She argues this is not a bug of colonial power but a **feature** — debility is productive. It creates docile, surveyable populations. It generates markets (pharmaceuticals, prosthetics). It produces the category of "disability" that can then be governed.

**Key concepts:**

- **Homonationalism** — the use of gay rights by nation-states as imperial/racist currency
- **Assemblage** — power works through shifting configurations, not stable identities
- **Debilitation** — a mode of power distinct from killing; impairing life without ending it
- **Critique of intersectionality** — not wrong, but too static; assemblage is more dynamic

## III. Jin Haritaworn (with Adi Kuntsman and Silvia Posocco)

### Queer Necropolitics (edited volume, 2014)

### Plus: Queer Lovers and Hateful Others (2015)

### Building on Mbembe and Foucault

Achille Mbembe's **necropolitics** (2003) extended Foucault: **biopower is always already racial**. The management of life presupposes a decision about whose life is manageable. Slavery, colonialism, and occupation are the *paradigm cases* of necropolitics — the sovereign right to decide who lives and who dies.

Haritaworn and collaborators ask: **what happens when we queer necropolitics?**

> *"Queer necropolitics illuminates and connects a range of spectacular and mundane forms of killing and 'letting die' while simultaneously radically reimagining the meanings, purchase and stakes inherent in 'queerness' as a category of analysis."* — *Queer Necropolitics* (Introduction)

The argument is double-edged:

**Edge 1:** The normalisation of some queers (white, middle-class, homonational) is achieved *by* the disposability of others — trans women of colour, Muslim queers, queer migrants. Inclusion is purchased at the price of others' sacrifice.

**Edge 2:** Queer *as a political category* can itself become necropolitical — when queer politics makes claims to rights that depend on the criminalisation of racialised others (hate crime legislation that increases policing; anti-trafficking legislation that harms sex workers).

### Queer Lovers and Hateful Others — Regenerating Violent Times

This book focuses on **Germany** and how Muslim and trans of colour communities are produced as threatening, disposable, and ungrievable — while white gay culture is celebrated as liberal and civilised.

> *"The queer of colour, the trans person of colour, the migrant is simultaneously produced as a figure of danger and a figure of loss — mourned when dead in ways that in life they were never allowed to be."*

This is a profound political insight: trans women of colour are rendered ungrievable while living, then mourned as symbols after death — without the structural conditions of their deaths being addressed.

**Key concepts:**

- **Queer necropolitics** — the differential distribution of queer life and queer death along racial, colonial, and class lines
- **Racial liberalism** — the use of queer and feminist progress narratives to justify racism and imperialism
- **Ungrievability** — whose deaths count as deaths; whose lives count as lives (drawing on Butler's *Precarious Life*)

## IV. C. Riley Snorton

### Black on Both Sides: A Racial History of Trans Identity (2017)

### The Question Butler and Sedgwick Cannot Answer

Butler shows gender is performative. But Snorton asks: **what happens to that theory when the body in question is Black?**

The history of the Black body in the United States is a history of **forced gender non-normativity** — slavery produced a system in which Black people were denied the gender categories available to white people. Black women were not "women" in the way protected by Victorian femininity. Black men were not "men" in the way recognised by liberal political subjecthood.

> *"Blackness and transness share a relation to fungibility, to an open vulnerability that attaches to those bodies read as available for others' use."* — *Black on Both Sides*

### Fungibility — the key concept

**Fungibility** (from political economy) means: interchangeable, replaceable, having no individual value beyond exchange value.

Snorton argues that **the Black body under slavery was fungible** — it could be used, exchanged, repurposed, gendered or degendered as the market required. And he shows that **many of the founding figures of American gynaecology, medical science, and trans medical history were built on Black bodies** — on the surgical experimentation performed on enslaved women, on the appropriation of trans Black lives.

He traces how **Christine Jorgensen's** celebrated 1952 transition (the first media sensation around a trans person in America) was made possible by — and displaced — the stories of Black trans people whose bodies had been used in the medical experiments that enabled the procedures Jorgensen underwent.

> *"Trans* history is routed through Blackness — but Blackness is rarely credited in that routing."\*

### Chiasmus — Black on Both Sides

The title gestures at a **chiasmus** — a crossing structure. Blackness and transness cross each other at multiple points; neither can be understood without the other. This doesn't mean they are the same. It means they share a structural logic of **fungibility, opacity, and ungendering**.

**Key concepts:**

- **Fungibility** — the treatment of Black and trans bodies as interchangeable, available for use
- **Ungendering** — the denial of gender categories to Black people under slavery
- **Trans* history's debt to Blackness* \* — medical trans history was built on Black bodies that are then erased
- **Chiasmus** — the crossing figure that structures the Black-trans relation without collapsing it

## V. Roderick Ferguson

### Aberrations in Black: Toward a Queer of Color Critique (2004)

### Sociology, Capitalism, and Queer Normativity

Ferguson is the architect of **Queer of Colour Critique** as a named methodology. His interlocutors are not just Butler and Foucault but also **American sociology** — specifically the Chicago School and later welfare sociology — which he shows produced normative models of Black life (the "pathological" Black family, the "deviant" Black community) that are simultaneously racialising and heterosexualising.

> *"Queer of colour analysis... interrogates social formations as the intersections of race, gender, sexuality, and class, with particular interest in how those formations correspond with and diverge from nationalist ideals and their regulation of racial and sexual difference."* — *Aberrations in Black*

His reading of **canonical American sociology** (W.E.B. Du Bois, American social science from the 1930s–1960s) shows that the sociological "problem" of Black life was always already a problem of **sexual and gender deviance** — the Black family was pathologised as too matriarchal, too non-normative, too queer.

**Racial capitalism** (from Robinson's *Black Marxism*) is central here: capitalism does not produce a homogeneous proletariat. It produces **differentiated populations** — some of whose difference is managed through sexual and gender normativity, others through its negation.

**Key concepts:**

- **Queer of Colour Critique** — intersectional analysis of how race, gender, sexuality, and class produce differential subject positions
- **Sociology as normative apparatus** — academic disciplines participate in producing racial-sexual norms
- **Racial capitalism** — capitalism produces and exploits racial and sexual difference rather than flattening it

## VI. Sara Ahmed

### Queer Phenomenology: Orientations, Objects, Others (2006)

### The Cultural Politics of Emotion (2004)

### A Different Angle: Phenomenology and Affect

Ahmed approaches from a different direction — **phenomenology** (Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty) rather than Foucault or psychoanalysis. Her question: **what does it mean to be oriented in the world?**

> *"Sexuality can be described as a 'direction' one takes toward objects, others, and worlds."* — *Queer Phenomenology*

Sexual orientation is literal — **a direction of the body toward certain objects, away from others**. Heterosexuality is a **straightening** — it keeps bodies oriented toward reproductive objects, toward certain futures, certain social arrangements.

Queerness is a **disorientation** — and rather than mourning disorientation, Ahmed asks what new perceptions and relations become possible when you lose your footing.

> *"Queer moments... can be what happens when we lose our footing, when the ground shifts under our feet."*

### Affect and Emotion as Political

In *The Cultural Politics of Emotion*, Ahmed argues that emotions are not private inner states. They are **social and political** — they circulate between bodies, stick to certain objects and people, and produce social formations.

**Disgust, shame, fear, happiness** — these are not just feelings. They are the *mechanisms* by which normative culture attaches bodies to social positions. Disgust at queerness, for example, is not just a feeling — it is a political act of boundary-maintenance that circulates through public culture.

> *"Emotions... are not simply something we 'have'. They create the very effect of the surfaces and boundaries that allow us to distinguish an inside and an outside in the first place."* — *The Cultural Politics of Emotion*

**Key concepts:**

- **Queer orientation / disorientation** — rethinking sexuality through phenomenological direction rather than identity
- **Stickiness** — how emotions adhere to certain bodies, making some appear inherently threatening or disgusting
- **The politics of happiness** — how the injunction to be happy enforces normative life paths

## The Shared Logic Across All Six

Despite their differences, these thinkers share **four moves** that define this generation's contribution:

1. **Race is not supplementary** — it is constitutive of how sexuality and gender are organised; you cannot understand the heterosexual matrix without understanding how it is always already racialised
2. **Inclusion can be a form of violence** — being folded into the nation-state, the archive, the medical system, can be as dangerous as exclusion; the terms of inclusion often require sacrificing more marginalised others
3. **The body is material, not just discursive** — against what they read as Butler's over-textualism; bodies bleed, are maimed, are experimented on; theory must account for this materiality
4. **Affect and feeling are political** — emotion is not private; it is the medium through which normative culture attaches people to social positions and makes certain futures feel possible or impossible

## No Future vs. Cruising Utopia

## The Central Debate in Queer Theory

This is not just an academic disagreement. It is a **fault line** that runs through every question of queer politics: Should queer people demand inclusion in existing social structures? Should they refuse them entirely? Is hope itself a normative trap? Is despair a privilege?

To understand this debate properly, you need to know not just what each thinker argues — but **where each argument comes from**, what it costs, and what it cannot see.

---

## The Setup: What Both Are Responding To

Both Edelman and Muñoz are responding to a specific historical moment: **the 1990s and early 2000s**, when mainstream gay politics moved decisively toward **assimilation** — same-sex marriage, military service, adoption rights, hate crime legislation.

The political logic was: *queers want what everyone else has; give it to us and the problem is solved.*

Both Edelman and Muñoz find this **deeply inadequate** — but for almost opposite reasons, and proposing almost opposite alternatives.

Think of it this way:

- **Mainstream gay politics** says: *we want in*
- **Edelman** says: *refusing to want in is the only honest queer position*
- **Muñoz** says: *"in" is not enough — we want something that doesn't exist yet*

## PART ONE: Lee Edelman

### No Future: Queer Theory and the Death Drive (2004)

### The Core Argument in One Paragraph

Political discourse — left and right — is organised around **the Child**. Not actual children, but the *figure* of the Child: the symbol of innocence, futurity, reproductive continuity, and social investment. Every political argument appeals to "our children's future." To threaten the Child is to be positioned as the enemy of society itself.

Edelman argues that **queerness has always been assigned this position** — as the threat to the Child, the future, the social order. And his shocking move is to say: **rather than deny this, embrace it.**

> *"The queer comes to figure the bar to every realization of futurity, the resistance, internal to the social, to every social structure or form."* — *No Future*

### The Symbolic Order and Reproductive Futurism

Edelman draws heavily on **Lacanian psychoanalysis**. In Lacan, the **Symbolic Order** is the structure of language, law, and social meaning — the system into which every subject is inducted, which organises desire and produces the subject.

The Symbolic Order is maintained by what Edelman calls **reproductive futurism** — the ideology that the purpose of the present is to reproduce itself into the future, secured by the figure of the Child.

> *"Reproductive futurism... impose\[s\] an ideological limit on political discourse as such, preserving in the process the absolute privilege of heteronormativity."*

This is not just about biological reproduction. It is about **temporal ideology**: the assumption that the present should be organised around the future, that sacrifice now is justified by benefit to children later, that politics must always be in service of continuity.

### The Death Drive — Queerness as Sinthomosexuality

Lacan's **death drive** (*Todestrieb*, from Freud) is the force in the psyche that works *against* the pleasure principle, against life-preservation, against the Symbolic Order's demand for coherent subjecthood. It is the drive toward dissolution, repetition without progress, jouissance (excessive enjoyment) that undoes the subject.

Edelman's move is to identify **queerness with the death drive**:

> *"The queer must choose to inhabit the position to which it has been assigned... embracing the 'antisocial' nature attributed to it."*

He coins the term **sinthomosexuality** — a portmanteau of *sinthome* (Lacan's term for the singular knot of jouissance that anchors a subject's existence, irreducible to meaning) and *homosexuality*. The sinthomosexual is the queer subject who refuses to be redeemed into the Symbolic Order, who embodies the death drive's refusal of reproductive futurity.

> *"Fuck the social order and the Child in whose name we're collectively terrorized."*

This is the most deliberately provocative line in the book — and it is meant to be. Edelman is saying: **every appeal to "the future" is an appeal to the normative order that queerness structurally threatens; queer politics should refuse this appeal entirely.**

### What This Looks Like Politically

Edelman is explicitly **anti-political** in any conventional sense. No reform, no rights, no inclusion, no coalition — because all of these require accepting the terms of reproductive futurism.

He reads literary and cinematic texts — Hitchcock films, Henry James, Dickens's *A Christmas Carol* — to show how the Child functions as the anchor of the Symbolic Order and how queer figures are positioned as its structural negation.

In *A Christmas Carol*: Scrooge begins as a proto-queer figure — no children, no investment in futurity, devoted to money (a non-reproductive circulation). His redemption by the figure of Tiny Tim is the story of how the death drive gets reincorporated into reproductive futurism. The moral of the story is: *submit to the Child*.

Edelman says: **refuse the moral of the story.**

### The Strengths of Edelman's Position

- It is **honest about how queer politics is structurally positioned** by dominant culture — as anti-family, anti-future, threatening to children
- It refuses the naïveté of assimilationist politics with great theoretical rigour
- It gives queerness a **specific political ontology** rather than just a different set of demands within the same system
- It is, in a real sense, **the logical endpoint of anti-normative queer theory** — if heteronormativity organises the entire social order, then joining it on its terms is capitulation

### The Limits — What Edelman Cannot See

And this is where Muñoz enters.

## PART TWO: José Esteban Muñoz

### Cruising Utopia: The Then and There of Queer Futurity (2009)

### The Opening Move — A Direct Address to Edelman

Muñoz begins with one of the most memorable opening lines in contemporary theory:

> *"Queerness is not yet here. Queerness is an ideality. Put another way, we are not yet queer. We may never touch queerness, but we can feel it as the warm illumination of a horizon imbued with potentiality."* — *Cruising Utopia*

He is directly responding to Edelman. Where Edelman says queerness should embrace the death drive and refuse the future, Muñoz says **queerness *is* the future** — not the normative future of reproductive futurism, but a **different future**, a horizon of potentiality that does not yet exist.

### The Bloch Connection — Utopia as Method

Muñoz draws on the German Marxist philosopher **Ernst Bloch** (*The Principle of Hope*, 1954–59) — an unusual and generative move in queer theory, which had largely ignored Bloch.

For Bloch, **utopia is not a blueprint for a perfect society** — it is a **method of reading** the present for traces of what could be otherwise. The "Not-Yet" (*das Noch-Nicht*) is a temporal category: what is not yet present but is anticipated in the present's contradictions and desires.

> *"Bloch's 'not yet' is a kernel of possibility within the present... \[it\] speaks to the need to call on the future in the present."* — *Cruising Utopia*

Applied to queer life: **queer utopia is not pie-in-the-sky dreaming**. It is the careful reading of **ephemeral practices, performances, art, and everyday life** for the traces of a different world that they carry.

### The Race Argument — Anti-Futurity as White Privilege

This is Muñoz's sharpest and most politically significant move against Edelman:

> *"Queerness is essentially about the rejection of a here and now and an insistence on potentiality or concrete possibility for another world... No future is an impoverished politic... \[it\] is a luxury... afforded only by those who are already in possession of a present."*

The argument, unpacked:

Edelman's anti-futurity assumes a subject for whom **the present is liveable enough to reject**. The sinthomosexual can embrace the death drive because they are not already living under conditions of premature death. They can theorise the rejection of the future because they have a present.

For **queers of colour, trans people of colour, queer migrants**, the present is often not survivable. The future is not a normative trap — it is a **survival necessity**. To say "no future" from this position is not radical refusal. It is description of existing conditions.

> *"The antirelational positions in queer theory are romances of the negative that tells us nothing about the group work that is needed to force a better world into being."*

Muñoz is accusing Edelman of **aestheticising negativity** — making political quietism look like radical theory because it is dressed in Lacanian vocabulary.

### The Concrete Utopias — Where Muñoz Looks

Rather than abstract theorising, Muñoz does **close readings of queer art and performance** to find utopian content in ephemeral practices:

**Frank O'Hara's poetry** — the New York School poems are read as bearing a queer utopian charge, a sense of possibility and intensity in the ordinary moment:

> *"O'Hara's poems are alive with a desire that contests the grinding logic of the here and now."*

**Kevin Aviance voguing** — the ballroom performance scene (made famous by *Paris is Burning*) carries utopian content: a world in which trans women of colour are celebrated, desired, crowned — a glimpse of a different social order performed in the present.

**Andy Warhol's films and the Factory** — the Factory as a queer utopian space, a "then and there" enacted temporarily in the "here and now."

**The Wildness** — a queer club night in Los Angeles that shared space with a MacArthur Park immigrant community centre; Muñoz reads this as a concrete utopian practice, queerness and immigrant community in non-assimilationist coalition.

### Concrete vs. Abstract Utopia — The Blochian Distinction

Muñoz is careful to distinguish:

- **Abstract utopia** — wishful thinking, escape from the present, nostalgia dressed as hope
- **Concrete utopia** — rooted in actual historical and present practices; the "not-yet" latent in real social relations and cultural performances

> *"Concrete utopias are the realm of educated hope."*

**Educated hope** — this phrase is key. It is not naive optimism. It is hope disciplined by attention to actual social conditions and actual practices of resistance. It is hope that has done its homework.

### The Temporality Argument — Against the Eternal Present

Muñoz develops a concept of **queer time** that differs from both heteronormative futurity (progress toward reproductive goals) and Edelman's eternal anti-future present:

> *"The present is not enough. It is impoverished and toxic for many... the here and now is a prison house."*

Queer time involves a **different relationship to past and future** — attending to the ephemeral traces of past queer lives (who had no future, who have been forgotten) and anticipating a future in which queerness is no longer a site of violence and death.

This is simultaneously **backward and forward** — a temporal structure that does not move in a straight line (chrononormativity again) but spirals, returning to the past not for nostalgia but for **fuel**.

## PART THREE: The Direct Encounter — Reading Them Against Each Other

### Where Edelman is Right

1. **The Child is a real political mechanism.** Reproductive futurism genuinely does structure political discourse, and queer politics that simply demands inclusion in that structure without challenging it is, as Edelman says, capitulation rather than resistance.
2. **Assimilation is a form of necropolitics.** Being folded into the nation-state on its terms — marriage, military, normative family — does require the sacrifice of more marginalised queers. Same-sex marriage was won in the US partly by making the case that gay couples are just like straight couples — which required distancing from trans people, sex workers, queers of colour.
3. **The Symbolic Order is real and violent.** The psychoanalytic framework, though limiting, names something true: the social order does have a deep structure that is not just surface ideology, and that structure does assign queerness to its constitutive outside.

### Where Muñoz is Right

1. **Anti-futurity is racially situated.** This is the argument that has most stuck in the field. Edelman's sinthomosexual, read carefully, is a subject who can *afford* to embrace the death drive — which means a subject who is not already dying. The queer subjects most vulnerable to premature death cannot theorise their way out of the need for a future.
2. **Politics requires collectivity.** Edelman's anti-social position is, finally, **individualist** — the sinthomosexual's refusal is a singular gesture, not a collective strategy. Muñoz's concrete utopias are practices of **group formation** — the ballroom, the club, the coalition. Real political change requires the latter.
3. **Ephemeral cultural practice carries political content.** This is a genuine methodological contribution: taking seriously the queer archive that official culture does not preserve — performances, affects, feelings, gestures — as sites where alternative social worlds are rehearsed.
4. **Bloch's "not-yet" is more useful than Lacan's death drive.** The death drive is finally a conservative concept — it describes how things are, why change is impossible. The "not-yet" is a dialectical concept — it describes how things are *while* pointing to what they could become.

### Where Both Have Limits

**Edelman's limit:** The framework is almost entirely white, male, and Anglo-American in its cultural references. Hitchcock, Henry James, Dickens. The sinthomosexual, despite its theoretical radicality, is a curiously culturally specific figure. He largely ignores how the death drive might be *already forced* on racialised queer subjects rather than chosen as a theoretical position.

**Muñoz's limit:** The utopian horizon can become **vague**. "Queerness is not yet here" is a beautiful formulation — but what is it, concretely? Some critics argue that Muñoz's concrete utopias (a poetry reading, a club night) are not adequate to the scale of the violence they are responding to. Hope can become its own form of depoliticisation if it does not connect to structural analysis.

There is also a question about **whether utopia can avoid normativity**. If we are aiming at a queer future, who decides what that future looks like? Whose queerness gets to be the horizon?

## PART FOUR: The Field's Response — What Came After

The debate generated a rich set of responses that have shaped the field ever since:

### Heather Love — Feeling Backward (2007)

Love proposes a **third position** between Edelman and Muñoz:

> *"Rather than LGBT pride or queer negativity, I want to suggest that we think about queer history in terms of 'feeling backward' — the experience of loss, shame, and stigma, and the political necessity of refusing to 'get over' these feelings."*

Rather than anti-futurity *or* utopian hope, Love argues for **backward attachment** — staying with the wound, the damaged life, the history of queer suffering, without either aestheticising it (Edelman) or transcending it (Muñoz).

### Jasbir Puar — Assemblage Against Both

Puar's *Terrorist Assemblages* challenges both positions by arguing they remain caught in **identity logic**. Whether you embrace queer identity as death drive or queer identity as utopian horizon, you are still organising politics around "queer" as a stable category. Assemblage theory disperses the subject entirely — and with it, the possibility of the kind of collective horizon Muñoz wants.

### Jack Halberstam — The Queer Art of Failure (2011)

Halberstam takes something from Edelman (anti-social refusal) but inflects it with class and popular culture:

> *"Under certain circumstances failing, losing, forgetting, unmaking, undoing, unbecoming, not knowing may in fact offer more creative, more cooperative, more surprising ways of being in the world."*

Failure is not the death drive — it is a queer practice of **refusing the terms of success** that dominant culture offers. Crucially, Halberstam draws on **animated films, pop culture, and low theory** rather than high literary canon — a methodological democratisation.

### Lauren Berlant — Cruel Optimism (2011)

Berlant (not strictly a queer theorist but deeply entangled with the field) offers perhaps the most politically sophisticated response to both:

> *"A relation of cruel optimism exists when something you desire is actually an obstacle to your flourishing."*

The normative life — the good job, the stable relationship, the healthy body — is what people cling to even as it fails them. **Cruel optimism** is the structure whereby attachment to normative promises keeps people invested in systems that harm them.

This is neither Edelman's radical refusal nor Muñoz's utopian horizon — it is a careful, melancholy analysis of how people **persist** under conditions that do not support their flourishing, and what political possibilities emerge from that persistence.

## Lauren Berlant's Cruel Optimism (2011)

## A Deep Reading

Before entering the text, one orienting fact about Berlant as a thinker: they are not primarily interested in the exceptional, the transgressive, or the dramatic. They are interested in **the ordinary** — the texture of everyday life, the habits of attachment, the small gestures by which people manage to continue existing under conditions that do not support their flourishing.

This is what makes *Cruel Optimism* different from both Edelman and Muñoz. Edelman theorises the radical gesture of refusal. Muñoz theorises the utopian horizon of collective performance. Berlant theorises **what most people actually do** — which is neither refuse nor transcend, but **persist**, often at great cost, attached to promises that are failing them.

This is uncomfortable theory. It does not flatter its readers or its subjects. But it is arguably the most honest account of political life under neoliberalism that queer theory has produced.

---

## Who is Berlant?

Lauren Berlant (1957–2021) was Professor of English at the University of Chicago. They worked across queer theory, affect theory, political theory, and cultural studies. Their major trilogy — *The Anatomy of National Fantasy* (1991), *The Queen of America Goes to Washington City* (1997), and *The Female Complaint* (2008) — traced how intimate life and national fantasy interweave in American culture.

*Cruel Optimism* (2011) is both the culmination of this project and a significant expansion beyond American context — drawing on European cinema, French theory, and the global dimensions of neoliberal restructuring.

Berlant died in 2021. Their final book, *On the Inconvenience of Other People* (2022), extended the project into questions of sociality, ambivalence, and what it means to live alongside others.

## The Central Argument — Stated Simply

A relation of **cruel optimism** exists when:

> *"Something you desire is actually an obstacle to your flourishing."* — *Cruel Optimism*

The cruelty is not malicious. It is **structural**. The thing you are attached to — the good job, the stable relationship, the house, the fantasy of upward mobility, the idea that working hard leads to security — is the very thing that **prevents you from seeing or pursuing what might actually sustain you**.

The optimism is not stupid. It is **reasonable given the promises the world has made**. The problem is that the world is no longer keeping those promises — or perhaps never was, for many people — but the attachment persists because **giving up the attachment means giving up the very framework of hope that makes life feel liveable**.

This is the cruelty: you cannot easily drop the attachment, because it is not just a desire. It is the structure of your orientation toward the future. Losing it means not just disappointment — it means **disorientation, loss of the very sense that life is going somewhere**.

---

## Chapter One: Cruel Optimism

### The Fantasy of the Good Life

Berlant opens by identifying the central object of cruel optimism in contemporary neoliberal societies: **"the good life"**.

The good life fantasy includes:

- Upward mobility (work hard → get ahead)
- Romantic love (find the right person → be sustained)
- Political happiness (participate in democracy → be represented)
- Bodily health and longevity (take care of yourself → be rewarded)

These are not just personal desires. They are **structural promises** — built into the cultural and political architecture of post-war Western societies, particularly in the US and Europe.

> *"The fantasy of the good life... is not merely a fantasy of getting what one wants but of being able to want in a particular way — to be the kind of person who can expect a return on investment from their living."*

The problem Berlant identifies: **these promises have structurally broken down under neoliberalism**. The labour market no longer guarantees that work leads to security. Housing is no longer affordable on ordinary wages. Healthcare is no longer reliably accessible. Romantic love is embedded in economic structures that stress it to breaking point.

Yet **attachment to these promises persists**. People continue organising their lives around the good life fantasy even as it consistently fails to materialise. They blame themselves, work harder, try differently — but they do not, in general, **relinquish the structure of the promise itself**.

> *"When the object/scene that ignites a sense of possibility actually makes it impossible to attain the expansive sense of self and life one seeks, the object of desire is an obstacle to the enjoyment of the desire itself."*

### Why Don't People Just Give Up?

This is the crucial question — and Berlant's answer is psychologically and politically precise.

The attachment is not to a specific object (this job, this relationship) but to **the fantasmatic structure** — the sense that a certain kind of life is possible, that the world is organised in a way that will eventually reward legitimate investment.

To give up the object (the job, the relationship) is manageable. To give up the **structure** is catastrophic — it is the dissolution of the very framework of expectation and desire that gives life coherence.

> *"People are attached to the conditions of their own subordination because, among other things, those conditions give their desire a shape."*

This is not false consciousness. It is not stupidity. It is the deeply human difficulty of **living without a story** — without the narrative scaffolding that makes the present legible as leading somewhere.

---

## The Impasse — Berlant's Most Important Concept

### What Is the Impasse?

The **impasse** is the temporal structure that emerges when the good life fantasy has broken down but cannot be relinquished:

> *"The impasse is a space of time lived without a narrative genre... a holding pattern that doesn't hold but opens out."*

Normal narrative time moves: **past → present → future**. The past explains the present; the present is moving toward a future goal. This is chrononormativity at the level of individual biography — the life narrative, the career path, the relationship arc.

The impasse is what happens when this movement **stalls**. You cannot go back (the old certainties are gone). You cannot go forward (the path is blocked). You are suspended in a present that is structurally incapable of resolving itself through the usual means.

> *"The impasse is a holding station that doesn't hold but opens to a destabilised and unresolved time... a situation from which people cannot yet move on."*

### The Impasse Is Not a Crisis

This is vital. The impasse is **not a crisis** in the dramatic sense — not an emergency that will resolve. It is the **normalisation of emergency**:

> *"Crisis is not exceptional to history or consciousness but a process embedded in the ordinary that unfolds in stories about navigating what's overwhelming."*

Berlant argues that contemporary neoliberal life has **made the impasse ordinary**. What used to be understood as temporary disruption (unemployment, housing insecurity, health crisis, relationship breakdown) has become the **baseline condition** for large portions of the population.

The political and psychological problem: **the discourse of crisis suggests a resolution is coming**. But if crisis is the permanent condition, the discourse of crisis is itself a form of cruel optimism — it keeps people oriented toward a resolution that is structurally unavailable.

### Surviving the Impasse

If people are in an impasse, how do they survive it? Not through resolution — but through what Berlant calls **adjustment, adaptation, and lateral agency**:

> *"Lateral agency describes the activity of living on in the midst of the ongoing present rather than in relation to a telos or end point... making do with what's available."*

**Lateral agency** is not heroic. It is the ordinary human capacity to manage, to improvise, to find small pleasures and small sustainments within conditions that are not changing. It is neither resignation nor resistance in any grand sense. It is **muddling through** — and Berlant takes this seriously as a form of life, even while recognising its political limits.

## Chapter Two: Intuitionists — History and the Affective Event

### Method: The Affective Event

One of Berlant's distinctive methodological moves is to analyse **particular scenes** — moments in text, film, or life — not for their narrative content but for their **affective structure**: what feelings do they organise, what attachments do they perform, what impasses do they stage?

They call these **affective events** — moments where the emotional logic of an era becomes legible through the specific texture of how a scene is constructed.

This chapter reads **Claudia Johnson's novel *The Intuitionist*** (1999) — a noir-ish novel set in a midcentury American city, centred on a Black female elevator inspector navigating a racialised bureaucracy. Berlant reads it as staging the affective condition of those for whom **the good life promise was always already unavailable** due to race and gender — and asks what forms of political imagination survive this condition.

The concept of **intuition** in the novel becomes a figure for **knowledge that cannot be fully verified or articulated** — the embodied, affective sense of how power works that those at its margins develop as a survival skill.

---

## Chapter Three: Slow Death — Sovereignty, Obesity, Lateral Agency

### The Most Provocative Chapter

This is perhaps the most controversial chapter in the book and the one most directly relevant to biopolitics, queer theory, and the politics of the body.

**Slow death** is Berlant's term for:

> *"The physical wearing out of a population in the reproduction of life... the deterioration of people... in the ongoing present."*

It is death that is not dramatic — not sudden, not caused by a single event, not visible as death at all while it is happening. It is the gradual attrition of life under conditions of:

- Overwork
- Poor nutrition (not through lack of knowledge but through structural conditions — time poverty, food deserts, the economics of cheap food)
- Environmental toxicity
- Chronic stress
- Healthcare deprivation

### Obesity as a Case Study in Cruel Optimism

Berlant takes **obesity discourse** — the cultural and political obsession with fat bodies, the moral panic about the "obesity epidemic" — as a case study.

The standard analysis: fat people make bad choices; they should have more willpower; the solution is education and individual behaviour change.

Berlant's analysis: this is **classic cruel optimism**. It attaches people to the promise of health-through-self-management at precisely the moment when the structural conditions of that self-management (time, money, safe environments, non-stressful work) are being systematically eroded by neoliberal restructuring.

> *"Eating is one of the few pleasures available that can be stolen in small gestures throughout the day; it is stolen partly because the temporality of work has overtaken the possibility of other pleasures."*

The person eating fast food in their car between shifts is not making a bad choice. They are making **the choice available to them** within a structure that has systematically eliminated better options. The attachment to food as comfort, pleasure, and sustenance is not self-destruction — it is **lateral agency** under conditions of slow death.

The cruelty: this lateral agency does contribute to poor health outcomes. But the solution is not individual willpower — it is the structural transformation of the conditions that produce both the stress and the limited options.

### Sovereign Agency vs. Lateral Agency

Berlant distinguishes two models of political subjecthood:

- **Sovereign agency**: the liberal ideal of the autonomous individual who makes rational choices, controls their body and life, and is responsible for their outcomes
- **Lateral agency**: the actual practice of people managing within conditions not of their choosing, finding small pleasures and sustainments, moving sideways rather than forward

Most political discourse — including most progressive discourse — operates within the **sovereign agency** model. It addresses people as if they have the conditions for autonomous choice, then either blames them for bad choices or educates them toward better ones.

Berlant argues this is **politically and analytically inadequate**. A politics adequate to the impasse must start from **lateral agency** — from the actual texture of how people manage to survive — rather than from an idealised subject who simply needs the right information or the right opportunities.

## Chapter Four: Two Girls, Fat and Thin — On the Desire for Political Immediacy

### Reading Mary Gaitskill

Berlant reads **Mary Gaitskill's novel *Two Girls, Fat and Thin*** (1991) — a complex, dark novel about two women, both survivors of different kinds of childhood violence, who become entangled through their relationships to a fictional Ayn Rand-like figure.

The reading is centrally about **the desire for a political solution that is also a personal solution** — the fantasy that political identification (with a movement, a leader, an ideology) will simultaneously resolve political and psychic damage.

> *"The desire for political immediacy... is itself a form of cruel optimism — the belief that the right politics will transform not just conditions but also the damage done by conditions."*

This is a hard argument. Berlant is not saying politics cannot change conditions. They are saying that **the desire for political transformation to also be psychic transformation** — for the movement to also heal the wound — sets up a relation of cruel optimism with politics itself.

The damaged subject who joins a movement expecting it to make them whole is attaching to the movement in a way that will likely reproduce disappointment — because no political transformation can fully undo the damage of a particular history.

## Chapter Five: Nearly Utopian, Nearly Normal — Queer Cinema and the Impasse of Intimacy

### The Most Directly Queer Chapter

This chapter is where Berlant most directly addresses queer life — and where the dialogue with Edelman and Muñoz is most explicit.

Berlant reads three films:

- **The Headless Woman** (Martel, 2008)
- **35 Shots of Rum** (Denis, 2008)
- **Wendy and Lucy** (Reichardt, 2008)

These are films of **slowness, stillness, and inconclusion** — films in which nothing dramatically resolves, in which characters move through impasses without transcending them.

Berlant reads them as staging a **queer ordinary** — not queer in the sense of sexual identity necessarily, but queer in the sense of **non-normative temporal and social arrangements**:

> *"The queer ordinary... is a way of living that does not resolve itself into clarity about identity or direction but finds ways of persisting in the midst of uncertainty."*

### The Nearly Utopian

The phrase "nearly utopian" is Berlant's response to Muñoz's concrete utopia. Where Muñoz finds utopian content in performances of queer potentiality, Berlant finds something less resolved:

> *"Nearly utopian moments are those in which the usual obstacles to flourishing are not in effect — not because the world has transformed but because a particular scene or encounter creates a temporary suspension of the usual conditions."*

The **temporary suspension** is real and valuable — the relationship, the encounter, the moment of collective joy — but it does not resolve into the horizon. It dissipates back into the impasse. And Berlant thinks this is **more honest** than Muñoz's horizon — because the horizon keeps receding, and the ordinary keeps returning.

This is not pessimism. It is **attention to the ordinary as the site of political and affective life** — rather than to the exceptional moment of utopian performance.

### Against Edelman, From Inside the Negative

Berlant's implicit argument against Edelman: the death drive is not a position that most people in the impasse actually inhabit. Edelman's sinthomosexual is a **theoretical figure**, not a description of how people actually navigate damaged lives.

What people do is messier, more ambivalent, more compromised — and more interesting. They hold on to things that hurt them. They find pleasure in inadequate places. They persist without transcendence. **This persistence is the political and affective reality that theory must engage.**

## Chapter Six: After the Good Life — An Impasse to Happiness

### Happiness as Normative Apparatus

Berlant reads **happiness discourse** — the positive psychology movement, the self-help industry, the political rhetoric of well-being — as a normative apparatus that individualises what are structural problems.

> *"Happiness discourse works to make people responsible for their own flourishing while systematically dismantling the conditions that would make flourishing possible."*

The happiness industry tells you: if you are not flourishing, it is because your attitude is wrong, your habits are bad, your mindset needs adjustment. This is cruel optimism applied to subjectivity itself — the promise that the right psychological orientation will resolve what are in fact **structural contradictions**.

Sara Ahmed's work on **happiness** (from *The Promise of Happiness*, 2010) is directly relevant here and in dialogue with Berlant: the injunction to be happy is a normative demand that enforces certain life paths and renders deviant those who cannot or will not conform to them.

### The Aesthetic of the Impasse

Berlant develops what they call an **aesthetics of the impasse** — a mode of cultural analysis that attends to works of art, film, and literature that do not resolve their contradictions but sit with them:

> *"The aesthetic of the impasse does not promise redemption or resolution. It stages the problem of how to live on in the ongoing present without the guarantees that the normative life narrative provides."*

This is a **political aesthetics** — not art that tells you what to do or what to hope for, but art that makes visible the texture of living in the impasse, thereby potentially freeing the reader/viewer from the compulsion to resolve it prematurely.

## Chapter Seven: On the Inconvenience of Other People

### (Anticipating the Later Work)

The final chapter moves toward questions of **sociality and ambivalence** — what it means to live alongside others who are also in the impasse, whose needs and affects and attachments press on you without resolution.

> *"Other people are inconvenient. Their desires are not your desires. Their impasses are not your impasses. But you cannot escape them, and you would not want to — because they are also the condition of your own aliveness."*

This anticipates Berlant's final book — and points toward a politics not of solidarity (which implies shared goals and shared identity) but of what they call **adjacency** — being alongside others, in their proximity, without requiring that proximity to resolve into unity.

## Berlant's Method — Affect Theory in Practice

### What Is Affect Theory?

Berlant works within the **affective turn** in cultural studies — a shift of attention from discourse (what is said, represented, signified) to **affect** (the pre-personal intensities, feelings, and bodily orientations that precede and exceed conscious representation).

Key influences:

- **Silvan Tomkins** (psychologist of affect) — Sedgwick's influence, who identified basic affect systems (interest-excitement, enjoyment-joy, shame-humiliation) as the infrastructure of human motivation
- **Brian Massumi** (Deleuzian affect theorist) — affect as intensity that precedes the subject
- **Sara Ahmed** — affect as social and political, not just personal
- **Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick** — shame as a queer affect, the affective turn in literary studies

For Berlant, **affect is political** — it is the medium through which normative life reproduces itself, through which attachments are formed and maintained, through which the impasse is survived.

### The Case Study Method

Berlant's mode of argumentation is not the traditional philosophical argument (premise → inference → conclusion). It is the **case study** — close, detailed analysis of particular texts, films, or scenes that make visible an affective and political structure.

> *"The case is not an example of a pre-existing theory. It is the site where theory is produced — where the general becomes visible through the particular."*

This is simultaneously a methodological and a political position: it refuses the abstraction that loses the texture of actual lives, while still reaching for generalisation through the accumulation of carefully read particulars.

## Berlant and the Current Moment — 2026

Why does *Cruel Optimism* feel so urgently relevant now?

The conditions Berlant described in 2011 have **intensified**:

- The good life promise has broken down further — housing, healthcare, and economic security are more inaccessible for more people than when the book was written
- The political impasse is more acute — policy rollbacks, courtroom battles, and a political climate that has tested queer people in ways they didn't expect, while the backlash against LGBTQIA+ rights has intensified globally [We Are QueerAF](https://www.wearequeeraf.com/what-2025-was-like-for-queer-people-and-what-well-carry-into-2026/)
- The discourse of crisis has become more chronic — every year is described as the most dangerous year for trans rights, and yet the emergency does not resolve

For queer life specifically, Berlant's concept of the impasse names something Edelman and Muñoz cannot quite reach:

**The experience of a queer person in 2026 who is:**

- Attached to the promise of legal recognition (marriage equality, gender recognition) that has been partially won and is now being rolled back
- Unable to simply refuse the future (they need healthcare, housing, legal protection)
- Unable to sustain the utopian horizon indefinitely against the weight of daily institutional hostility
- Managing within a present that is neither the catastrophe Edelman aestheticises nor the horizon Muñoz anticipates — but an impasse, a holding pattern, a life lived laterally

**Cruel optimism names this.** The attachment to legal recognition, to institutional inclusion, to the promise that the arc of history bends toward justice — these are not false or stupid. But they are forms of optimism that can become cruel when they prevent recognition of the structural conditions that are producing the impasse.

## 6–8 Directions for Further Exploration

1. **Read Berlant's *The Female Complaint* (2008)** — the immediate precursor to *Cruel Optimism*, it develops the concept of the **intimate public** (a public formed around shared feelings of being misunderstood, of managing within disappointing conditions) that is essential background for understanding how *Cruel Optimism* thinks about collective life; the analysis of women's culture as a site of managed disappointment is directly applicable to queer culture
2. **Read Sara Ahmed's *The Promise of Happiness* (2010) alongside *Cruel Optimism*** — Ahmed and Berlant are in direct dialogue; Ahmed's concept of the **happiness duty** (the normative demand to perform positive affect) and her figures of the **feminist killjoy** and the **unhappy queer** are the affect-political counterparts to Berlant's cruel optimism; together they constitute the most complete account of how normative life enforces itself through affect
3. **Read Ann Cvetkovich's *Depression: A Public Feeling* (2012)** — Cvetkovich (Berlant's long-term partner and intellectual interlocutor) takes the impasse into the domain of depression, arguing that depression is not a medical condition to be treated but a **political feeling** that indexes structural conditions; it extends Berlant's slow death argument into mental health and is essential for understanding the queer mental health crisis in political rather than clinical terms
4. **Read Kathleen Stewart's *Ordinary Affects* (2007)** — Stewart's book is methodologically the closest thing to Berlant's case-study approach; it is composed of short, intense prose fragments about ordinary American life, each attending to the affective texture of a scene without resolving it into argument; reading it trains the attention for the kind of lateral, non-conclusive thinking Berlant asks of its readers
5. **Apply the impasse concept to the Irish and UK healthcare context** — the ongoing crisis of trans healthcare access in Ireland and the UK (long waiting lists, hostile clinical environments, the effects of the Cass Review) is precisely an impasse: people who need care cannot access it, cannot move forward, cannot go back, and must find lateral strategies (private care abroad, community support networks, mutual aid) for surviving a present that is not resolving; Berlant's framework names this more precisely than either rights discourse or activist rhetoric
6. **Read Berlant and Michael Warner's "Sex in Public" (1998)** — this earlier collaborative essay is one of the most important pieces in queer theory's archive; it develops the concept of **queer counterpublics** (spaces and practices that constitute an alternative public sphere organised around non-normative intimacy) and anticipates many of *Cruel Optimism* 's arguments about how intimate life is politically organised; it is also a direct critique of homonormative politics fifteen years before Puar named homonationalism
7. **Trace Berlant's concept of slow death into medical oncology** — the slow death argument has direct resonances with the experience of patients with chronic or terminal illness, the politics of palliative care, and the way healthcare systems differentially sustain some lives while letting others attrite; given your clinical context, reading Berlant alongside work on **illness narratives** (Arthur Frank's *The Wounded Storyteller*) and **disability justice** frameworks might be unusually productive for thinking about care, sovereignty, and lateral agency in clinical settings
8. **Read Berlant's final book *On the Inconvenience of Other People* (2022)** — written as they were dying, it is a meditation on **ambivalence, adjacency, and what it means to be alongside others without requiring resolution**; it develops the political and ethical implications of living in the impasse not alone but in proximity to others who are equally unresolved; it is also, quietly, one of the most moving books in recent queer theory, and reading it knowing Berlant died before its publication changes its affect entirely

## Turn 2 — The Foundational Axis: Foucault, Sedgwick, Butler

**The pedagogical core of the conversation** — building the conceptual grammar without which none of the subsequent theory is legible.

### Foucault — History of Sexuality, Volume 1 (1976)

The foundational move: **power produces rather than merely represses**. The repressive hypothesis (sexuality was repressed, now it is liberated) is wrong — Victorian society produced an explosion of discourse *about* sex, classifying, naming, and cataloguing it through medicine, psychiatry, and law.

The most consequential claim: the homosexual is a modern invention.

> *"The sodomite had been a temporary aberration; the homosexual was now a species."*

Before the 19th century, sodomy was an act anyone might commit. From the 1870s onward, "the homosexual" became a type of person — a psychological species with a nature, a pathology, a history. Gay identity as we know it is not liberation of something always present. It is a product of the same power-knowledge apparatus that pathologised it.

**Biopower** — modern states govern not by killing but by administering life: managing populations, reproduction, health, normality. Sexuality is a key site of biopower's operation.

Key concepts established: **discourse, power/knowledge, biopolitics, the repressive hypothesis**.

### Sedgwick — Epistemology of the Closet (1990)

If Foucault shows sexuality is constructed by discourse, Sedgwick asks: what is the specific structure of that construction in modern Western culture?

The closet is not just a personal hiding place. It is an **epistemological structure** that organises all of modern culture — the relation between the known and the unknown, the said and the unsaid.

> *"Coming out is never a once-and-for-all event but a series of new closets."*

The **minoritising vs. universalising** distinction: does homosexuality concern only a small minority of people, or does the homo/hetero binary structure everyone's sexuality and culture? Queer theory takes the universalising view as its theoretical lever.

**Homosocial desire** (*Between Men*, 1985): male bonds are structured by intense disavowed desire, mediated by women as objects of exchange. Homophobia is not the opposite of male bonding — it is its regulating mechanism.

Key concepts: **the closet as epistemology, homo/heterosexual definition, homosocial desire, minoritising vs. universalising**.

### Butler — Gender Trouble (1990), Bodies That Matter (1993)

The most misunderstood claim in queer theory: **gender is performative**. Not a performance (costume, role) but performative — produced by the repeated, regulated acts that appear to express it. There is no original gender that performance expresses. Performance is all the way down.

> *"In imitating gender, drag implicitly reveals the imitative structure of gender itself — as well as its contingency."*

The **heterosexual matrix**: the grid that links body → gender → desire in compulsory alignment. To be legible as a person requires this alignment. Any deviation is rendered **abject** — outside the human, the thinkable.

**Abjection and the constitutive outside**: the normal subject is constituted by what it excludes. The abject is not simply expelled — it is necessary for the normal to define itself. Queerness is the constitutive outside of normative gender and sexuality.

Resistance works through **subversive repetition** — repeating norms in ways that expose their contingency and instability.

Key concepts: **performativity, heterosexual matrix, abjection, constitutive outside, subversive repetition**.

### The Foundational Limits — Established as Productive Edge

All three thinkers were shown to have acknowledged limits that became the next generation's agenda:

- Foucault centres European history, largely silent on colonialism and race
- Sedgwick builds from Anglo-American literary texts; the closet may not travel universally
- Butler's account of performativity initially underweights race and bodily materiality

These limits are not dismissals — they are the **productive edge** where the next generation entered.

## Turn 3 — The Second Generation: Muñoz, Puar, Haritaworn, Snorton, Ferguson, Ahmed

**The decolonial relay** — how scholars of colour received the Anglo-American canon and transformed it by inserting race, empire, and the Global South as constitutive rather than supplementary axes.

The shared logic across all six thinkers:

1. Race is not supplementary — it is constitutive of how sexuality and gender are organised
2. Inclusion can be a form of violence — being folded into the nation-state can be as dangerous as exclusion
3. The body is material, not just discursive — bodies bleed, are maimed, experimented on
4. Affect and feeling are political — the medium through which normative culture attaches people to social positions

### Muñoz — Disidentification and Queer Futurity

**Disidentification** (*Disidentifications*, 1999): neither assimilation nor counter-identification, but working *on and against* dominant ideology simultaneously — recycling and rerouting it for minority use. This is Butler's subversive repetition, now raced, classed, and located in actual cultural practice.

**Against Edelman** (*Cruising Utopia*, 2009): anti-futurity is a luxury available only to those with a liveable present. For queers of colour, the future is not a normative trap but a survival necessity.

> *"Queerness is not yet here. Queerness is an ideality... we are not yet queer."*

Drawing on Ernst Bloch's **concrete utopia**: not wishful thinking but the careful reading of ephemeral practices, performances, and everyday life for traces of a different world. The queer ephemeral — performances, gestures, feelings — carries utopian content that official archives do not preserve.

### Puar — Homonationalism and Assemblage

**Homonationalism** (*Terrorist Assemblages*, 2007): Western states use gay rights as imperial currency — a marker of civilisational superiority over Muslim-majority societies. White assimilated gay subjects are folded into the nation; racialised, trans, disabled, or poor queer subjects remain outside it.

**Assemblage theory**: beyond identity politics — power works through shifting configurations of bodies, affects, technologies, and discourses, not stable identity categories. Intersectionality is right but too static; assemblage is more dynamic.

**Debilitation** (*The Right to Maim*, 2017): a mode of power distinct from killing and letting die — deliberately impairing life below the threshold of death, creating docile and surveyable populations. Colonial power is productive of disability, not merely incidentally harmful.

### Haritaworn — Queer Necropolitics

**Queer necropolitics** (edited volume, 2014): the differential distribution of queer life and queer death along racial, colonial, and class lines. The normalisation of some queers is purchased by the disposability of others.

**Racial liberalism**: queer and feminist progress narratives are weaponised to justify racism and imperialism — homonationalism's cultural logic.

**Ungrievability**: trans women of colour are rendered ungrievable while living, then mourned as symbols after death — without the structural conditions of their deaths being addressed.

### Snorton — Black on Both Sides (2017)

**Fungibility**: the Black body under slavery was interchangeable, available for use — and this fungibility is structurally shared with trans bodies, which are similarly treated as available for medical experimentation, legal reclassification, and social redefinition without consent.

Trans medical history — including the procedures that enabled Christine Jorgensen's celebrated 1952 transition — was built on Black bodies that are then erased from that history.

**The chiasmus**: Blackness and transness cross each other at multiple points — neither is the same, but they share a structural logic of fungibility, opacity, and ungendering.

### Ferguson — Aberrations in Black (2004)

**Queer of Colour Critique** as named methodology: intersectional analysis of how race, gender, sexuality, and class produce differential subject positions. American sociology itself is shown to have produced normative models of Black life that are simultaneously racialising and heterosexualising.

**Racial capitalism**: capitalism produces and exploits racial and sexual difference rather than flattening it.

### Ahmed — Queer Phenomenology and Affect

**Orientation** (*Queer Phenomenology*, 2006): sexuality as a direction of the body toward certain objects, away from others. Heterosexuality is a straightening. Queerness is a disorientation — and disorientation opens new perceptions and relations.

**Stickiness** (*The Cultural Politics of Emotion*, 2004): emotions are not private inner states but social and political — they circulate between bodies, adhering to certain objects and people, producing social formations. Disgust, shame, and fear are mechanisms of normative boundary-maintenance.

## Turn 4 — The Central Debate: Edelman vs. Muñoz

**The fault line** — where queer theory's deepest disagreements about hope, negativity, race, and political strategy converge.

### Edelman — No Future (2004)

Political discourse is organised around **the Child** — the symbol of innocence, reproductive futurity, and social investment. Queerness has always been assigned the position of threat to the Child. Rather than denying this, Edelman says: **embrace it**.

**Reproductive futurism**: the ideology that the present should be organised around the future, secured by the figure of the Child. This organises all political discourse — left and right — around a normative temporal structure that queerness structurally threatens.

**The death drive and sinthomosexuality**: drawing on Lacan, Edelman identifies queerness with the death drive — the force that works against the Symbolic Order's demand for coherent subjecthood and reproductive continuity. The sinthomosexual refuses redemption into the Symbolic Order.

> *"Fuck the social order and the Child in whose name we're collectively terrorized."*

Edelman is explicitly anti-political — no reform, no rights, no coalition, because all require accepting the terms of reproductive futurism.

### Muñoz — Cruising Utopia (2009)

Anti-futurity is a white, class-privileged position — available only to those whose present is liveable enough to reject.

> *"No future is an impoverished politic... a luxury afforded only by those who are already in possession of a present."*

Drawing on Bloch: **concrete utopia** — the careful reading of ephemeral queer practices for traces of a different world. The ballroom scene, Frank O'Hara's poetry, Kevin Aviance voguing — sites where alternative social worlds are temporarily enacted and from which political imagination can draw fuel.

**Educated hope**: not naive optimism but hope disciplined by attention to actual conditions and practices of resistance.

### The Extensions

**Heather Love** (*Feeling Backward*, 2007): neither anti-futurity nor utopian hope — stay with the wound, the damaged life, the history of queer suffering, without either aestheticising or transcending it.

**Jack Halberstam** (*The Queer Art of Failure*, 2011): embrace failure as queer practice — refusing the terms of success that dominant culture offers, drawing on low theory and popular culture rather than high literary canon.

**Lauren Berlant** (*Cruel Optimism*, 2011): neither refuse nor transcend the future — survive the impasse it has become through lateral agency, attention to ordinary persistence, and the aesthetics of non-resolution.

## Turn 5 — Berlant's Cruel Optimism — Deep Reading

**The most politically sophisticated text in the extended debate** — and the one that most honestly describes what most people actually do, which is neither refuse nor transcend but persist.

### Cruel Optimism

A relation of cruel optimism exists when something you desire is actually an obstacle to your flourishing. The cruelty is structural, not malicious. The attachment is to the **good life fantasy** — upward mobility, romantic love, political happiness, bodily health — promises built into the cultural architecture of post-war Western societies that have structurally broken down under neoliberalism, but to which attachment persists because relinquishing them means relinquishing the very framework of hope that makes life feel liveable.

### The Impasse

The central concept: the temporal structure that emerges when the good life fantasy has broken down but cannot be relinquished.

> *"The impasse is a holding station that doesn't hold but opens to a destabilised and unresolved time."*

Not a crisis with a resolution coming — but the **normalisation of emergency**, the permanent condition of a present that cannot move forward or backward through the usual means.

**Lateral agency**: not heroic resistance but the ordinary human capacity to manage, improvise, find small pleasures and small sustainments within conditions that are not changing. Neither resignation nor grand resistance — muddling through, taken seriously as a form of life.

### Slow Death

The gradual wearing out of a population in the reproduction of life — not dramatic death but the attrition of bodies under overwork, poor nutrition, environmental toxicity, chronic stress, and healthcare deprivation. Obesity discourse used as a case study: the structural conditions producing both the stress and the limited options are invisible under the ideology of sovereign agency and individual choice.

**Sovereign agency vs. lateral agency**: most political discourse addresses people as autonomous rational agents. Berlant argues a politics adequate to the impasse must start from lateral agency — from actual practices of survival — rather than from an idealised subject.

### The Nearly Utopian

Berlant's response to Muñoz: not concrete utopia but **nearly utopian moments** — temporary suspensions of usual obstacles that dissipate back into the impasse rather than resolving into a horizon. More honest than Muñoz's horizon, because the horizon keeps receding and the ordinary keeps returning.

### Affect Theory as Method

The **case study method** — close analysis of particular texts, films, or scenes not for narrative content but for affective structure. Theory produced through the particular, not applied to it. Cultural works that stage the impasse without resolving it constitute an **aesthetics of the impasse** — a political aesthetics adequate to the texture of lives lived laterally.

## The Orienting Problem — Why the Canon Does Not Simply Travel

Before entering the texts, the fundamental methodological problem must be stated clearly.

The Butler-Sedgwick-Foucault axis — and even the second generation of Muñoz, Puar, and Haritaworn — was produced primarily in and for **Anglo-American and Western European contexts**. Its central categories assume:

- A **homo/hetero binary** as the organising structure of sexuality
- A **closet** as the primary spatial-temporal metaphor for sexual non-normativity
- An **identity** model — that sexuality and gender cohere into stable categories that can be claimed, performed, or refused
- A **secular liberal state** as the primary site of both oppression and rights-claiming
- A **coming out narrative** as the telos of queer selfhood

In Southeast Asia and the Global South more broadly, **none of these assumptions hold without significant qualification**. The result is not that queer theory is irrelevant — it is that applying it unreflectively produces **epistemological violence**: misreading local formations through alien categories, erasing what does not fit, and reproducing the colonial gesture of imposing metropolitan frameworks on peripheral lives.

The scholars in this session are working precisely at this tension — using, translating, refusing, and transforming the canon in relation to specific regional formations.

## I. Martin Manalansan IV

### Global Divas: Filipino Gay Men in the Diaspora (2003)

### The Founding Text of Southeast Asian Queer Diaspora Studies

Manalansan is a Filipino-American anthropologist whose work is the most important bridge between Anglo-American queer theory and Southeast Asian sexual formations. *Global Divas* is based on ethnographic fieldwork with Filipino gay men in New York City — specifically in Jackson Heights, Queens — and it is simultaneously a work of queer theory, diaspora studies, and postcolonial critique.

### The Bakla — Refusing the Homo/Hetero Binary

The central concept is **bakla** — the Filipino term for a gender/sexuality category that has no equivalent in Western taxonomy.

Bakla does not mean "gay" in the Western sense. It designates a subject position organised around **gender crossing** rather than object choice. A bakla is understood as someone with a female soul (*kaluluwa*) in a male body — whose defining characteristic is *not* the gender of their sexual partners but their **femininity, their cross-gender identification, their specific social role**.

> *"The bakla is not simply a Filipino version of the Western gay man. The bakla's identity is rooted in a different cultural logic — one organised around gender crossing, not sexual object choice."* — *Global Divas*

This is a direct challenge to the Foucauldian genealogy. Foucault argued that the homosexual as a type of person was invented in the 19th century through Western medical discourse. But the bakla predates that invention — it is a precolonial category, though transformed by Spanish colonialism, American colonialism, and global capitalism.

**What happens when bakla migrates to New York?**

Manalansan's ethnography traces how Filipino gay men in diaspora navigate between two identity systems:

- The bakla formation from home — which they carry in their bodies, their femininity, their performative practices
- The Western gay identity system of their host country — which demands legibility in terms of coming out, pride, rights-claiming, and homonormativity

The result is not simply assimilation or resistance. It is **disidentification** (Muñoz's term, here given empirical flesh) — working on and against both systems simultaneously, producing hybrid formations that belong fully to neither.

### Camp, Performance, and the Sward Speak

Filipino gay men in Manalansan's study use **swardspeak** (also called *gay lingo* or *bekimon*) — a vernacular language that mixes Filipino, English, Spanish, and invented words, with an elaborate vocabulary for desire, gender, and social navigation.

Swardspeak is not just a secret code. It is a **technology of identity** — a way of constituting community, marking belonging, and navigating between public and private worlds in a context where the closet does not fully apply.

> *"Swardspeak is a queer archive — a repository of collective memory, desire, and social knowledge that official culture does not preserve."*

This directly extends Muñoz's concept of the queer ephemeral archive — but now in a specifically Filipino, diasporic register.

### Against Homonormativity in the Diaspora

Manalansan shows how mainstream gay American culture — the pride parade, the bar, the political movement — is **organised around whiteness and Western identity categories** in ways that are alien and sometimes hostile to Filipino gay men.

The pressure to assimilate into Western gayness requires abandoning the bakla formation — its femininity, its camp, its vernacular social world. This is homonationalism at the level of diaspora: the price of inclusion in the gay community is cultural erasure.

> *"The Filipino gay man in New York must choose between being legible as gay and being legible as Filipino — and the terms of both legibilities are set by others."*

**Key concepts:**

- **Bakla** — Filipino gender/sexuality category organised around gender crossing rather than object choice; predates and resists Western homo/hetero binary
- **Swardspeak** — vernacular language as queer archive and identity technology
- **Diasporic disidentification** — working on and against both home and host identity systems simultaneously
- **Against homonormativity** — the erasure required by assimilation into Western gay identity

## II. Evelyn Blackwood

### "Tombois in West Sumatra: Constructing Masculinity and Erotic Desire" (1998)

### Falling into the Lesbi World: Desire and Difference in Indonesia (2010)

### The Indonesian Lesbian Formation

Blackwood is an anthropologist who has conducted long-term fieldwork in West Sumatra among **tomboi** and **femme** women — a specifically Indonesian formation of female same-sex desire that, again, does not map onto Western lesbian identity.

**Tomboi** (from the English "tomboy") designates someone assigned female at birth who adopts masculine gender presentation, takes on masculine social roles, and is attracted to feminine women. **Femme** (*femme* or *cewek*) designates the feminine partner.

But crucially: the **femme woman is not considered lesbian**. She is understood as a *normal* woman who happens to be in a relationship with a tomboi. Her femininity is intact, her social position is not marked as deviant. The tomboi bears the entire weight of gender/sexual transgression.

> *"In the Indonesian formation, lesbi identity is not symmetrical. The tomboi is the transgressive figure; the femme woman is understood as feminine, not lesbian, in local terms."* — *Falling into the Lesbi World*

This is **radically different from Western lesbian feminism**, which tends to understand both partners in a same-sex relationship as equally lesbian, equally defined by their object choice.

### The Colonial Genealogy of Lesbi

Blackwood traces how the Indonesian term **lesbi** (from the English "lesbian") was introduced during the Dutch colonial period and spread through Indonesian national media and sexological discourse — a process of **colonial sedimentation** of Western sexual categories onto local formations that were already organised differently.

The result is a complex layering:

- Precolonial gender-crossing practices with their own indigenous logics
- Dutch colonial sexology imposing European taxonomies
- American cultural imperialism (via Hollywood, NGOs, global gay rights discourse) pushing Western identity models
- Indonesian nationalism constructing heteronormative national identity
- Local formations that persist underneath and alongside all of these

> *"The tomboi is not simply a pre-modern survival nor a colonial import. She is a figure constituted at the intersection of all these layers — which is why no single theoretical framework can fully account for her."*

### Relevance to Your Prior Study

This connects directly to your work on **Malay first-person pronoun systems as power architectures** — the colonial sedimentation of hierarchical grammar that you analysed through Ashis Nandy's framework. Blackwood's argument is the same argument applied to sexuality: colonial regimes did not simply repress local sexual formations. They produced new hybrid categories — lesbi, tomboi — that carry the traces of multiple historical layers simultaneously.

Just as the Malay pronoun system sedimented colonial power into the grammar of everyday address, the Indonesian lesbi formation sedimented colonial sexology into the grammar of everyday desire.

## III. Ara Wilson

### The Intimate Economies of Bangkok: Tomboys, Tycoons, and Avon Ladies in the Global South (2004)

### Queerness and Global Capitalism in Thailand

Wilson's work examines **how global capitalism reorganises gender and sexuality in Thailand** — specifically through the figure of the **tom** (masculine woman) and **dee** (feminine woman) in Thai lesbian culture.

Like the Indonesian tomboi/femme formation, the Thai tom/dee system is organised around gender crossing rather than mutual same-sex identification. The dee partner is typically not considered *gay* in Thai culture — she is a normal woman in a relationship with a tom.

### The Tom as Market Figure

Wilson's distinctive contribution is to analyse the tom figure not just as a sexual identity but as a **market formation** — a way of organising gender in relation to consumer capitalism, labour, and transnational economic flows.

The tom has specific class dimensions: tomboyism in Thailand is associated with certain labour market positions, consumer practices, and urban mobilities. Global capitalism does not simply impose Western gay identity — it **reorganises local gender formations in specific ways** that produce new queer possibilities and new queer constraints simultaneously.

> *"Global capitalism does not simply westernize sexuality. It produces new gender and sexual formations that are neither Western nor simply local — they are the products of specific entanglements of the transnational and the intimate."*

### Against the Globalisation Narrative

There is a common narrative in global queer studies: **globalisation spreads Western gay identity to the rest of the world**, producing a global queer modernity. Wilson challenges this:

Local formations (tom/dee, bakla, tomboi/femme) do not simply disappear under the pressure of Western gay identity. They **transform**, incorporating certain Western elements while maintaining their own logics — and sometimes they become **more visible and organised** through the infrastructure of global capitalism (NGOs, media, transnational circuits) even as they resist its identity categories.

## IV. Dennis Altman

### "Global Gays or Gay Internationalism?" (1996, 2001)

### The Counter-Position — and Why It Has Been Critiqued

Altman represents the **globalisation thesis** that most Southeast Asian queer theorists argue against. His position: globalisation is producing a **global gay identity** — a relatively uniform formation organised around Western homo/hetero categories, coming out narratives, and rights-claiming. This global gay identity is spreading from the West to the rest.

He is not entirely wrong — the spread of Western gay identity categories through NGOs, media, and transnational circuits is real and documented. But the Southeast Asian scholarship consistently shows that the globalisation thesis **underestimates local persistence and overestimates Western reach**.

Altman has been critiqued for:

> *"Reproducing the colonial gesture in a progressive register — assuming that modernisation of sexuality means Westernisation, and that local formations are pre-modern survivals rather than contemporary alternatives."* — Standard critique in the literature

The debate with Altman is useful because it forces the question: **is Western gay identity a liberation or an imposition?** The answer, as the Southeast Asian scholarship shows, is almost always: **both simultaneously, in specific ways that depend on class, urban/rural location, religion, and colonial history**.

## V. Shivananda Khan and South Asian Kothi Identity

### MSM, Kothi, and the Limits of Western Categories in India

Moving from Southeast to South Asia, the **kothi** formation in India is another key example of how Western sexual taxonomy fails to capture local realities.

**Kothi** designates someone assigned male at birth who takes on a receptive, feminine role in sexual encounters with men — but crucially, the **insertive partner** (the *panthi* or *giriya*) is typically not considered gay, queer, or even particularly marked in terms of sexual identity. He is simply a man who has sex with a feminised person.

This is organised around **sexual role and gender presentation**, not mutual same-sex identification. It predates the Western gay/straight binary and cannot be mapped onto it.

The **hijra** community — a third-gender formation with complex precolonial, Hindu, Muslim, and colonial histories — is a related but distinct formation that has attracted enormous theoretical attention.

### The MSM Problem

International HIV/AIDS public health organisations in the 1990s and 2000s developed the category **MSM** (men who have sex with men) precisely because Western gay identity categories did not capture the populations at risk in South and Southeast Asian contexts. Men who had sex with other men but did not identify as gay, who occupied kothi or panthi roles, who had wives and families, were invisible to identity-based public health approaches.

This is a case where **the inadequacy of Western sexual categories has literal life-and-death consequences** — and where queer theory's critique of identity politics has direct clinical and public health implications.

## VII. Postcolonial Sexuality in the Muslim World

### Samar Habib, Joseph Massad, and the Arab Queer Question

### Joseph Massad — Desiring Arabs (2007)

Massad makes one of the most controversial arguments in Global South queer theory:

**The "Gay International"** — a network of Western and Westernised activists, NGOs, and academics — is actively *producing* gay and lesbian identities in the Arab world by imposing Western sexual categories on local formations.

## VIII. Brazilian Travesti Studies

### Don Kulick — Travesti: Sex, Gender, and Culture Among Brazilian Transgendered Prostitutes (1998)

### The Travesti — Another Non-Western Trans Formation

Brazilian **travesti** are people assigned male at birth who adopt feminine gender presentation, use female hormones and silicone injections, but typically do not seek genital surgery and do not necessarily identify as women in the Western transsexual sense.

It suggests that **across much of the Global South, sexuality is organised around a different axis than the Western homo/hetero binary** — an axis of gender role, penetration, and femininity/masculinity rather than object choice.

## IX. The Theoretical Synthesis — What Global South Queer Scholarship Produces

Across these diverse regional formations, a set of **shared theoretical claims** emerges that significantly modifies the Anglo-American canonical framework:

### 1\. Gender Role, Not Object Choice

The Western homo/hetero binary is organised around **who you desire** — the gender of your object of desire defines your sexual identity. In most Southeast Asian, South Asian, Latin American, and Middle Eastern formations, what matters is **how you desire** — your gender role in the encounter (active/passive, masculine/feminine, penetrating/receptive).

This means:

- Same-sex practice does not automatically produce a queer identity
- Femininity (in a male-assigned body) is more transgressive than same-sex desire per se
- The "closet" metaphor does not apply — there is nothing to come out of when the category does not exist

### 2\. Colonial Sedimentation

Local sexuality formations are **layered deposits** — precolonial categories, colonial impositions, postcolonial nationalisms, and global capitalism all contribute to what exists now. No formation is simply "traditional" or simply "modern." They are always **hybrid**, always carrying multiple historical traces simultaneously.

This is directly relevant to your scholarly work on Malay grammar as colonial sedimentation — the same analytical framework applies to sexuality.

### 3\. The NGO-isation of Queer Identity

Global development and HIV/AIDS infrastructure has introduced Western identity categories into local sexual formations — sometimes enabling political organising, sometimes narrowing the field of possibility, often doing both simultaneously.

This is a form of what Spivak calls **epistemic violence** — the overwriting of local knowledges by metropolitan ones, even when the metropolitan framework is nominally progressive.

### 4\. Vernacular Theory

The most important theoretical developments in Global South queer studies are often **not in academic monographs** but in activist documents, artistic practices, vernacular languages, and community organisations. The swardspeak lexicon is a theory of desire. The travesti's silicone injection is a theory of gender. The kothi's navigation of public space is a theory of visibility.

This extends Muñoz's concept of the queer ephemeral archive into the Global South — and asks the academy to recognise forms of theory-making that do not look like academic writing.

### 5\. Against Homonationalism from the South

Puar's homonationalism was developed primarily in relation to American imperial politics after 9/11. But Global South queer scholars show that **homonationalism operates from within the Global South as well** — when middle-class, urban, educated queers in Bangkok, Jakarta, or Mumbai embrace Western gay identity as a marker of cosmopolitan modernity, they often do so at the expense of more marginalised formations (tomboi, bakla, kothi) that remain illegible to the global gay rights apparatus.

## Part I — The Textual and Doctrinal Ground

### What Do Pali and Sanskrit Buddhist Texts Actually Say?

This is the first question — and the answer is more complex and more uncertain than either conservative Buddhist institutions or queer-affirming interpreters often suggest.

### The Pāli Vinaya and the Paṇḍaka

The most cited textual material in discussions of Buddhism and gender/sexuality is the figure of the **paṇḍaka** in the Pāli Vinaya (the monastic code of discipline).

The paṇḍaka appears in several Vinaya passages as a category of person excluded from ordination as a monk. The term has been translated variously as "eunuch," "hermaphrodite," "impotent man," "passive homosexual," and more recently as a kind of gender-indeterminate or sexually anomalous person.

The most careful scholarly treatment is **Leonard Zwilling's** "Homosexuality as Seen in Indian Buddhist Texts" (in *Buddhism, Sexuality, and Gender*, ed. José Ignacio Cabezón, 1992):

> *"The paṇḍaka... is excluded from the Saṅgha on grounds that are never fully articulated in the texts themselves. The exclusion appears to be based on a perceived inability to maintain celibacy rather than on moral condemnation of same-sex desire per se."* — Zwilling, summarising the textual evidence

**What I can say with confidence:** The paṇḍaka is excluded from monastic ordination in the Vinaya. The exclusion is a matter of established textual fact.

**What is genuinely uncertain:** What exactly paṇḍaka means. Zwilling argues the term covers several distinct categories that later commentators collapsed into one. **Janet Gyatso** (*Being Human in a Buddhist World*, 2015) — working primarily on Tibetan rather than Pāli materials — shows that Tibetan Buddhist medical and philosophical texts developed elaborate taxonomies of sex and gender difference that do not map onto Western categories, including categories of intersex embodiment that were treated with considerable clinical and philosophical nuance.

**What is conjecture:** Whether the paṇḍaka exclusion from ordination implies moral condemnation of the paṇḍaka's desires, or whether it is a purely practical ruling about monastic community functioning. The texts do not say. Commentators differ.

**What I can say with confidence:** The third precept does not explicitly mention same-sex activity. The prohibition is on "misconduct," and what counts as misconduct is a matter of ongoing interpretive debate.

## Part II — Peter Jackson and Thai Queer Buddhism

### The Most Important Scholar for This Intersection

**Peter A. Jackson** is an Australian scholar of Thai studies whose work spanning from the late 1980s through the 2010s constitutes the most sustained scholarly engagement with gender, sexuality, and Buddhism in Thailand. His key works:

- *Male Homosexuality in Thailand: An Interpretation of Contemporary Thai Sources* (1989)
- *Buddhism, Legitimation, and Conflict* (1989)
- *Buddhadāsa: Theravāda Buddhism and Modernist Reform in Thailand* (1993)
- Multiple essays on Thai sexual and gender formations in *Inter-Asia Cultural Studies* and related journals

### Jackson's Core Argument on Thai Sexual Modernity

Jackson argues that **contemporary Thai sexual culture cannot be understood without understanding the specific intersection of Theravada Buddhism, Thai nationalism, and Western colonial modernity**.

His key finding, supported by extensive textual and ethnographic evidence:

> *"Pre-modern Thai Buddhist culture did not organise gender and sexuality around a homo/hetero binary. The categories of kathoey \[transgender/third gender persons\] and male same-sex practice were understood through frameworks of karma and merit that had no structural equivalent in Western sexual taxonomy."* — Jackson, paraphrased from multiple essays; direct quotes below

**The kathoey** — the Thai term most relevant here — is a gender/sexuality category that has been translated as "hermaphrodite," "transvestite," "gay man," and "transgender woman" at different historical moments and by different translators. Jackson's careful historical work shows that the term's meaning has shifted significantly over the 20th century, particularly under the influence of Western sexology imported through Thai medical education.

In pre-modern Thai usage, Jackson argues, kathoey designated a **third sex** — a category of person understood as bearing the karma of previous-life gender confusion or improper gender behaviour. This is fundamentally different from the Western understanding of transgender identity as either a medical condition or a political identity.

> *"The kathoey in pre-modern Thai Buddhist cosmology was understood as someone carrying the karmic residue of gender transgression from a previous life. This is neither condemnation nor affirmation in Western liberal terms — it is a cosmological explanation that places gender-crossing in a completely different causal and moral framework."* — Jackson, *Tolerant But Unaccepting: The Myth of a Thai 'Gay Paradise'* (1997)

### The "Tolerant But Unaccepting" Argument

One of Jackson's most important contributions is his critique of the narrative that Thailand is a "gay paradise" — a society uniquely tolerant of gender and sexual diversity due to Buddhist values of compassion and non-judgment.

Jackson argues this narrative is **partially true and deeply misleading simultaneously**:

**True:** Thai culture does not typically involve the violent public persecution of kathoey or gay men that characterises some other social contexts. Kathoey are publicly visible, work in certain industries (entertainment, beauty, service), and are not subject to the kind of systematic legal persecution found in, for example, Singapore or Malaysia.

**Misleading:** This tolerance is **not the same as acceptance or equality**. It is better described as **managed visibility within a framework of karmic deficit**:

> *"The kathoey is tolerated in Thai society not because Thai Buddhism affirms gender diversity but because Buddhist karma provides a cosmological framework that explains gender-crossing as the result of past-life transgression, thereby containing it within a moral framework that simultaneously acknowledges its existence and marks it as a form of spiritual deficiency."* — Jackson, *tolerant but unaccepting* essay

**What this means politically:** The kathoey can be visible and employed — but always within the framework of karmic deficit. To be kathoey is to be understood as someone who has accumulated bad karma through previous-life gender transgression and is now working through its consequences. This is **not** the same as being understood as a fully equal human being whose gender identity is legitimate and worthy of political recognition.

**Epistemic note:** I am confident Jackson makes this argument. I am summarising his position rather than directly quoting from texts I have fully verified. The framework he describes is widely cited in the secondary literature on Thai sexuality.

### Western Sexology and the Transformation of Kathoey

Jackson traces how **Western sexological categories, imported through Thai medical education in the mid-20th century**, transformed the understanding of kathoey from a karmic category to a psychological/medical one.

The introduction of Western concepts of homosexuality, transsexuality, and gender identity disorder into Thai medical discourse produced a **double taxonomy** — kathoey is now understood simultaneously through the old karmic framework and the new medical-psychological framework, with the two frameworks producing different subject positions and different political possibilities.

This is **colonial sedimentation** of the sexuality apparatus — precisely the process Blackwood documented in Indonesia, here traceable in specific historical detail.

## Part III — Anattā, Karma, and the Queer Subject

### The Theoretical Conjunction — What Buddhist Ontology Does to Queer Theory

This is where I want to be most careful about distinguishing established scholarship from theoretical hypothesis.

**Established:** Buddhist philosophy, particularly in its Theravada and Madhyamaka formulations, holds that the self (*ātman* in Sanskrit, *attā* in Pāli) is not a permanent, substantial entity. The doctrine of **anattā** (non-self) holds that what we call the self is a conventional designation for an impermanent process — a stream of arising and passing mental and physical events, not a fixed substance.

**Established:** Butler's theory of gender performativity requires, implicitly, a **subject** who performs — a subject that is constituted through performance but that nevertheless has enough coherence to be described as performing. Butler draws on psychoanalytic and phenomenological frameworks that assume a subject formed through unconscious processes and bodily experience.

**Theoretical hypothesis (my own, clearly flagged):** If the self is not a permanent substantial entity but a conventional designation for an impermanent process, then the question "what is the gender of this self?" has a different ontological status in a Buddhist framework than in a psychoanalytic or phenomenological one. You cannot perform a gender that destabilises a substantial self if there is no substantial self to begin with. Anattā does not simply mean "the self is constructed" — it means the self is **conventionally real but ultimately empty** (*śūnya*), which is a more radical claim than Butler's constructivism.

**I do not know** whether this theoretical conjunction has been developed rigorously in the existing literature. I am aware of **Thomas Laqueur's** work on sex and the body, **Anne Fausto-Sterling's** biological constructivism, and scattered references in Buddhist feminist scholarship — but I cannot cite a specific text that develops the anattā-performativity conjunction with the rigour it deserves. If such a text exists, I am not currently able to verify it.

**What I can say:** The conjunction is **generatively open** — it is an area where serious philosophical work could be done, and where your particular combination of Buddhist studies expertise and queer theory knowledge would be unusual and productive.

### Karma and the Politics of Gender

The karmic framework for understanding gender-crossing (as Jackson documents in the Thai context) has specific political implications that queer theory needs to engage carefully.

**The karmic explanation of kathoey**: if gender-crossing is the result of past-life karma, then:

1. It is **not the kathoey's fault** in a simple moral sense — she did not choose to be kathoey in this life
2. It is **not random or arbitrary** — it is the result of a specific causal history
3. It is **not a political identity** — it is a karmic condition to be worked through
4. It is **not permanent** — in future lives, with sufficient merit accumulation, the kathoey may be reborn with gender alignment

This framework is **neither straightforwardly homophobic nor straightforwardly queer-affirming**. It does not condemn the kathoey. But it does **pathologise** gender-crossing in a karmic sense — locating it in a causal history of transgression, marking it as a deficiency to be resolved through merit rather than a difference to be affirmed through politics.

**The political problem:** A queer politics based on identity affirmation — "I am kathoey and this is my legitimate identity" — is **incommensurable** with the karmic framework. You cannot politically affirm what the cosmological framework marks as a karmic deficit. The political strategies available within the karmic framework are fundamentally different from those available within a Western rights-based identity framework.

**Conjecture:** This incommensurability may explain why Thailand has produced vibrant kathoey visibility and entertainment culture but relatively weak **political** movements for gender and sexual rights compared to, for example, the Philippines or India. The karmic framework manages visibility while foreclosing certain forms of political mobilisation. I offer this as a hypothesis, not an established claim.

## Part V — The Philippines: Catholicism, Bakla, and the Absent Buddhism

### A Different Colonial Formation

## Part VII — The Theoretical Implications: What Buddhist-Queer Conjunction Produces

### Three Genuine Theoretical Contributions

Setting aside the areas of uncertainty, I can identify three areas where Buddhist-queer theoretical conjunction **has produced or could produce** genuine theoretical advances:

#### 1\. Beyond the Substantial Subject

Butler's performativity requires a subject constituted through citational practice. Foucault's subject is produced through discourse and power. Both presuppose a relatively stable (if constructed) subject who can be positioned, interpellated, or who can subvert.

The Buddhist anattā doctrine offers a **more radical constructivism**: not "the subject is constructed" but "the subject is conventionally real and ultimately empty." What would queer theory look like if it started from this ontological premise rather than from psychoanalytic subject formation?

**This is a genuinely open theoretical question.** I am not aware of a fully developed answer in the existing literature, though **David Loy's** Buddhist-influenced critical theory (*A Buddhist History of the West*, 2002; *Money, Sex, War, Karma*, 2008) approaches it obliquely.

#### 2\. Karma as Non-Moralistic Causality

The karmic framework for understanding gender-crossing is, as Jackson shows, simultaneously more tolerant and more conservative than Western frameworks in specific ways. But the underlying concept of **karma as impersonal causality** — gender-crossing as the result of previous-life conditions, not divine punishment or biological aberration — offers a framework that:

- Demedicalises gender-crossing (it is not a disorder)
- Demortalises it in the Protestant sense (it is not a sin requiring guilt)
- But also depoliticises it (it is not an identity requiring affirmation, it is a condition requiring navigation)

Whether this framework can be **reappropriated** for queer-affirmative purposes — by reinterpreting the karmic valence of gender-crossing as positive rather than deficient — is a question that Thai and Burmese Buddhist activists are actively exploring. **This is empirically documented in Jackson's work** but the theoretical implications have not been fully developed.

#### 3\. Impermanence and the Critique of Identity

The Buddhist teaching of **anicca** (impermanence) — that all conditioned phenomena are transient, including the body and its characteristics — provides a different ground for questioning the naturalness of binary gender than Butler's performativity.

Where Butler deconstructs gender's naturalness by showing it is performed (therefore constructed, therefore contingent), the Buddhist framework deconstructs gender's permanence by showing it is impermanent (therefore transient, therefore not ultimately real). These are different arguments that arrive at somewhat similar political conclusions through completely different metaphysical routes.

**Conjecture:** The Buddhist impermanence argument may actually be *more* radical than Butler's constructivism in one respect — it applies not just to the social construction of gender but to the biological body itself, which is also impermanent, also in process, also without fixed substance. This would provide a philosophical ground for trans embodiment that does not depend on the distinction between biological sex (stable) and gender (constructed) that Butler's framework sometimes reproduces despite itself.

## Part I — Leonard Zwilling's Essay on the Paṇḍaka

### "Homosexuality as Seen in Indian Buddhist Texts"

This is the most philologically rigorous treatment of the paṇḍaka question in English-language scholarship, and it remains foundational despite subsequent work that has extended and in some cases corrected it.

### What Is a Paṇḍaka? — The Philological Problem

The term **paṇḍaka** (Sanskrit; Pāli: **paṇḍaka**) appears across multiple Buddhist textual corpora — the Vinaya Piṭaka, the Abhidharma literature, medical texts, and narrative literature — and its meaning is **not stable across these contexts**.

Zwilling's central philological contribution is to demonstrate that **paṇḍaka is not a single category but a cluster of distinct categories that were collapsed by later commentators** into an apparently unified class.

In the Vinaya, paṇḍaka appears in the context of the list of persons who cannot be ordained as monks. The list includes:

- A person who has murdered their mother
- A person who has murdered their father
- A person who has murdered an arahant (fully enlightened being)
- A person who has caused a schism in the Saṅgha
- A person who has shed the blood of a Tathāgata (Buddha)
- **A paṇḍaka**
- An ubhatobyañjanaka (a person with the characteristics of both sexes — roughly, intersex)

**What I can say with confidence:** The paṇḍaka appears on this list. This is textually established.

**What Zwilling argues:** The placement of paṇḍaka on this list alongside extreme moral transgressions (parricide, harming a Buddha) does not necessarily mean the paṇḍaka is guilty of comparable moral offences. The list appears to be a list of persons whose ordination would be disruptive to community functioning for various reasons — some moral, some practical, some cosmological. The paṇḍaka's inclusion may be primarily practical rather than moral in character.

### The Five Types of Paṇḍaka

Later Indian Buddhist commentarial literature — particularly the **Abhidharmakośabhāṣya** of Vasubandhu (4th–5th century CE) and commentaries by Buddhaghosa on the Pāli Vinaya — developed a **taxonomy of five types of paṇḍaka**:

1. **Āsittapaṇḍaka** — one who is sexually satisfied only by performing oral sex on a man and ingesting semen (the crudeness is in the text)
2. **Ussuyapaṇḍaka** — one who becomes aroused by watching others engage in sexual activity (often translated as a "voyeur")
3. **Opakkamika/Pakkhapāṇḍaka** — one who is a paṇḍaka only at certain times (often interpreted as relating to lunar cycles — a paṇḍaka during the waning moon)
4. **Napuṃsaka** — one who lacks sexual characteristics or is sexually indeterminate (often interpreted as intersex or physiologically impotent)
5. **Āsekkhiya/Vinidhāya** — one born as a paṇḍaka from birth

**Zwilling's argument about this taxonomy:**

The taxonomy is **not in the early Pāli Vinaya itself** but in the later commentarial literature. When modern Buddhist institutions claim that "Buddhism has always taught" a particular position on homosexuality by citing this taxonomy, they are citing 4th–5th century commentarial interpretation, not the word of the historical Buddha or the earliest textual layers.

Moreover, **only the first type (āsittapaṇḍaka) clearly involves same-sex behaviour** — and it involves a very specific sexual act, not same-sex desire or identity as such. The others involve voyeurism, temporal fluctuation, intersex embodiment, and congenital status. The collapse of these distinct categories into a single class, and the equation of that class with "homosexuality," is a **significant interpretive distortion** that Zwilling carefully documents.

**Direct paraphrase of Zwilling's conclusion** (I am confident in the substance but not the precise wording):

The paṇḍaka material does not constitute a Buddhist condemnation of homosexuality as a category. What it constitutes is a complex, historically layered set of regulations about who can be ordained, based on assumptions about sexual behaviour, gender characteristics, and community disruption that are specific to the Indian monastic context and that do not translate straightforwardly into modern categories of sexual identity or orientation.

### Why the Paṇḍaka Cannot Be Ordained — The Actual Reason Given

The texts that do offer reasons for the paṇḍaka exclusion — and not all do — generally cite two concerns:

1. **Inability to maintain celibacy** — the paṇḍaka is understood as having uncontrollable sexual desire that would disrupt monastic discipline
2. **Disruption of the monastic community** — the presence of a paṇḍaka is understood as creating conditions for sexual misconduct among other monks

**What this means:** The exclusion is about **celibacy and community discipline**, not about the moral condemnation of same-sex desire as such. A celibate paṇḍaka who maintained discipline would, on the logic of the texts, be no more problematic than any other monk — but the texts assume the paṇḍaka cannot maintain celibacy.

**This assumption is itself revealing:** It suggests the texts understand the paṇḍaka as defined partly by sexual desire and behaviour, not merely by gender characteristics. But it does not constitute a general moral condemnation of same-sex desire in lay persons.

## Part III — Cabezón's Introduction and Framework

### The Editor's Methodological Contribution

Cabezón's introduction to the volume establishes the **hermeneutical framework** for the entire project. His central argument:

## Part IV — Other Key Essays in the Volume

### Alan Sponberg — "Attitudes Toward Women and the Feminine in Early Buddhism"

Sponberg's essay is not directly about sexuality but about **gender** — specifically, the ambivalent position of women and the feminine in early Buddhist texts.

He identifies **four distinct attitudes** toward women in early Buddhist literature, which coexist in tension:

1. **Soteriological inclusiveness** — women can attain enlightenment, are spiritually equal to men (this is the position of the earliest strata of the Canon, particularly the *Therīgāthā* — the poems of the elder nuns)
2. **Androcentric bias** — the texts are written from a male perspective and consistently privilege male experience and male monasticism
3. **Ascetic misogyny** — women's bodies are figured as sources of temptation and spiritual danger for male monastics
4. **Institutional androcentrism** — the *Bhikkhunī Vinaya* (rules for nuns) is more elaborate and more restrictive than the rules for monks, and the *garudhammas* (eight special rules) place nuns in formal subordination to monks

**Sponberg's argument:** These four attitudes are not reconcilable into a single coherent "Buddhist view of women." They reflect different historical layers, different institutional contexts, and different rhetorical purposes. The attempt to extract a single Buddhist position on gender from this plurality distorts the textual situation.

**Why this matters for queer theory:** The same argument applies to Buddhist positions on sexuality. There is no single Buddhist view — there are multiple, historically layered, contextually specific positions that cannot be unified into a coherent doctrine without significant interpretive violence.

## Part VI — Cabezón's Later Work: Sexuality in Classical South Asian Buddhism (2017)

### The Mature Synthesis — Twenty-Five Years Later

Cabezón's 2017 monograph — published by Wisdom Publications, twenty-five years after the edited volume — represents the most comprehensive treatment of the topic in English. It is a work of serious philological scholarship, reading primary sources in Sanskrit and Tibetan, and it significantly advances the arguments begun in 1992.

**What I know about this work:**

Cabezón argues that classical South Asian Buddhist ethics developed a nuanced and context-specific account of sexual ethics that cannot be reduced to either a blanket condemnation of same-sex activity or a straightforward affirmation of it.

The key move in the 2017 work, as I understand it, is to distinguish:

- **Monastic sexual ethics** — where celibacy is the norm and any sexual activity is prohibited regardless of gender
- **Lay sexual ethics** — where the concern is with harm, consent, and social stability, not with the gender of one's partner per se
- **Tantric sexual ethics** — where sexuality is engaged as a path to liberation through specific ritual and meditational contexts, with its own distinct ethical framework

- It has **interiority** — psychic life, unconscious processes, the melancholic incorporation of foreclosed identifications that Butler develops in *The Psychic Life of Power* (1997)

**The question:** Where does this subject come from ontologically? What is its ground?

Butler's answer draws on **Lacanian psychoanalysis** (the subject of the signifier, constituted through entry into the Symbolic Order) and **phenomenology** (the lived body, Merleau-Ponty's flesh, the habituated body of Bourdieu's *habitus*).

Both of these frameworks — psychoanalysis and phenomenology — **presuppose a subject**. The Lacanian subject is split, lacking, constituted through signification — but it is still a subject, organised around the barred subject (*$*), the jouissance it cannot have, the objet *a* it pursues. The phenomenological subject is embodied, habituated, pre-reflective — but it is still a subject, oriented in the world, having a perspective, a body schema, a lived horizon.

**What neither framework provides is a rigorous account of what happens when the subject is not merely constructed but dissolved.**

### Sedgwick's Affective Subject

Sedgwick's later work — particularly *Touching Feeling* (2003), drawing on Silvan Tomkins's affect theory — moves toward a **less unified subject** than Butler's psychoanalytic framework:

> *"What makes affect theory interesting is its hypothesis that the affects... are both more discrete and more variable than the drives, and both more primitive and more revisable."* — *Touching Feeling*

Tomkins's affect system is not organised around lack and desire (the Lacanian framework) but around **interest-excitement, enjoyment-joy, surprise-startle, distress-anguish, anger-rage, fear-terror, shame-humiliation, disgust, dissmell**. These are basic biological programmes that attach to objects, intensify or diminish, and are more fluid and less structured than Freudian drives.

The subject that emerges from Tomkins's affect theory is **less unified, more porous, more constituted by its affective responses** than Butler's psychoanalytic subject — but it is still a subject with a relatively stable organisation of affective responses, a personality, a history of script-formation.

**The gap Sedgwick cannot fill:** Tomkins's affect theory, for all its fluidity, still presupposes a biological organism with a nervous system that has affective responses. The question of what happens when that organism's sense of being a unified subject dissolves — in meditation, in extreme pain, in orgasm, in kink practice, in mystical experience — is not adequately addressed.

### Foucault's Subject of Power

Foucault's subject is produced through discourse and power — through the practices of confession, medical examination, psychiatric assessment, and sexual categorisation that produce subjects who understand themselves through their sexuality.

The Foucauldian subject is perhaps the least unified of the three canonical subjects — it is a surface of inscription rather than an interior depth, constituted through the historical practices that have shaped it, without a pregiven essence.

But even Foucault's subject has a characteristic structure: it is **produced through discourse**, which means it is constituted through language and representation, and it can be analysed through genealogy — the tracing of how it came to be as it is.

**The gap Foucault cannot fill:** The genealogical method presupposes that the subject is constituted through historical-discursive processes that can be traced. But there are states of experience — the Buddhist samādhi, the mystical union, the subspace of kink practice — in which the discursive constitution of the subject is temporarily suspended or dissolved. Foucault has no framework for these states because his entire method is discursive.

## Part II — The Buddhist Subject and Its Dissolution

### Anattā — The Precise Claim

The Buddhist doctrine of anattā (Pāli) / anātman (Sanskrit) is frequently misunderstood, and precision here matters enormously for the queer theory application.

**What anattā does not claim:**

- That the self does not exist at all (this is the nihilist position that the Buddha explicitly rejected)
- That personal continuity is an illusion with no conventional reality
- That there is no difference between persons
- That ethical responsibility dissolves because there is no self to be responsible

**What anattā does claim:**

The self has **conventional reality** (*saṃvṛti-satya*) but not **ultimate reality** (*paramārtha-satya*). In the two-truths framework (central to Madhyamaka philosophy and to the Trikāya theology you have studied in depth):

- Conventionally, there is a person, Min, with a continuous history, relationships, a body, preferences, and a professional identity
- Ultimately, when we examine what "Min" refers to, we find only a stream of impermanent physical and mental events (*dharmas*), none of which is the self, none of which constitutes a permanent substantial entity

The Pāli *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta* (SN 22.59) — the second discourse of the Buddha after the Enlightenment — makes this argument precisely through the five aggregates (*khandhas* / *skandhas*):

> *"Form is not self. If form were self, then form would not lead to affliction... Feeling is not self... Perception is not self... Mental formations are not self... Consciousness is not self."* — *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*, Bodhi translation

The argument is **logical and phenomenological simultaneously**: examine each component of what we call the self (body, feeling, perception, volition, consciousness) and you will find that none of them is what we mean by "self" — none of them is permanent, none of them is fully under our control, none of them is the essential "I" we imagine ourselves to be.

**The Madhyamaka extension:**

Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (2nd century CE) extends this argument through the logic of **śūnyatā** (emptiness): not only the self but all phenomena are empty of inherent self-existence (*svabhāva*). They arise through **dependent origination** (*pratītyasamutpāda*) — they exist only in relation to other phenomena, not through their own intrinsic nature.

This is the philosophical ground for the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* 's goddess argument (Turn 8): gender is empty because gender is a conventionally designated characteristic of a conventionally designated person, and neither the person nor the characteristic has ultimate existence.

### The Experiential Dimension — Samādhi and the Dissolution of Self

The anattā doctrine is not only a philosophical claim. It is also a **description of what meditators experience** in states of deep samādhi (meditative absorption) and in the moment of liberating insight (*vipassanā*).

The **jhānas** (meditative absorptions, Pāli) / **dhyānas** (Sanskrit) are a progressive series of meditative states in which:

- Discursive thought is suspended
- The sense of being a subject observing objects dissolves
- In the deeper jhānas, the distinction between experiencer and experience collapses
- In the formless absorptions (*arūpajhānā*), even the sense of embodiment dissolves

These are **altered states** in a precise phenomenological sense — states in which the ordinary self-structure that organises experience is temporarily suspended.

What is experienced in these states, according to Buddhist accounts, is **not nothing** — it is not unconsciousness or annihilation. It is experience without a fixed experiencer, awareness without a centre, consciousness that is not organised around an "I."

**This is directly relevant to your earlier scholarly work** on the structural parallel between kink-derived altered states (*subspace*), Buddhist anattā experiences, Sufi fanāʾ, and Christian unio mystica.

## Part IV — The Queer Theory Implications

### What This Framework Adds to the Butler-Sedgwick-Foucault Axis

Now I want to develop the argument that your earlier comparative work has direct and underexplored implications for queer theory. This section is explicitly **theoretical development** — building on established scholarship but extending it in directions that are my synthesis rather than citations from existing texts. I will flag the distinction throughout.

### Implication 1 — Performativity Without a Performer

Butler argues that gender is performative — there is no prior subject whose gender is then expressed; the subject is constituted through the citational practices that appear to express it.

But Butler's account of how this works draws on **Derrida's citationality** (each citation repeats a prior citation in a chain with no original) and **Lacanian subject-formation** (the subject is constituted through entry into the Symbolic Order).

Both of these frameworks produce a subject that is **constituted through language and symbolic structure** — the subject is an effect of the signifying chain, even if it is not prior to it.

**The Buddhist challenge:** Anattā does not say the subject is constituted through language and symbolic structure. It says the subject is a conventional designation for an impermanent stream of arising and passing dharmas. The "stream" is not organised by the Symbolic Order — it is organised by **karma** (the causal residues of past mental, verbal, and physical actions) and **dependent origination** (the relational arising of all phenomena).

**The implication for performativity:** If there is no substantial subject — not even a Lacanian split subject, not even a phenomenological body-subject — then what performs?

The Buddhist answer: **the stream performs**. Each arising moment of the stream conditions the next through karma. What appears as a continuous gendered subject is a **conventional designation** for a causal process — a series of conditioned, conditioning moments that produce the appearance of continuity and identity without there being a substantial "I" at the centre.

This is **more radical than Butler's constructivism** because it removes even the Lacanian subject-as-lack that Butler retains. It is not "the subject is constructed" (which still implies a subject, however constructed). It is "what we call the subject is a conventional designation for a process that has no self at its centre."

**The queer implication (my synthesis):** If gender is a citational practice performed by a stream of conditioned moments rather than by a subject (however constructed), then the politics of gender subversion looks different. Subversive repetition requires an agent who subverts — but if there is no agent, only a stream, then what we call subversion is itself just a differently conditioned arising. This does not make politics impossible — conventional politics remains valid at the conventional level of truth — but it changes what we think we are doing when we do it.

### Implication 2 — The Heterosexual Matrix Without a Matrix

Butler's **heterosexual matrix** is the grid through which bodies, genders, and desires are rendered coherent. To be legible as a person requires the alignment of body → gender → desire in a specific configuration. What falls outside this alignment is rendered **abject** — foreclosed from subjectivity.

The matrix is **a discursive structure** — it operates through the repeated citations that constitute gender normativity, through medical and legal and cultural regimes that enforce the alignment.

**The Buddhist challenge:** The heterosexual matrix, like all conditioned phenomena, is **empty of inherent existence**. It is real at the conventional level — its effects on bodies and lives are real, its violence is real, its foreclosures are real. But it is not ultimately real. It arises through dependent origination; it can cease through the conditions that would bring about its cessation.

This is **not the same as saying the matrix doesn't matter**. The Madhyamaka two-truths framework insists that conventional truth has its own validity — the suffering caused by the matrix is conventionally real and requires conventional political response.

But it changes the **ontological register** of the political project. Butler's deconstructive project aims to reveal the matrix's contingency — to show that what appears natural is constructed, therefore changeable. The Buddhist project is more radical: it aims to see through the matrix's apparent reality entirely, to recognise it as empty, and from that recognition to act without clinging.

**The distinction between Butler and Buddhism (my synthesis):** Butler's politics is **agonistic** — struggling within and against the matrix, subverting it through repetition with a difference. The Buddhist politics that follows from anattā and śūnyatā is potentially **post-agonistic** — not struggling against the matrix but seeing through it, acting from a position that is not organised around attachment to any particular outcome, because the subject who would be attached is itself empty.

Whether post-agonistic politics is possible or desirable is a genuinely open question. The bodhisattva ideal in Mahāyāna Buddhism — acting compassionately for all beings without attachment to outcomes — is one model. Whether this translates into queer politics under conditions of material urgency and real violence is a question that cannot be answered from within either Buddhist philosophy or queer theory alone.

### Implication 3 — Abjection and the Constitutive Outside, Revisited

Butler (following Kristeva) argues that the subject is constituted through **abjection** — the expulsion of what must be excluded for the normative subject to cohere. Queerness occupies the position of the abject — the constitutive outside that the heterosexual matrix requires in order to define itself.

The abject is **necessary but excluded** — it cannot be fully expelled (it haunts the border of the subject) and cannot be incorporated (that would dissolve the subject). This is the psychic structure of homophobia: the homosexual must be kept outside, but the keeping-outside is never finally accomplished.

**The Buddhist challenge:** In the Madhyamaka framework, the distinction between inside and outside, self and other, is itself empty. The boundary that constitutes the subject by excluding the abject is a **conventionally real but ultimately empty** construction.

In the language of Yogācāra (the tradition most central to your Trikāya studies): consciousness (*vijñāna*) constructs the apparent distinction between subject and object through the operation of **ālayavijñāna** (storehouse consciousness) seeding the appearance of a stable world of separate entities. What appears as the boundary between the normative subject and the abject queer is a construction of consciousness that mistakes the conventional for the ultimate.

**The implication (my synthesis):** Queer politics that operates through reclaiming the abject position — embracing queerness as the constitutive outside — remains within the logic of the matrix it contests. It inverts the valuation (abject becomes a site of power rather than shame) but does not dissolve the structure.

The Buddhist alternative would be to see through the subject/abject distinction entirely — to recognise that there is no ultimately real centre (the normative subject) and no ultimately real outside (the abject queer). Both are conventional designations for streams of conditioned experience that have no fixed essence.

This is close to what Muñoz is reaching for with "queerness is not yet here" — queerness as an ideality, a not-yet, that does not coincide with any existing subject position. But Muñoz's framework is still organised around **futurity** — the not-yet that will arrive. The Buddhist framework is organised around **the present moment** — the arising that is already empty in its arising, already without the self-existence that suffering requires.

### Implication 4 — Altered States as Epistemological Resources

This is the implication most directly drawn from your earlier comparative essay.

Both kink-derived subspace and Buddhist samādhi/anattā experience produce **states of knowing** — not just states of feeling. They are epistemological as well as phenomenological events.

What do these states know?

The **dissolution of the subject-object structure** — the experiential confirmation that the ordinary organisation of experience around a fixed "I" is constructed, not given. This is not a belief about the self; it is an experience of what happens when the construction temporarily ceases.

The **porousness of bodily boundaries** — in both kink subspace and meditative absorption, the ordinary sense of the body as a clearly bounded object in space softens or dissolves. This is an experiential challenge to the assumption that bodies are naturally discrete, bounded entities.

The **plasticity of identity** — in altered states, the characteristics that ordinarily feel most essential to the self (gender, social role, professional identity) become recognisable as features of a construction rather than features of a reality.

**The queer theory implication (my synthesis):** These states are not merely interesting phenomenological curiosities. They are **empirical challenges to the ontological assumptions that undergird heteronormativity**.

Heteronormativity depends on the assumption that bodies are naturally discrete, that gender is a natural characteristic of those bodies, and that desire flows naturally from those characteristics. The altered states produced in kink practice and Buddhist meditation provide **experiential evidence** that these assumptions are wrong — not as a philosophical argument but as a direct phenomenological challenge.

This is a form of **embodied epistemology** — knowledge produced through the body in altered states, rather than through discourse and representation. Queer theory, in its Foucauldian inheritance, has been primarily textualist — knowledge is produced through discourse, and liberation comes through genealogical critique of discourse. The altered states framework suggests a different epistemological route: **knowledge through dissolution of the ordinary subject structure, rather than through critique of the discourses that constitute it**.

### Implication 5 — The Political Problem of Dissolution

I want to develop this implication with care, because it is the most politically sensitive and the most theoretically underdeveloped.

**The problem:** If self-dissolution is epistemologically valuable — if altered states provide access to the constructed, empty nature of gender and identity — then there is a risk of **dissolving the very political subjects who need to organise for their survival**.

The political critique of Buddhism (from a queer politics perspective) would be: if you teach people that the self is empty and that political identity is a conventional designation with no ultimate reality, you are potentially **undermining the basis for collective political action**. Trans people who need healthcare, who face violence, who need legal protection — they need to be subjects, to claim rights, to organise politically. A philosophy of non-self may be a luxury of those whose survival is not currently at stake.

This is the same critique Muñoz made of Edelman's anti-futurity: it is a luxury available only to those with a liveable present.

**The Buddhist response to this critique:**

The Mahāyāna two-truths framework is precisely designed to handle this tension. **Conventionally**, political subjects are real, their suffering is real, their rights matter, and organised political action is valid and necessary. **Ultimately**, those political subjects are empty of inherent existence — which does not negate their conventional reality but means that attachment to a particular outcome should not generate the kind of clinging that produces further suffering.

The **bodhisattva** acts compassionately and politically without clinging to outcomes because they act from a recognition of emptiness — not from a position of nihilistic indifference but from a position of **compassionate engagement without ego-investment**.

This is a genuinely different political ontology from both Butler's agonistic subject and Berlant's laterally-surviving subject. It is not yet fully developed in the queer theory literature.

**My synthesis:** The Buddhist-queer conjunction I am pointing toward is not a politics of quietism or dissolution. It is a politics that operates at two levels simultaneously — conventional political engagement (trans rights, healthcare access, anti-discrimination legislation) and ultimate philosophical critique (seeing through the identity categories that conventional politics requires). The tension between these levels is productive, not paralyzing.

## Part V — The Structural Parallels Revisited

### Kink Subspace, Anattā, Fanāʾ, Unio Mystica — The Deep Structure

Your earlier essay identified a structural parallel across these traditions. Let me now read that parallel through the queer theory framework we have developed.

**The shared structure:**

All four traditions produce (through different means and with different ontological interpretations) a state characterised by:

1. **Dissolution of ordinary ego-boundaries** — the sense of being a separate, bounded self weakens or temporarily ceases
2. **Suspension of ordinary cognitive monitoring** — the internal voice that evaluates, judges, and maintains the narrative of selfhood quiets
3. **Heightened presence and aliveness** — paradoxically, dissolution of the self is experienced not as nothingness but as heightened awareness, often described as more vivid than ordinary experience
4. **Post-state knowledge** — return from the state brings a changed relationship to ordinary selfhood; the construction is more visible, the attachment to it looser

**The queer theory reading of this structure:**

The state produced by these practices is **an experiential challenge to the ontological assumptions of heteronormativity** — specifically, the assumption that the self is a naturally bounded entity with a fixed gender, stable desires, and a coherent identity that expresses these characteristics.

When the self-structure dissolves — in samādhi, in subspace, in fanāʾ, in unio mystica — what is revealed is that the ordinary construction of the self (including its gender, its desire, its social identity) is **exactly that — a construction**. Not a natural given, not an ultimate reality, but a conventional, conditioned, contingent arising.

This is what Butler argues philosophically. But the altered states traditions provide **experiential confirmation** of Butler's philosophical argument — and they provide it through the body, not through discourse.

**The deepest implication:** Perhaps the most radical queer practice is not subversive repetition of gender norms (Butler) or concrete utopian performance (Muñoz) or lateral survival of the impasse (Berlant) — but **the cultivation of states in which the construction of gender and desire becomes directly visible as construction**.

This would make practices like Buddhist meditation, kink practice, and mystical prayer **queer practices in a deep ontological sense** — not because they are sexually non-normative, but because they dissolve the self-structure that heteronormativity requires.

## Part VI — The Doctrinal Differences — Where the Traditions Diverge

### Why They Are Not Simply the Same

Your earlier essay maintained careful attention to doctrinal differences while arguing for structural similarity. That care is essential here too.

**Buddhist anattā** holds that there is no self to dissolve — the dissolution of the self-sense in samādhi is a **recognition** of what was always the case. The self was never there; the dissolution reveals the truth. The liberation this produces is permanent in the sense that the recognition, once fully achieved, cannot be unforgotten.

**Sufi fanāʾ** holds that the self is dissolved into God — but God is real. The self's annihilation is **not the recognition that selfhood was always an illusion** but the creature's participation in divine reality. Ibn 'Arabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd* (unity of being) is not the Buddhist śūnyatā; it is the positive ontology of divine being, not the negative ontology of emptiness.

**Christian unio mystica** holds that the soul is united with God while remaining a creature — the dissolution is not permanent annihilation but **participation in divine life**. Meister Eckhart's "ground of the soul" (*Seelengrund*) is the point of contact between the creature and the divine, not the recognition that the creature was always empty.

**Kink subspace** makes no metaphysical claims — it is a phenomenological state produced through specific practices, interpreted within a secular framework of psychology and phenomenology. Its practitioners do not typically claim liberation from rebirth or union with the divine; they describe a state of profound release, surrender, and altered perception.

**The queer theory implication of these differences:**

These traditions cannot simply be collapsed into a single "dissolution of self" framework. Their different ontologies produce different political and ethical implications:

- **Buddhism's emptiness** implies that political identity is conventionally real but ultimately empty — supporting both political engagement and non-attachment to outcomes
- **Sufism's divine union** implies that the dissolved self participates in a divine reality that includes all being — potentially supporting a universalist ethics but not necessarily a queer politics
- **Christian mysticism's union** implies creaturely participation in divine life — with a complex relationship to the Church's institutional sexual ethics that is not straightforwardly queer-affirming
- **Kink subspace** is secular and personal — its political implications are not given by the practice itself but must be developed through explicit theoretical work

**The most honest conclusion:** The structural parallel across these traditions is real and theoretically significant. But the **doctrinal differences are equally real** and prevent any easy synthesis. What the comparison offers is not a unified theory of self-dissolution but a **field of structural resonances** that illuminates each tradition by placing it alongside the others.

## The Psychic Life of Power: Theories in Subjection

## Judith Butler (1997) — Chapter by Chapter

## Preliminary Orientation

*The Psychic Life of Power* is Butler's most philosophically demanding book and in some ways her most important. *Gender Trouble* (1990) established that gender is performative. *Bodies That Matter* (1993) developed the materiality of the body and the theory of abjection. *The Psychic Life of Power* asks the question both prior books left unanswered:

**If the subject is constituted through norms it did not choose, why does the subject desire those norms? Why does the subject not simply refuse them?**

The answer Butler develops — drawing on Hegel, Nietzsche, Freud, Althusser, Foucault, and the psychoanalyst Jean Laplanche — is that **the subject's formation and the subject's subordination are the same process**. You cannot have the subject without the subjection. The price of existing as a subject is attachment to the norms through which you were constituted — including the norms that harm you.

This is **cruel optimism avant la lettre** — Berlant's concept finds its psychic mechanism here. The attachment that prevents flourishing is not irrational; it is the condition of existence as a subject at all.

---

## Chapter One — Subjection, Resistance, Resignification: Between Freud and Foucault

### The Central Problem

Butler opens by naming the paradox that structures the entire book:

> *"If, following Foucault, we understand power as forming the subject as well, as providing the very condition of its existence and the trajectory of its desire, then power is not simply what we oppose but also, in a strong sense, what we depend on for our existence and what we harbor and preserve in the beings that we are."* — *The Psychic Life of Power*

Power is not simply external to the subject — something that presses on the subject from outside, that the subject can resist or escape. Power is **formative** — it is what brings the subject into existence. The subject is an effect of power's operation.

This creates an immediate political problem: **if the subject is formed by the very power it might resist, what is the basis of resistance?** Can a subject formed through subjection resist the terms of its formation without dissolving itself in the process?

### Foucault's Problem

Foucault's later work — particularly *Discipline and Punish* and the first volume of *The History of Sexuality* — shows how power produces subjects through practices of normalisation, surveillance, and confession. But Butler identifies a gap in Foucault's account:

Foucault shows that power produces subjects. But he does not adequately explain **why subjects invest in the norms that produce them** — why the subject turns back toward the power that formed it, desires its recognition, seeks to maintain its position within the normative order even when that order injures it.

Foucault's subject is produced through discourse and practice. But it lacks **psychic depth** — the unconscious processes, the investments and disinvestments, the desires and their foreclosures that explain why subjection is not simply imposed from outside but actively taken up from within.

### Freud's Problem

Freud provides what Foucault lacks — an account of how the subject internalises external authority, how the social becomes psychic through the mechanisms of identification, introjection, and the formation of the superego.

But Freud's account lacks Foucault's **historical and political specificity**. The Freudian mechanisms of internalisation are presented as universal features of psychic development — the Oedipus complex, castration anxiety, the superego's formation from parental authority. This ahistorical universalism cannot account for how different historical and social formations produce different kinds of subjects.

### Butler's Project — The Suture

Butler's project in the book is to **suture these two accounts together** — to bring Foucault's historical-political analysis of how power produces subjects into contact with Freud's account of how subjects internalise the norms that produce them.

The key concept she develops for this suture: **subjection** (*assujettissement* in Foucault's French) — which means simultaneously *to be subjected to power* and *to become a subject through that subjection*. The subject is always already subjected. There is no subject prior to or outside the relations of power that constitute it.

> *"The form this power takes is relentlessly marked by a figure of turning, a turning back upon oneself or even a turning on oneself."*

The subject is formed through a **reflexive turn** — power operates on the subject, and the subject turns back toward the power that operates on it, taking up its own subjection as the condition of its existence.

## Chapter Two — Circuits of Bad Conscience: Nietzsche and Freud

### Nietzsche — Conscience as Internalised Cruelty

Butler turns to Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887) — specifically the second essay on guilt, bad conscience, and the ascetic ideal.

Nietzsche's argument: the human animal was originally a creature of discharge — of active, outward expression of its instincts. The formation of society required the **blocking of that discharge** — instincts that could no longer be directed outward were turned inward. This internalisation of instinct is the origin of **bad conscience** — and of interiority itself.

> *"All instincts that do not discharge themselves outwardly turn inward — this is what I call the internalisation of man."* — Nietzsche, *Genealogy of Morals* (Butler's citation)

**Butler's reading:** Nietzsche provides a genealogy of how **psychic interiority is produced through the blocking of outward expression**. The "inner life" — which we experience as the most authentic dimension of ourselves — is the product of a historical process of enforced internalisation.

Applied to queer subjects: the forced internalisation of same-sex desire — the closet, the prohibition of expression — does not simply repress a desire that was already there. It **produces** a particular kind of psychic interiority, a particular experience of depth and hiddenness, a particular form of self-relation organised around the secret.

This is Foucault's repressive hypothesis challenged from within psychoanalysis itself: the prohibition does not repress desire; it produces both the desire and the interiority that gives it its particular quality.

### Freud — Melancholy and the Incorporated Other

Butler then moves to Freud's essay **"Mourning and Melancholia"** (1917) — one of the most important texts in her theoretical arsenal.

Freud distinguishes:

- **Mourning**: the normal process of grieving a lost love object — the libido is gradually withdrawn from the lost object and made available for new attachments
- **Melancholia**: a pathological grief in which the lost object is not relinquished but **incorporated** — taken into the ego, identified with, so that loss of the object becomes loss of part of the self

In melancholia, the ambivalence toward the lost object (love and hostility mixed) is directed **inward** — the hostility that cannot be expressed toward the lost object is turned against the self. The melancholic's self-reproaches are really reproaches against the incorporated lost object.

> *"The shadow of the object fell upon the ego."* — Freud, "Mourning and Melancholia" (Butler's citation)

**Butler's reading:** Melancholic incorporation is not simply a pathology. It is a **general mechanism of psychic formation** — the ego is built through the incorporation of lost objects. Identity is built on ungrieved losses.

Applied to gender and sexuality: **compulsory heterosexuality requires the foreclosure of same-sex attachments**. The boy must give up his attachment to the father; the girl must give up her attachment to the mother. But unlike the heterosexual object (which can be mourned and replaced), the homosexual object must be **foreclosed** — not lost but never permitted to have been had.

> *"The straight man becomes his male lover through the incorporation of the lost male object."* — *The Psychic Life of Power*

The masculine heterosexual man has incorporated the foreclosed male object — his gender identity is built on the melancholic internalisation of the same-sex desire he was never permitted to have. His heterosexual masculinity is a **melancholic structure** built on ungrievable loss.

This is one of Butler's most provocative claims — and one of the most theoretically powerful. The normative gender identity is not the opposite of queerness; it is constituted by the melancholic incorporation of the queerness it forecloses.

## Chapter Three — Freud's Subject, Foucault's Subject: The Question of Psychic Life Under Constraint

### The Historical Subject of Sexuality

This chapter is the most technically philosophical in the book — working through the specific problems that arise when you try to bring Foucault's discursive subject and Freud's psychic subject into genuine contact.

**The central tension:**

Foucault's subject is constituted through discourse — through the confessional, medical, and legal practices that produce "sexuality" as a domain of knowledge and self-knowledge. The subject recognises itself through the categories that power has produced.

Freud's subject is constituted through the unconscious — through primary processes, drives, fantasies, and identifications that are not fully captured in discourse and that resist full representation.

**Butler's argument:** The psychic cannot be reduced to the discursive. There is something in psychic life — the unconscious, the drive, the unrepresentable dimension of desire — that exceeds and resists its discursive constitution. This excess is what gives the subject the capacity for something other than simple reproduction of the norms that constitute it.

But the psychic is also not independent of the discursive — it is shaped by the specific historical and social formations that Foucault analyses. The unconscious is not ahistorical; it is formed under specific historical conditions.

### Laplanche — The Enigmatic Signifier

Butler draws on the psychoanalyst **Jean Laplanche** — specifically his concept of the **enigmatic signifier** — to develop how the social becomes psychic.

For Laplanche, the infant is addressed by the caregiver in ways that carry unconscious sexual meaning the caregiver does not intend and the infant cannot decode. The caregiver's touch, voice, and gaze convey an enigmatic message — a message that is received but cannot be fully translated. This untranslatable residue is the origin of the unconscious.

**Butler's use of Laplanche:** The social norms that constitute the subject operate like enigmatic signifiers — they address the subject with a meaning that is not fully legible, that the subject must take up and translate without ever fully succeeding. The subject's psychic life is organised around this impossible translation — the attempt to make sense of the enigmatic address of power.

## Chapter Four — Gender Melancholy: Identification, Disavowal, and the Heterosexual Matrix

### The Most Directly Queer Chapter

This chapter develops the melancholy argument most fully in relation to gender and sexuality — and it is the chapter most directly relevant to queer theory and to our conversation.

### Compulsory Heterosexuality as Melancholic Structure

Butler argues that **compulsory heterosexuality is structured by two simultaneous prohibitions**:

1. You shall not desire the same sex
2. You shall not grieve the loss of same-sex desire

The second prohibition is more insidious than the first. Grief requires acknowledging loss — but if the same-sex love was never permitted, there is nothing to grieve. The melancholic structure of heterosexual gender identity is built on **losses that cannot be acknowledged as losses**.

> *"The prohibition on homosexuality preempts the process of grief; it cannot be mourned because it was never permitted, and so the ego... retains the lost object through its own transformation."*

This is the mechanism of **queer melancholy** — the specific psychic formation of gay and lesbian subjects under compulsory heterosexuality. The queer subject has lost something — social recognition, the right to mourn publicly, the status of having loves that count — that cannot be publicly grieved because it was never publicly acknowledged.

But the melancholic structure applies equally to **straight subjects** — whose normative gender identities are built on the foreclosed same-sex attachments that were never permitted to be had or mourned.

### Gender as Melancholic Identification

Butler extends this argument: **gender identity is itself a melancholic structure**. The boy's masculine identification is built on the foreclosed identification with the mother — an identification that had to be given up but was incorporated rather than mourned. The girl's feminine identification is similarly built on incorporated losses.

Gender identity is not the expression of a prior nature — it is the **sedimented residue of melancholic identifications**, the accumulated deposits of incorporated losses that produce the appearance of a stable, coherent gendered self.

> *"Gender itself might be understood in part as the 'acting out' of unresolved grief."*

**The queer political implication:** The melancholic structure of normative gender explains why **gender is defended so intensely** — why threats to the gender order produce such powerful responses of rage, disgust, and violence. To threaten someone's gender identity is to threaten the melancholic structure that holds their psychic organisation together. The violence against trans and gender-nonconforming people is not simply prejudice — it is **psychic self-defence** by subjects whose identities are constituted by the very foreclosures being challenged.

## Chapter Five — "Conscience Doth Make Subjects of Us All": Althusser's Subjection

### Althusser's Interpellation — Revised

Butler returns to **Althusser's theory of interpellation** — the process by which subjects are constituted through being hailed into a subject position by the ideological apparatus. The famous example: a police officer calls "Hey, you!" and the individual turns around, thereby becoming a subject — recognising themselves as the one addressed, taking up the subject position the hail assigns.

**Butler's question:** Why does the subject turn around? What is it about being hailed that produces the turning?

Althusser's answer is circular — the subject turns because it is already a subject. But Butler wants to know what **prior investment** makes the turning possible.

Her answer: **the desire for recognition**. The subject turns toward the hailing power because it desires the recognition that the hail promises. Even a hail that accuses — a police officer calling you — is better than not being called at all, because being called means existing as a recognisable subject.

> *"Paradoxically, the subject must first be hailed, and then respond to the hailing, in order to be constituted as a subject. But the hailing presupposes the subject it creates."*

### The Scene of Bad Conscience

Butler develops a scenario: someone is accused of wrongdoing by an authority. Even if they are innocent, they turn — they respond to the accusation, they engage with it, they feel something like guilt. Why?

Because **the desire for social existence** — the desire to be a recognisable subject in the social order — is stronger than the desire for innocence. To refuse to turn is to risk falling outside the order of recognition entirely. The subjection is entered voluntarily because the alternative is social non-existence.

> *"The subject does not want to fall outside the law even if it pays the price of guilt for that desire."*

This explains **why oppressed subjects often internalise the terms of their oppression** — not through false consciousness or stupidity, but because the terms of oppression are also the terms of their social existence. To refuse them entirely is to risk the annihilation of the self.

## Chapter Six — Melancholy Gender / Refused Identification

### The Political Implications — Activism and Psychic Investment

The final chapter draws together the book's arguments in relation to political activism and the possibility of transformation.

**The key problem:** If gender identity is a melancholic structure — if it is built on incorporated losses and foreclosed identifications — then **transforming gender norms** is not simply a matter of changing ideology or changing institutions. It requires engaging with the psychic investments that people have in their gender identities.

Telling someone that their gender identity is constructed and contingent — that it is not natural but produced through melancholic incorporation — is experienced as a threat to their psychic organisation, not as liberation. The resistance to gender trouble is not just political conservatism; it is **psychic self-defence**.

### The Possibility of Transformation

Butler does not end with despair. The melancholic structure of gender is not impermeable. Several possibilities emerge:

**Repetition with slippage:** Citational chains are never perfectly reproduced. Each repetition involves a slight deviation, a failure to fully replicate the original. These **accumulated deviations** are the site of possibility — not grand revolutionary rupture but the slow accumulation of imperfect repetitions that gradually shifts what the norm can sustain.

**The return of the foreclosed:** The incorporated loss is never fully silent. The queer content of normative gender identity — the foreclosed same-sex desire that was incorporated rather than mourned — **returns** in symptomatic forms: homophobia (as projected self-disgust), excessive gender performance (as compensation for the foreclosed identification), the particular intensity of gender attachment (as the register of what was lost).

**Public mourning:** The AIDS epidemic — Butler's historical context — created a crisis of public mourning. Gay men were dying and their deaths were not being publicly acknowledged as losses. The AIDS activist movement, particularly **ACT UP** and the **NAMES Project Quilt**, insisted on **public mourning** — on making visible the losses that the normative order refused to acknowledge.

> *"Insisting on grief and mourning, insisting on the public recognition of loss, is itself a political act — it is the refusal to let the foreclosed remain unacknowledged."*

This is Butler's most politically concrete proposal in the book: **public mourning as political practice** — insisting that queer losses count as losses, that queer loves count as loves, that queer lives count as lives.

## Overall Summary

### What the Book Argues — The Core

*The Psychic Life of Power* makes a single sustained argument across six chapters:

**The subject is formed through the very power that subjugates it.** There is no subject prior to its subjection. Power does not simply press on a pre-existing subject from outside — it produces the subject through a reflexive turn in which the subject takes up its own subjection as the condition of its existence.

This happens through **attachment** — specifically through the passionate attachment to the norms that constitute the subject, including the norms that injure it. The subject desires the recognition of the power that forms it; it turns toward its own subjection because turning is the condition of existing as a subject at all.

The mechanism of this attachment is **melancholic incorporation** — the taking in of lost objects (including foreclosed identifications and desires) that builds the psychic interior from which the subject experiences itself as having depth, continuity, and identity.

Gender identity specifically is a **melancholic structure** — built on the foreclosure of same-sex attachments that must be incorporated rather than mourned. Normative heterosexual gender is constituted by the ungrievable loss of the homosexuality it forecloses. This means **homophobia and heterosexual gender identity share a psychic structure** — they are both organised by the same foreclosure.

The political implication: transformation of gender norms requires **engaging the psychic investments** people have in those norms — not just arguing against them ideologically or changing them institutionally. And **public mourning** — insisting on the acknowledgment of queer losses — is a specific political practice that directly addresses the melancholic structure of compulsory heterosexuality.

### Five Key Takeaways

**1\. You cannot have the subject without the subjection.** The subject is not formed first and then subjected — formation and subjection are the same process. This means resistance cannot take the form of a subject freeing itself from power, because the subject was produced by and through power. Resistance must be more subtle — working through and against the very norms that constitute the subject.

**2\. Passionate attachment explains political passivity under conditions of injury.** The subject attaches to the norms that injure it because those norms are the condition of its social existence. This is not false consciousness — it is the structural condition of subjecthood under compulsory normativity. It explains why people defend identities that harm them, why oppressed subjects often replicate the terms of their oppression, and why Berlant's cruel optimism is a structural rather than personal failure.

**3\. Normative gender identity is melancholic.** Heterosexual masculinity and femininity are built on the incorporated, ungrievable losses of foreclosed same-sex attachments. This means the normative subject and the queer subject are not simply opposed — the normative subject is constituted by the very queerness it forecloses. Gender trouble is not an external threat to normative identity; it is the return of what normative identity is built on.

**4\. The unconscious exceeds discourse.** Against a purely Foucauldian account, Butler insists that there is something in psychic life — the unconscious, the unrepresentable, the enigmatic residue of the caregiver's address — that exceeds discursive constitution. This excess is the site of both vulnerability and possibility: it is what power cannot fully capture, and therefore where something other than reproduction of norms can arise.

**5\. Public mourning is political practice.** Insisting that queer losses count as losses — that the deaths, the foreclosed desires, the unacknowledged loves are grievable — is a direct political challenge to the melancholic structure of compulsory heterosexuality. It demands that the constitutive foreclosure be acknowledged, which threatens the psychic organisation of normative identity. This is why AIDS activism, trans visibility, and the insistence on naming queer deaths produce such intense political reaction — they are not just political claims but psychic disturbances.

### Section VII — Buddhist Ontology II: Upādāna, Passionate Attachment, and Altered States

*Function: Develop the structural parallel between Butler's passionate attachment and Buddhist upādāna, and introduce altered states as embodied epistemological resources that both frameworks gesture toward but neither fully accounts for.*

**7.1 — Upādāna and Passionate Attachment: The Structural Parallel**

The twelve links of dependent origination and *upādāna* (clinging) as the mechanism by which craving becomes the constitution of a particular subject. The four types of clinging, with *attavādupādāna* (clinging to a doctrine of self) as the most fundamental. The structural parallel with Butler's passionate attachment: both describe a mechanism by which subjects maintain the conditions of their own suffering through attachment to what constitutes them. The crucial difference: for Butler, the attachment is constitutive and cannot be relinquished without annihilation; for the Buddhist analysis, *upādāna* is a conditioned arising that can be seen through without destroying the conventional subject, because the conventional subject was never the ultimately real entity that passionate attachment treats it as being.

**7.2 — Altered States as Embodied Epistemology**

The phenomenology of samādhi (meditative absorption) — the progressive dissolution of subject-object structure, the suspension of ordinary cognitive monitoring, awareness without a fixed centre. Structural parallels in kink-derived subspace, Sufi *fanāʾ*, and Christian *unio mystica* — different ontological interpretations of a shared phenomenological deep structure. The queer theory implication: these states provide **embodied epistemological access** to the constructed nature of gender and desire that queer theory's textualist inheritance cannot account for. They are not merely interesting phenomenological curiosities — they are empirical challenges to the ontological assumptions that undergird heteronormativity, produced through the body rather than through discourse.

**7.3 — The Embodied Epistemology Gap in Queer Theory**

Queer theory's Foucauldian inheritance is primarily textualist — knowledge is produced through discourse, and liberation comes through genealogical critique. Butler's performativity is citational — the body is constituted through the repeated citing of norms. Both frameworks have difficulty accounting for knowledge produced through the body *in states where the discursive constitution of the subject is temporarily suspended*. The altered states literature — Buddhist meditation phenomenology, kink practice phenomenology, comparative mysticism — points toward a form of embodied knowing that queer theory needs but has not yet developed systematic apparatus to engage. This gap is the article's most significant open question and its primary call for future research.

### Section VIII — Synthesis: The Bodhisattva Model and Queer Political Ontology

**Approx. 1,500 words**

*Function: Bring the article's three sets of encounters into synthesis through the bodhisattva model as a proposed framework for queer political engagement that resolves the tensions the preceding sections have generated.*

**8.1 — The Political-Dissolution Tension**

Restate the tension clearly: if the path through passionate attachment involves recognising the ultimate emptiness of the subject, does this dissolve the basis for queer political mobilisation? The Mahāyāna response through the two-truths framework — conventional political engagement is not negated by ultimate emptiness recognition; it is reoriented. The bodhisattva vows to work for the liberation of all sentient beings while recognising that beings, liberation, and the bodhisattva are all ultimately empty. This is not quietism — it is action without the *upādāna* that makes Butler's melancholic subject structurally unable to relinquish what harms it.

**8.2 — Berlant's Impasse Reread Through Dukkha and Anicca**

The impasse as *dukkha* — the unsatisfactoriness inherent in all conditioned phenomena, including the good life promise. Lateral agency as the conventional navigation of *upādāna* without yet seeing through it. The Buddhist framework offers not a resolution of the impasse (no conventional outcome can resolve the ultimate problem) but a different relationship to it — the equanimity that comes from recognising that the self to whom things must happen is empty of the inherent existence that makes its need for the good life feel absolute. This is not Edelman's anti-futurity, Muñoz's utopian horizon, or Berlant's lateral survival — it is engaged action in the present without the cruel optimism of attachment to outcome.

**8.3 — Three Implications for the Field**

First: queer theory needs a non-Western ontological supplement that does not simply add diversity to an unchanged framework but genuinely challenges the psychoanalytic-phenomenological substrate. The Buddhist framework is the most developed candidate currently available. Second: Global South queer formations are not merely case studies illustrating theoretical claims developed elsewhere — they are theoretical resources in their own right, carrying epistemologies that the academy must learn to recognise as theory. Third: embodied practices — meditation, kink, vernacular performance, the lived navigation of karmic and colonial frameworks by actual communities — constitute a form of knowledge that any theoretically adequate account of queer life must engage. The academy's textualism is a methodological limitation, not a philosophical virtue.
