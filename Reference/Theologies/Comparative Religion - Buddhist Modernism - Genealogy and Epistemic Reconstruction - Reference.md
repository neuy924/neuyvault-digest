## Abstract (English)

This essay undertakes a systematic critical survey of the epistemological foundations of Buddhist Studies and contemporary Buddhist practice, tracing a movement from genealogical critique through canon analysis to constructive reconstruction. Beginning with David McMahan's genealogy of Buddhist modernism, the discussion establishes that what passes as "Buddhism" in both Western convert communities and Asian reformist movements is a historically contingent hybrid produced through encounter with Protestant Christianity, Enlightenment rationalism, and Romantic expressivism — not a recovery of ancient Dharma. Gregory Schopen's archaeological and epigraphic critique is then mobilised to show that the textual-doctrinal "original Buddhism" privileged by Western philology reflects a Protestant epistemological inheritance rather than historical practice, which was overwhelmingly material, ritual, and institutional. Robert Sharf's critique of the "rhetoric of meditative experience" further demonstrates that meditators' reports are institutionally scripted rather than phenomenologically transparent, and that the meditation-centred model of Buddhism is itself a modern, largely colonial-era innovation.

The critical apparatus is then extended to the Taishō Shinshū Daizōkyō, showing that this foundational dataset of East Asian Buddhist Studies carries Meiji nationalist, pan-Asianist, and Protestant-philological freight that has been naturalised through CBETA digitisation into a globally authoritative but ideologically opaque corpus. The genealogy of meditation-centred Buddhism is traced from Ledi Sayadaw's colonial-crisis theology through Mahasi Sayadaw's noting technique, the American Insight Meditation movement, and Jon Kabat-Zinn's clinical secularisation, demonstrating that contemporary mindfulness has identifiable inventors, contingent institutional histories, and systematic suppressions of ethical, communal, and soteriological dimensions. Natalie Gummer's performative theory of sūtra composition and Luis Gómez's visionary-samādhi hypothesis are then examined as correctives to Schopen's privileging of epigraphy over text, arguing that Mahāyāna sūtras are best understood as ritual performance traditions and practice records rather than doctrinal treatises, and that their composition was generatively linked to meditatively induced visionary states.

The final movement turns constructive. Drawing on Georges Dreyfus's reconstruction of Dharmakīrti's pramāṇa epistemology, Evan Thompson's neurophenomenological programme, Bhikkhu Analayo's phenomenological philology, Jay Garfield's Madhyamaka, and Śāntideva's ethics of no-self, the essay argues that a post-critical Buddhist epistemology is both possible and necessary. What survives the critique: a naturalist, internally self-critical epistemological framework (pramāṇa); a carefully delimited phenomenological programme subject to mutual constraint with cognitive science; the structural claim of dependent origination as an independently evaluable account of causality; the no-self thesis in phenomenological and ethical rather than metaphysical registers; and an ethics grounded in the reduction of suffering without metaphysical foundations. What does not survive: perennialism, textual essentialism, experiential foundationalism, cosmological literalism, and the authority of lineage as such. The residual tension between constructivist ceiling and transformative claim is identified as productive rather than paralysing — constituting, in a recognisably Buddhist register, a contemporary formulation of the Middle Way between essentialism and nihilism.

最後一部分轉向建設性重構。本文借助德萊福斯（Georges Dreyfus）對法稱（Dharmakīrti）量論（pramāṇa）的重建、湯普森（Evan Thompson）的神經現象學方案、阿那羅比丘（Bhikkhu Analayo）的現象學文獻學、加菲爾德（Jay Garfield）的中觀哲學，以及寂天（Śāntideva）的無我倫理學，論證一種後批判的佛教知識論不僅可能，且為必要。批判之後仍能成立的內容包括：一種自然主義的、具有內在自我批判性的認識論框架（量論）；一項受認知科學相互制約的、謹慎界定的現象學綱領；緣起論作為獨立可評估的因果性說明；在現象學與倫理學（而非形上學）層面上的無我論題；以及一種無需形上學基礎、以減少苦難為指向的倫理學。不能成立的內容則包括：永恆哲學、文本本質主義、體驗基礎主義、宇宙論字面主義，以及宗派傳承本身的認識論權威性。建構主義上限與轉化性主張之間的殘餘張力被界定為具有生產性而非令人癱瘓的——這在一種可辨識的佛教語境中，構成了關於中道（避免本質主義與虛無主義之間極端）的當代表述。

## I. David McMahan — *The Making of Buddhist Modernism* (2008)

### Core Argument

McMahan's thesis is that what passes today as "Buddhism" — particularly in Western convert communities but also in Asian reformist movements — is not a retrieval of ancient Dharma but a **historically contingent hybrid** produced through the encounter of Asian Buddhist traditions with three dominant strands of Western modernity: **Protestant Christianity, Enlightenment rationalism, and Romantic expressivism**.

This hybrid, which he calls *Buddhist modernism*, is characterised by:

- **Demythologisation**: stripping cosmological and ritualist elements (hungry ghosts, merit-transfer, tantric deity yoga) in favour of a rationalised, ethicised core
- **Psychologisation**: relocating Buddhist soteriology inward, making meditation a technology of the self rather than a cosmic reorientation
- **Romanticism of nature and experience**: recasting awakening as an immediate, unmediated encounter with the natural world or with "pure awareness"
- **Protestant textualism**: privileging scripture over ritual, the individual practitioner over the saṅgha-priest structure, and interior conviction over external performance

### Historical Mechanics

McMahan traces this hybrid through specific figures and movements:

- **Anagarika Dharmapāla** (1864–1933): reformist Sinhalese Buddhism explicitly constructed in dialogue with Victorian scientific rationalism and Protestant evangelical forms. His Buddhism is activist, socially engaged, historically self-conscious, and shorn of "superstition."
- **D.T. Suzuki**: translated Zen into a Romantic-experientialist idiom for Western audiences, emphasising sudden enlightenment as a kind of ineffable, pre-conceptual encounter — deeply indebted to German Idealism and American Transcendentalism.
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, Shunryu Suzuki, MBSR (Jon Kabat-Zinn)**: successive nodes where Buddhist practice gets further secularised, therapeutised, and decoupled from doctrinal commitments.

### Methodological Stakes

McMahan is not saying Buddhist modernism is *false* or *inauthentic* — he explicitly resists that move. His argument is **genealogical** in a Foucauldian register: the conditions of possibility for this Buddhism are historically specifiable, and recognising this should modify practitioners' and scholars' confidence that they are accessing some timeless Dharma unmediated by historical accident.

This has significant implications:

- **"Original Buddhism" is not a stable referent**: even Theravāda reform movements are already products of 19th-century colonial-era textual reconstruction.
- **Meditation-centred Buddhism is an innovation**: the centering of *vipassanā* practice as the core of Theravāda is largely a 20th-century development (associated with figures like Ledi Sayadaw), not an ancient continuous tradition.
- **The "perennial philosophy" claim is historically provincial**: the idea that all mystical traditions share a common experiential core reflects Romantic universalism more than comparative phenomenology.

### Critical Appraisal

McMahan's strength is breadth and narrative integration. His weakness is that he sometimes treats "traditional Buddhism" as a more stable baseline than it is — the internal complexity of pre-modern Asian Buddhism (sectarian disputes, regional variation, continuous ritual innovation) tends to be underplayed as a foil for modernism's novelty. Critics have also noted that the book's implied audience shapes its argument: it reads partly as an intervention into Western convert Buddhism, urging self-awareness.

## II. Gregory Schopen — Archaeological and Epigraphic Critique

### The Target: Textual Buddhism

Schopen's project, developed across a career of articles collected primarily in *Bones, Stones, and Buddhist Monks* (1997) and *Buddhist Monks and Business Matters* (2004), constitutes a sustained methodological assault on what he calls **"the tyranny of the text"** in Buddhist Studies.

The dominant paradigm in 20th-century Buddhist Studies — shaped by figures like T.W. Rhys Davids, Étienne Lamotte, and the Pāli Text Society — assumed that:

1. The earliest recoverable texts (Pāli Nikāyas, early Āgamas) represent the most authentic Buddhism
2. Text-based doctrine reflects actual monastic practice
3. Later development = corruption or degeneration from original purity

Schopen's critique attacks each assumption.

### The Archaeological Turn

Schopen insists on **the evidentiary priority of inscriptions and archaeology** over texts for reconstructing what Buddhists actually *did*. Inscriptions are:

- **Dateable** with far greater precision than manuscripts
- **Produced at the moment of practice**, not retrospectively canonised
- **Resistant to later editorial intervention** in the way texts are not

When you read the inscriptions from early Indian Buddhist sites — Sāñcī, Bhārhut, Nāgārjunakoṇḍa, Mathurā — a strikingly different Buddhism emerges from the textual picture.

### Key Findings

**1. The cult of the book is late; the cult of the stūpa is early and central.**
The Pāli texts present themselves as the supreme locus of the Buddha's presence and merit-field. But epigraphic evidence shows that for centuries, the **stūpa** — containing relics — was the primary site of worship, donation, and merit-making. The textual privilege of scripture over relic-cult appears to be a later *textual* self-promotion, not a description of practice.

**2. Monks owned property, held wealth, and engaged in legal and commercial transactions.**
Canonical Vinaya presents monks as propertyless, dependent on lay generosity, holding no personal possessions beyond the eight requisites. Inscriptions from the 1st–5th centuries CE show monks making individual donations to stūpas in their own names, holding permanent residences, engaging in money-lending, and acting as legal agents. The "pure monk" of Vinaya is, at minimum, an **ideological construction**, not a portrait of historical practice.

**3. The dead were central to Buddhist practice.**
Inscriptional evidence saturates early Buddhist practice with concern for deceased monks and nuns — transfer of merit to the dead, stūpa construction for deceased individuals, memorial donations. The canonical demotion of merit-transfer as a concession to lay "folk religion" while monks maintained purity misreads the archaeology: elite monastics were deeply embedded in funerary cult.

**4. Mahāyāna origins are obscure and non-monastic in ways texts obscure.**
Schopen has argued controversially that Mahāyāna probably began not as a broad movement but as small, forest-dwelling, text-focused groups — but this hypothesis too depends on reading inscriptional *silence* (Mahāyāna texts appear in inscriptions far later than their putative composition dates), which is methodologically tricky.

### Methodological Manifesto

Schopen's essay "Archaeology and Protestant Presuppositions in the Study of Indian Buddhism" (1991) is the locus classicus of his critique. He argues that the textual priority of Western Buddhist scholarship is not neutral methodology but a **Protestant epistemological inheritance**: the assumption that the "real" religion is the scriptural/doctrinal core, and that ritual, material culture, and popular practice are secondary accretions. This is not a universal scholarly principle — it is a culturally specific prejudice that has distorted the field.

### Critical Appraisal

Schopen is enormously influential and his material is now broadly accepted as transforming the field. Critiques include:

- **The inscription/text dichotomy can be overstated**: inscriptions are also texts, embedded in their own rhetorical contexts and power relations; they don't transparently reveal "practice" any more than scriptures do.
- **Argumenta ex silentio** (from the silence of inscriptions regarding Mahāyāna) are inherently fragile.
- His work is also methodologically somewhat normative in reverse: where textual scholars privileged doctrine, Schopen risks privileging material residue as the real.

## III. Robert Sharf — The Critique of Buddhist Experience

### The Problem: Experience as Explanatory Category

Sharf's critical intervention, developed in essays like "Buddhist Modernism and the Rhetoric of Meditative Experience" (1995), "Experience" (in *Critical Terms for Religious Studies*, 1998), and "Is Mindfulness Buddhist?" (2015), targets a different but related assumption: that **meditation produces a distinctive, reportable, cross-culturally accessible inner experience** that can serve as the bedrock of Buddhist practice and the basis for comparative religion.

This assumption underlies:
- William James's *The Varieties of Religious Experience*
- The "perennialist" school (Aldous Huxley, Huston Smith)
- Much of the Western mindfulness movement
- Academic use of "experience" as an emic category for understanding religious life

### The Critique of "Experience"

Sharf draws on Wittgenstein, Foucault, and the linguistic turn to argue that **"experience" is not a pre-theoretical given but a culturally constructed category of interpretation**. Problems with the experiential paradigm:

**1. The privacy paradox**: Religious experiences are, by definition, private and subjective. But religions are public, communal, institutional. How does a private inner state motivate and sustain communal practice across centuries? The appeal to experience explains too little.

**2. The report problem**: What meditators report as experience is not unmediated phenomenal data but culturally scripted narrative. The Zen student who reports "kenshō" is producing a discourse that fits an institutionally sanctioned template. The Theravāda meditator who reports "the arising and passing of phenomena" is deploying Abhidhamma-saturated interpretive categories. There is no phenomenologically neutral access point.

**3. Experience is not the object of most Buddhist practice**: Sharf's historical argument is that pre-modern Asian Buddhism was overwhelmingly concerned with **ritual efficacy, soteriological karma-management, textual transmission, and communal practice** — not with individual meditative experience as a goal in itself. The experience-centred model is a modern, largely Western, largely Protestant-inflected projection onto traditions that were doing something quite different.

**4. The "rhetoric of experience"**: Sharf coins this phrase to describe how claims to authoritative meditative experience function *rhetorically* — to establish authority, validate lineages, and silence critics — rather than as honest phenomenological reports. The Japanese Zen master's claim to have "verified" awakening is a social-institutional claim, not a transparent phenomenological one.

### Sharf on Mindfulness

His 2015 essay "Is Mindfulness Buddhist?" is a precision critique of the MBSR/secular mindfulness movement. His argument:

- The "bare attention" model claimed to be traditional Theravāda is itself a 20th-century Burmese innovation (Mahasi Sayadaw's "noting" technique has no pre-modern precedent as a system)
- The claim that mindfulness is separable from its doctrinal context (the Four Noble Truths, dependent origination, karma, Nibbāna) betrays a fundamental misunderstanding of how it functioned within the tradition
- "Mindfulness" in the Pāli Nikāyas (*sati*) means something closer to "recollective awareness" — holding in mind what has been heard, studied, and committed to — not the decontextualised present-moment awareness of the clinical model

### Critical Appraisal

Sharf's critique of experiential essentialism is compelling and largely accepted in the academic study of religion. However:

- His argument sometimes risks overcorrecting toward a purely discursive/constructivist view where practitioners' reported states are entirely artefacts of language and training — leaving no room for genuine phenomenological difference between meditative and non-meditative states
- His historical argument (that experience wasn't central to pre-modern Buddhism) may itself be reconstructed through the same textual evidence he critiques
- The essays can feel more destructive than constructive — stronger on showing what's wrong with experiential accounts than on what should replace them

## IV. Triangulating the Three

When read together, McMahan, Schopen, and Sharf form a powerful convergent critique:

| Dimension | Target | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| McMahan | The content of modern Buddhism | Genealogy / intellectual history |
| Schopen | The evidentiary base of Buddhist Studies | Epigraphy / archaeology |
| Sharf | The experiential claims of Buddhist practice | Philosophy of language / discourse analysis |

Each shows that a different stratum of "Buddhism as normally understood" rests on historically contingent, methodologically suspect, or ideologically freighted foundations. Together they suggest:

1. **The "original Buddhism" recovered by textual scholarship is largely a 19th-century Protestant-academic construction** (Schopen, reinforced by McMahan)
2. **The "contemplative Buddhism" of Western modernism is a Romantic-experientialist projection** (Sharf, reinforced by McMahan)
3. **What practitioners actually did across Asian history is largely illegible through either textual or experiential lenses** — we need material, institutional, and ritual-practice histories instead

The implication is not nihilistic — it doesn't mean Buddhism is "fake" — but it demands a **radical methodological humility** and a willingness to treat "Buddhism" as a family of historically specific practices, institutions, and discourses rather than a transhistorical essence.

### Shared Blind Spots

All three scholars share a tendency to let their critique do more work than it can:

- **They implicitly reinstate a "real Buddhism"** even while deconstructing normative accounts — for Schopen, it's the epigraphy; for Sharf, it's institutional-ritual practice; for McMahan, it's a pluralism that resists modernist reduction. Each has a residual referent they don't fully interrogate.
- **The Protestant critique of Protestant presuppositions** is itself a Protestant rhetorical move — appeal to evidence, suspicion of tradition, iconoclasm against authority. There's a reflexive irony here none of them fully addresses.
- **Southeast Asian and Chinese Buddhism** tend to get underweighted; the field's India/Japan bias inflects all three scholars in ways not always acknowledged.

## I. The Foundational Problem: Who Gets to Critique?

Before cataloguing specific scholars, it's worth naming a structural irony: McMahan, Schopen, and Sharf are all producing critique **from within Euro-American research universities**, using methodological frameworks (Foucauldian genealogy, Protestant-presupposition analysis, linguistic constructivism) that are themselves Western intellectual inheritances. Their critique of Western-projected Buddhism is conducted in the idiom of Western critical theory.

Asian scholars have identified this recursion as itself a problem. The critique of Buddhist modernism, if it remains confined to Western academia, simply **relocates authority** rather than redistributing it — now the Western scholar who deconstructs the Western convert's Buddhism becomes the arbiter of what "real Buddhism" was. This is what Taiwanese scholar **Shih Heng-ching** and others have called the **second colonisation**: first Western modernity deforms Buddhism, then Western academia claims authority to diagnose the deformation.

## II. Japanese Buddhist Studies — Internal Critique and the Critical Buddhism Movement

### The Hossō/Critical Buddhism Controversy

The most significant organised internal critique of modern Japanese Buddhism came from two scholars at Komazawa University (a Sōtō Zen institution): **Hakamaya Noriaki** (袴谷憲昭) and **Matsumoto Shirō** (松本史朗), who in the 1980s–90s launched what became known as **Hihan Bukkyō** — **Critical Buddhism** (*Kritische Buddhologie*).

Their argument was explosive within Japanese Buddhist Studies:

**Core thesis**: The concept of *hongaku* (本覚) — "original enlightenment," the idea that all sentient beings are already and inherently Buddha — is **not Buddhist**. It is a Chinese and Japanese philosophical interpolation derived from *tathāgatagarbha* (Buddha-nature) doctrine that fundamentally **contradicts** the Buddha's actual teaching of *pratītyasamutpāda* (dependent origination) and *anātman* (no-self).

Hakamaya argued that *hongaku* thought — which became the doctrinal bedrock of Tendai, much of Zen, and later Japanese new religions — collapses the distinction between awakening and non-awakening, between ethical action and inaction, between discrimination and non-discrimination. This produces what he calls **topos philosophy** (場所哲学): a monistic, place-based metaphysics where all distinctions dissolve into an undifferentiated ground.

**The ethical stakes**: Hakamaya made the remarkable claim that *hongaku* thought is **historically and structurally responsible** for Japanese Buddhism's failure to oppose militarism, discrimination against *burakumin* (outcaste communities), sexism within the saṅgha, and complicity with state violence. If all is originally Buddha, then the existing social order is already sacred — critique and reform become soteriologically unnecessary.

This was a critique aimed not at Western Buddhist modernism but at **the classical Japanese tradition itself** — specifically at Dōgen's later writings, Kūkai's Shingon, and the whole apparatus of medieval Japanese Buddhism.

### Reactions and Contestation

The Critical Buddhism movement generated enormous controversy:

- **Paul Swanson, Jamie Hubbard** (eds.) produced *Pruning the Bodhi Tree* (1997) — the primary English-language resource engaging Hihan Bukkyō. Contributors include both sympathetic and hostile interlocutors.
- **Traditionalist Japanese scholars** responded that Hakamaya's "original Buddhism" (essentially an Abhidharma-inflected reading of early Pāli teaching) is itself a reconstruction — the very move Schopen critiques in Western scholars.
- **Sallie King** and others argued that the Critical Buddhism dismissal of Buddha-nature doctrine is too blunt — throwing out the contemplative and soteriological richness of *tathāgatagarbha* traditions on the basis of a narrow textual criterion.
- Ironically, Hakamaya's Buddhism has been compared to **Protestant textualism** — he returns to "original scripture" against tradition, which is precisely McMahan's diagnosis of Buddhist modernism. The critique thus loops back on its authors.

### Significance

Despite its problems, Hihan Bukkyō represents a genuine **internal Asian critique of Buddhism** that:
1. Refuses to accept that Asian Buddhism is simply a victim of Western distortion
2. Locates doctrinal error within the tradition's own historical development
3. Takes seriously the political and ethical consequences of Buddhist metaphysics
4. Operates entirely within Japanese academic Buddhism, in Japanese, with Japanese institutional stakes

## III. Chinese Buddhist Scholarship — The Yinshun Tradition and its Critics

### Master Yinshun (印順, 1906–2005)

Yinshun is arguably the most important Chinese Buddhist scholar-monk of the 20th century, and his work constitutes a sophisticated **internal critique of Chinese Buddhist tradition** that parallels and in some ways anticipates McMahan's concerns — but from a completely different methodological and institutional position.

Trained in the Chinese Buddhist tradition but deeply influenced by Japanese Buddhist Studies (*Bukkyōgaku*) methods, Yinshun applied **historical-critical methodology** to Chinese Buddhism while remaining a practicing bhikṣu committed to the Dharma.

**His central arguments**:

- **Chinese Buddhism has over-prioritised Sinitic developments** — particularly Chan/Zen and Tiantai — at the expense of Indian Buddhist origins. The result is a Buddhism that has become too acculturated, too integrated with Chinese Confucian and Taoist cosmologies, and too disconnected from the Āgama literature (Chinese translations of early Indian texts).
- **Humanistic Buddhism** (*renjian fojiao* 人間佛教) — his signature position — is not simply an accommodation to modernity but a recovery of what he regarded as the **original social and engaged orientation** of Śākyamuni's teaching. The Buddha taught in the human world, for human beings, in historical time — not primarily for celestial beings or in a purely transcendent register.
- **Pure Land and esoteric Buddhism** received Yinshun's sharpest criticism as representing **degenerate accommodations** to popular religion, magical thinking, and cosmological inflation rather than the ethical-psychological core of the Dharma.

This is a striking inversion of Schopen's findings: where Schopen uses archaeological evidence to show that **stūpa-worship and relic-cult were early and central** (undermining the textual-doctrinal picture), Yinshun uses textual-critical methods to argue that **ritual and cosmological accretion are late corruptions** of an original humanistic teaching. The two scholars would fundamentally disagree about the evidentiary basis and directional arc of Buddhist development — yet both are critiquing "Buddhism as normally practised."

### Yinshun's Legacy and Critique

Yinshun's influence in Taiwan is enormous — he is the intellectual godfather of **Tzu Chi** (Cheng Yen), **Dharma Drum Mountain** (Shengyen), and **Fo Guang Shan** (Xingyun), all major Taiwanese Buddhist organisations practising engaged, humanistic forms of Buddhism. 

But he has also attracted significant criticism:

- **Lan Ji-fu and other Taiwanese scholars** have argued that Yinshun's "recovery of original Buddhism" is itself shaped by Japanese Meiji Buddhist scholarship, which was itself shaped by German philology and Protestant assumptions — making Yinshun an unwitting vector of the very Western influence he sought to correct.
- **Charles Jones** (in *Buddhism in Taiwan*, 1999) traces how Yinshun's renjian fojiao, despite its anti-modernist framing, effectively produces a **socially engaged, this-worldly, rationalist Buddhism** that fits McMahan's definition of Buddhist modernism perfectly.
- **Tibetan and esoteric Buddhist practitioners** have been understandably hostile to Yinshun's dismissal of Vajrayāna as degenerate.

### Shengyen (聖嚴, 1930–2009)

Shengyen deserves separate mention as a scholar-practitioner who engaged directly with questions of authenticity and modernism. His work on **Chan history** — particularly his studies of the Linji and Caodong lineages — attempted to recover what he called the **"method of no-method"** of classical Tang-dynasty Chan against what he saw as its modern deformations (both the Japanese Rinzai systematisation critiqued by Sharf and the secular mindfulness movement). His book *The Method of No-Method* is a sophisticated practitioner-scholar response to exactly the issues Sharf raises, though rarely cited in Western academic contexts.

---

## IV. The Postcolonial Turn — Asian Voices in Western-Adjacent Academia

### Ama Adhe and the Sociology of Knowledge Problem

A different kind of critique emerges from scholars of Asian origin working in Western or diasporic contexts who apply **postcolonial theory** to Buddhist Studies as a discipline.

**Gustavo Benavides** and **José Cabezón** have both written about how Buddhist Studies as a Western academic discipline constitutes Buddhism as its object in ways that serve institutional and ideological interests. Cabezón's *Buddhism and Language* (1994) argues that Western scholasticism's encounter with Buddhist scholasticism involves **systematic misreadings** produced by incommensurable epistemic frameworks.

### Wakoh Shannon Hickey

Hickey's work, particularly *Mind Cure: How Meditation Became Medicine* (2019), traces how **American racial politics** shaped the reception of Buddhist meditation — specifically how Asian teachers were systematically displaced from authority as mindfulness was medicalised. This is a critique of Buddhist modernism that centres **race and racialisation** as structural forces, something McMahan's intellectual history largely sidesteps. The whitening of mindfulness (Jon Kabat-Zinn rather than Thich Nhat Hanh as the authoritative figure; the clinical rather than the communal as the legitimate venue) is shown to be not incidental but structural.

### Ann Gleig

Gleig's *American Dharma: Buddhism Beyond Modernity* (2019) engages directly with whether "convert Buddhism" in America is moving beyond the modernist synthesis McMahan describes — including emerging voices from Asian-American Buddhists, teachers of colour, and "nightstand Buddhists" whose relationship to practice doesn't fit the white-convert-meditator model.

---

## V. Tibetan and Himalayan Scholarly Voices

### Tibetan Critique of Western Tibetology

The Tibetan case is particularly acute. Western Tibetology — from Giuseppe Tucci to Donald Lopez — has produced an extensive scholarly apparatus for studying Tibetan Buddhism, but **Tibetan scholars themselves** have raised serious objections:

**Geshe Lhundub Sopa**, **Geshe Rabten**, and in the anglophone space **Thupten Jinpa** have all pointed out that Western academic Tibetology systematically privileges **textual analysis and anthropological observation** over the **oral transmission and commentarial tradition** that Tibetan Buddhism understands as the actual vehicle of knowledge. The scholar who reads the *Lamrim Chenmo* without having received transmission, empowerment (*dbang*), and oral instruction (*lung*) is, from a Tibetan epistemological standpoint, not accessing the text at all — they are reading a husk.

This is not traditionalist obscurantism — it is a genuinely distinct **epistemology of religious knowledge** that Western academic method simply brackets as methodologically inconvenient.

**Donald Lopez** himself, in *Prisoners of Shangri-La* (1998), partially acknowledges this — his critique of Western romanticisation of Tibet is conducted with awareness that he is also a Western scholar constituting Tibet as his object. But Tibetan scholars have noted that Lopez's critique, however incisive about Western fantasies, still leaves the Western scholar as the diagnostic authority.

**Dungkar Lobsang Trinley** (東噶洛桑赤列, 1927–1997) — perhaps the greatest 20th-century Tibetan philologist — produced scholarship that demonstrated the historical contingency and internal variation of Tibetan Buddhist tradition from *within* Tibetan scholarly methods, without any recourse to Western theory. His encyclopaedia (*Dungkar's Great Dictionary*) is a monument to an **indigenous critical scholarship** that has almost no presence in Western Buddhist Studies.

---

## VI. Korean and Southeast Asian Voices

### Korean Buddhism — The Wŏnhyo Revival

Korean Buddhist Studies has its own internal critical tradition centred on the recovery and re-evaluation of **Wŏnhyo** (617–686 CE) — arguably the most philosophically original Korean Buddhist thinker. Contemporary Korean scholars like **Robert Buswell** (Korean-American, trained in Korean monasteries) and **Pori Park** have argued that Korean Buddhism's development — including the **Jogye Order's** meditation-centred practice and the *kanhwa sŏn* (看話禪) system of hwadu/koan practice — represents a distinctive East Asian synthesis that cannot be understood through either Western modernist or Western academic categories.

**Sung Bae Park** and other Korean scholars have made the more provocative argument that **Western comparative religion**, by treating Korean Buddhism as a regional variant of "Buddhism in general," systematically distorts the specific philosophical and institutional contours of Korean practice.

### Southeast Asian Theravāda — The Siam Nikaya Debate

In Sri Lanka and Thailand, there is a robust internal debate about Buddhist authenticity that largely escapes Western academic attention. **P.D. Premasiri** (University of Peradeniya) has critiqued both Western romanticisation and Buddhist modernism from within Sinhalese academic philosophy, arguing that the **Abhidhamma tradition** represents a genuine philosophical achievement systematically undervalued by both Western scholarship (which treats it as scholastic ossification) and Buddhist modernism (which treats it as superstitious elaboration).

Thai academic Buddhist Studies — associated with figures like **Phra Payutto** (พระพยุตโต, b. 1938), perhaps Thailand's most eminent scholar-monk — has produced sophisticated critiques of Western mindfulness appropriation. Phra Payutto's *Buddhadhamma* (expanded English translation 2017) is a comprehensive systematic exposition of Theravāda doctrine explicitly framed as a corrective to both Western misreading and Thai popular Buddhism's magical accretions.

---

## VII. The Meta-Critical Point — Whose Critique Counts?

A final and perhaps most important point: the scholarship above is **differentially cited** in Western Buddhist Studies. Hakamaya gets discussed because his work was translated and engaged by Western scholars (Hubbard, Swanson). Yinshun gets discussed partly because his influence on Taiwanese Buddhism is so structurally significant it cannot be ignored. Dungkar Lobsang Trinley is almost entirely absent from Western Buddhist Studies syllabi despite being more philologically rigorous than most Western Tibetologists.

This differential citability is itself a datum. It shows that **the critique of Western-centric Buddhist Studies has not yet produced a genuinely pluralist methodological field** — it has produced a field where certain Asian voices are selectively incorporated (usually those legible within Western theoretical frameworks) while others remain peripheral.

The scholars doing the most interesting work on this meta-problem include:

- **Kuan Tse-fu** (關則富) on Sinophone Buddhist scholarship's relationship to Japanese and Western methods
- **Chen-kuo Lin** (林鎮國) on the philosophy of comparative Buddhist studies
- **Jinhua Chen** (陳金華) on Chinese Buddhist hagiography and the epistemics of Buddhist historical writing
- **Tansen Sen** on Buddhism across Asian trading networks — a truly non-Eurocentric historical framework that bypasses the whole modernism debate by starting from a different spatial-historical unit of analysis

## I. Framing the Problem

The Taishō Shinshū Daizōkyō (大正新脩大藏經), compiled between 1924 and 1934 under the editorial leadership of **Takakusu Junjirō** (高楠順次郎) and **Watanabe Kaigyoku** (渡邊海旭), is the foundational reference corpus of East Asian Buddhist Studies. Every citation to a Chinese Buddhist text in Western, Japanese, Korean, and Taiwanese scholarship uses its volume and page numbers (T. XXXX). CBETA (Chinese Buddhist Electronic Text Association, est. 1998 in Taiwan) digitised it and made it the backbone of computational Buddhist Studies globally.

This ubiquity is the problem. A corpus that began as a Meiji-era Japanese editorial project — with specific political, nationalist, institutional, and methodological commitments — has become so naturalised as *the* Chinese Buddhist Canon that its constructedness has largely disappeared from view. It functions, in Barthes's sense, as **mythology**: a historical product that presents itself as natural fact.

To understand what ideological freight it carries, we need to reconstruct four intersecting contexts: the Meiji religious settlement, the state of Japanese Buddhist scholarship circa 1900–1920, the specific editorial choices made in compilation, and the long afterlife of those choices in the global discipline.

---

## II. The Meiji Religious Settlement and Buddhism's Precarious Position

### Shinbutsu Bunri and the Threat to Buddhism

The Meiji Restoration (1868) inaugurated a violent reorganisation of Japan's religious landscape. The policy of **shinbutsu bunri** (神仏分離) — the forced separation of Shinto and Buddhism — and the subsequent **haibutsu kishaku** (廃仏毀釈) movement, the "abolition of Buddhism and the discarding of Śākyamuni," subjected Japanese Buddhism to a systematic campaign of institutional destruction: temples demolished, monks forcibly laicised, Buddhist icons burned, temple lands confiscated.

This was not a minor perturbation. In some domains (Satsuma, Mito) it came close to the elimination of institutional Buddhism entirely. The threat was existential.

### Buddhism's Strategic Response

Japanese Buddhist institutions responded with a multi-pronged strategy:

1. **Demonstrate rational respectability**: Buddhism must show it is compatible with — indeed, productive for — Meiji civilisational modernisation. This meant engaging Western scholarship, producing academic institutions, and presenting Buddhism as a philosophical system rather than a ritual superstition.

2. **Demonstrate national utility**: Buddhism must show it is a resource for Japanese national identity and imperial expansion, not a foreign religion (its Indian and Chinese origins were a vulnerability). The construction of Buddhism as a **pan-Asian civilisational heritage** of which Japan was the supreme inheritor and custodian was a strategic response to this vulnerability.

3. **Demonstrate scholarly authority**: Japanese Buddhism must produce *scholarship* — in the Western academic sense — that commands international respect and establishes Japan as the global centre of Buddhist Studies. This is the direct institutional context of the Taishō project.

Takakusu Junjirō himself studied Sanskrit at Oxford under Monier Monier-Williams and Pāli in Paris — he was explicitly forming Japanese Buddhist scholarship in dialogue with and competition with European Orientalism. The Taishō is in part a **demonstration of Japanese scholarly capacity** directed at a European audience.

---

## III. The Political Economy of Canon Compilation

### Previous Canons and the Manuscript Situation

Before the Taishō, East Asian Buddhism had a long history of canon compilation — the Kaibao Canon (開寶藏, Song dynasty, 983 CE), the Goryeo Canon (고려대장경, completed 1251, still extant at Haeinsa), the Jiaxing Canon (嘉興藏, Ming-Qing, 1589–1676), and the Qianlong Canon (乾隆藏, Qing imperial, 18th century). Japanese Buddhism had its own printed canons: the Ōbaku Canon (黃檗版, 1678–1681).

These canons differed significantly — in texts included, in textual variants, in ordering systems, in the presence or absence of indigenous Japanese and Korean compositions. They were also manuscript and woodblock-print cultures: texts existed in multiple recensions with variant readings, and "the canon" was less a fixed object than a distributed, institutionally variable textual ecology.

The Taishō project transformed this ecology by applying **Western philological method** — the critical edition, the apparatus criticus, systematic collation of variants, standardised pagination — to this material. This is simultaneously a genuine scholarly achievement and an act of violent simplification.

### Editorial Choices with Long-Term Consequences

**1. Selection and inclusion criteria**

The Taishō editors made decisions about which texts to include that were not neutral:

- **Apocryphal texts** (疑偽經, *giweikyō*) — texts composed in China, Korea, or Japan but claiming Indian origin — were included but marked as questionable. This marking has had enormous downstream effects: texts central to East Asian Buddhist practice (the *Awakening of Faith*, *Sutra of Perfect Enlightenment*, *Brahma Net Sutra*) carry a scholarly stigma that affects how they are taught, studied, and valued — even though their Chinese composition may be historically irrelevant to their soteriological efficacy or doctrinal significance.

- **Tantric and esoteric texts** (the Taishō's volumes 18–21) were included comprehensively, partly because of Shingon institutional involvement in the project — this was not an editorially neutral decision but reflected the power of esoteric Buddhism within the Meiji Japanese Buddhist establishment.

- **Texts surviving only in Chinese translation** were treated as secondary to Sanskrit or Pāli "originals" — a hierarchical assumption built into the apparatus that encodes the Indo-centric bias of Western Indology directly into the canon's self-presentation.

**2. Ordering and classification**

The Taishō's organisation — Āgamas, Vinaya, Abhidharma, Prajñāpāramitā, Avatamsaka, Ratnakūṭa, Nirvāṇa, Tantric, History/Biography, Encyclopaedic/Catalogue, Japanese compositions — reflects a **hybrid of traditional Chinese canon organisation and Western genre taxonomy**. The separation of "Doctrina" from "History" encodes a Western academic distinction between normative religious teaching and historical documentation that Chinese Buddhist bibliographic tradition did not make in the same way.

The placement of Japanese Buddhist compositions (*Nichiren, Kūkai, Dōgen* excerpts) in the final volumes is a subtle **nationalist claim**: Japanese Buddhism is the culminating development of the tradition, the apex toward which the Indian-Chinese-Korean trajectory points.

**3. Variant readings and the apparatus**

The Taishō collated variants from the Goryeo Canon, the Song Canon, and other sources, but its **base text** choices consistently favoured Japanese manuscript traditions over Korean or Chinese ones where they differed. This has been criticised by Korean Buddhist scholars — particularly those associated with the **Haeinsa Tripitaka project** — as encoding Japanese textual superiority into what presents itself as a neutral critical apparatus.

Marcus Bingenheimer (one of the few Western scholars to have written explicitly on the Taishō's editorial politics) has noted that the choice of base text was often driven by **availability in Japanese temple libraries** rather than by systematic evaluation of textual quality — a practical contingency that hardened into scholarly authority.

---

## IV. The Imperial and Nationalist Dimensions

### Pan-Asianism and Buddhist Soft Power

The Taishō project was compiled during Japan's imperial expansion. Takakusu was not apolitical — he was an active participant in **pan-Asianist intellectual circles** that used Buddhism as a civilisational idiom for Japanese regional hegemony. The argument ran: Japan has preserved and perfected the Buddhist heritage that India lost and China partially corrupted; Japan therefore has both the scholarly authority and the moral responsibility to curate this heritage for all of Asia.

This logic — **Japan as trustee of Asian Buddhist civilisation** — is directly parallel to the British Museum's logic for holding the Elgin Marbles. It naturalises an imperial power relation as custodial responsibility.

The timing matters: the Taishō was compiled between 1924 and 1934, overlapping with the consolidation of Japanese control over Korea (annexed 1910), the establishment of Manchukuo (1931), and the early stages of the Second Sino-Japanese War. The canon's claim to represent "the Chinese Buddhist heritage" was compiled during a period when Japan was militarily subjugating China.

### Korean Erasure

The **Goryeo Canon** (팔만대장경) is by most philological assessments the most carefully compiled and textually reliable East Asian Buddhist canon — produced through two massive compilation projects (11th and 13th centuries) with systematic collation and correction. It survives intact at Haeinsa, which is extraordinary.

The Taishō used the Goryeo Canon as a collation source but did not treat it as a base text, and the organisational framework of the Taishō makes Korean Buddhist contributions largely invisible. Korean indigenous compositions are not included. The implicit narrative is: India → China → Japan, with Korea as a transmission corridor rather than a site of original Buddhist thought.

This erasure has had lasting consequences. **Wŏnhyo** — arguably the most philosophically original thinker in the history of East Asian Buddhism — is almost entirely absent from the Taishō except in fragmentary quotations. His works survive in Korean sources and are now being systematically recovered through the **Korean Buddhist Canon project** (한국불교전서, *Hanguk Bulgyo Jŏnsŏ*), but the Taishō-centred curriculum of East Asian Buddhist Studies has systematically marginalised him.

## V. CBETA and the Digitalisation of Ideological Freight

### What CBETA Did

The Chinese Buddhist Electronic Text Association, based in Taipei and founded in 1998 under the patronage of **Dharma Drum Mountain** (Shengyen's institution), digitised the Taishō and made it freely available online. This was an enormous scholarly achievement that democratised access to the corpus globally.

But CBETA **digitised the Taishō's structure**, not just its content. Every editorial decision made in 1924–1934 — the text selections, the ordering, the apocrypha markings, the variant apparatus, the base text choices — was encoded into a searchable database and given the additional authority that comes with computational infrastructure.

### The Downstream Effects

**In computational humanities**: every study using CBETA for textual analysis, topic modelling, network analysis, or stylometric comparison is working with a dataset whose construction is rarely interrogated. The "Chinese Buddhist textual tradition" as a computational object is the Taishō edition, with all its 1920s Japanese editorial decisions now laundered through algorithmic respectability.

**In translation projects**: the standard reference for virtually all English translations of Chinese Buddhist texts is the Taishō number. When Bhikkhu Bodhi, Thomas Cleary, or the BDK translation series produces an English version of a Chinese sūtra, they work from the Taishō text — the Meiji editorial choices are now present in English translations used by practitioners globally.

**In Buddhist pedagogy**: courses on East Asian Buddhism everywhere use Taishō numbers as the reference system. Graduate students are trained to cite T. 1666 rather than to locate the text in its manuscript tradition, its institutional context, or its variant recensions. The Taishō's organisation becomes the implicit taxonomy of "what exists" in Chinese Buddhism.

### CBETA's Own Ideological Context

It is worth noting that CBETA itself carries the institutional imprint of **Taiwanese engaged Buddhism** — specifically Shengyen's Dharma Drum Mountain and its renjian fojiao orientation (itself, as noted in the previous turn, entangled with Yinshun's modernist reconfiguration of Chinese Buddhism). The selection of texts to digitise beyond the Taishō core, the interface design decisions, and the pedagogical framing of the CBETA website all reflect these institutional commitments.

The recent expansion of CBETA to include texts outside the Taishō — the **CBETA Online** platform now incorporates the Xinzuan Xuzangjing (新纂卍續藏), the Jiaxing Canon, and other collections — is a partial correction, but these additions remain framed within the organisational logic established by the Taishō.

---

## VI. Corrective Projects and Their Limitations

### The SAT Database (Japan)

The **SAT Daizōkyō Text Database** (Saṃgaṇikīkṛtaṃ Taiśotripiṭakam), developed at the University of Tokyo, provides an alternative digital access point to the Taishō with improved scholarly apparatus. But it corrects textual errors rather than the canon's structural premises.

### The Korean Buddhist Canon (한국불교전서)

The *Hanguk Bulgyo Jŏnsŏ* project at Dongguk University systematically compiles Korean Buddhist compositions excluded from the Taishō. This is the most significant corrective to the Taishō's Korean erasure, and it has enabled the recovery of Wŏnhyo, **Jinul** (知訥), **Uicheon** (義天), and other major figures. But it operates as a supplement to rather than replacement of the Taishō system — Korean Buddhist Studies remains secondary to Chinese Buddhist Studies institutionally.

### The BUDA Platform (Tibetan)

The **Buddhist Digital Archives** project for Tibetan Buddhist texts operates entirely outside the Taishō system and has developed its own metadata frameworks, organisational principles, and access infrastructure. This represents a genuinely alternative digital canon project that could serve as a model for how to build Buddhist textual infrastructure without inheriting the Taishō's framework — but it serves a different linguistic tradition.

### Digital Sanskrit Buddhist Canon

Similar projects for Sanskrit materials (the **GRETIL** corpus, the **Buddhist Digital Resource Center's** Sanskrit holdings) are fragmentary and have not achieved the institutional consolidation of the Taishō-CBETA system.

## VII. The Meta-Level: What a Canon Does

Drawing on **Moshe Halbertal's** *People of the Book* (1997) and **Wendy Doniger's** work on canonisation, we can name what the Taishō does as a canon beyond its content:

**1. It creates a community of reference**: scholars who share Taishō citation practices share an implicit world. The canon produces the discipline as much as the discipline produces the canon.

**2. It naturalises exclusions**: what is not in the Taishō tends, over time, to seem not to exist. The texts, traditions, and practices that the Taishō marginalises or excludes become invisible to the scholars trained within its framework.

**3. It encodes a temporal narrative**: the Taishō's organisation implies a story about how Buddhism developed — from India through China to Japan — that is a specific historical interpretation, not a neutral description.

**4. It stabilises textual variance**: the printed, paginated, numbered Taishō text presents a stability that the actual manuscript tradition does not possess. This is epistemically productive (it enables shared reference) but ontologically misleading (it suggests texts have a fixed form they do not have).

**5. It launders political origins**: the specific geopolitical context of 1920s imperial Japan disappears behind the scholarly authority of the completed canon. The ideological work it performed becomes invisible once the project is finished and institutionalised.

---

## VIII. What Would a Post-Taishō Buddhist Studies Look Like?

This is genuinely an open question, but some directions are visible:

- **Manuscript studies** as a corrective to print-canonical thinking — following Schopen's epigraphy, but for the textual tradition: treating individual manuscripts as historical objects rather than imperfect witnesses to an ideal text
- **Multi-canonical reference systems** that give equal weight to the Goryeo, Qianlong, and Jiaxing canons rather than treating them as collation sources for the Taishō
- **Inclusion of oral and performative traditions** that canons by definition exclude — liturgical texts, ritual manuals in active use, living commentary traditions
- **Decentring the India-China-Japan narrative** through Southeast Asian, Central Asian, and Tibetan materials that construct different geographical imaginaries of Buddhist transmission
- **Explicit metacritical framing** of CBETA and similar databases as historical objects with editorial histories — the way a critical edition in classical studies includes a history of the text

## I. The Historiographical Problem First

The default narrative runs: the Buddha meditated under the Bodhi tree → meditation is therefore Buddhism's heart → practitioners today continue this ancient practice. This narrative is **genealogically false** in almost every particular.

What the genealogy actually shows:

- Systematic lay meditation practice is a **20th-century innovation**
- The specific techniques taught globally today have identifiable inventors with names and dates
- The institutional conditions enabling mass meditation are **colonial and post-colonial**, not ancient
- The centering of meditation as *the* soteriological technology required the **marginalisation** of the vast majority of what Asian Buddhists actually did — ritual, merit-making, devotion, textual recitation, ethical conduct — as "mere popular religion"

This is Sharf's core claim, but the genealogy fills in what Sharf states programmatically.

---

## II. What Pre-Modern Buddhist Soteriology Actually Looked Like

### The Canonical Picture Is Already Complex

The Pāli Nikāyas present meditation (*bhāvanā*) as one component of a threefold training: **sīla** (ethical conduct), **samādhi** (mental unification), **paññā** (wisdom). But several things are clear from careful reading:

- The meditating figures in the Nikāyas are **wandering renunciants** (bhikkhus) who have abandoned household life entirely. Lay practice is overwhelmingly framed around **dāna** (giving), **sīla** (precepts), and **bhāvanā** in the attenuated sense of devotional mental cultivation — not formal sitting meditation.
- The **Abhidhamma** and early commentarial tradition (Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga*, 5th century CE) systematises meditation into an elaborate technical apparatus — but this apparatus is explicitly **for monastics**, specifically for those pursuing the path to arahantship. It is not a technology for laypeople.
- **Samatha** (calm-abiding) and **vipassanā** (insight) are distinguished in the Nikāyas, but the sharp opposition between them — and the privileging of vipassanā as the distinctively Buddhist contribution — is a **later interpretive move**, not an original structure.

### What Monks Actually Did

Schopen's archaeological work is directly relevant here. The inscriptions from early Indian Buddhist sites show monks and nuns engaged in:

- Stūpa construction and veneration
- Relic cult
- Merit-transfer for deceased relatives
- Manuscript copying and preservation
- Ritual recitation (*pāṭha*)
- Property management and legal transactions

Systematic sitting meditation in forest hermitages was a **specialist vocation** — the *āraññika* (forest-dweller) — occupying one end of a spectrum that included urban *gāmavāsika* (village-dwelling) monks whose practice was almost entirely ritual and textual. The assumption that all monks were meditating is a back-projection.

### Chinese and East Asian Buddhism

In Chinese Buddhism, the dominant soteriological technologies across most of history were:

- **Scriptural recitation and copying** — the merit of copying the Lotus Sūtra, for instance, is elaborately described within the text itself
- **Buddha-name recitation** (*nianfo* 念佛) — Pure Land practice, which by the Tang dynasty was the dominant form of Buddhist practice across social strata
- **Ritual confession and repentance** (*chanhui* 懺悔)
- **Precept-taking and maintenance**
- **Chan encounter dialogue** — but note that even Chan monasteries operated primarily through liturgical schedule, communal labour, and ritual; the image of the Chan monk perpetually sitting in *zazen* is itself a modern construction

The Chan/Zen image of meditation-as-essence is partly produced by the tradition's own rhetoric (the "special transmission outside the scriptures" claim) and partly by how D.T. Suzuki presented Zen to the West — stripping out the ritual, institutional, and liturgical dimensions that constituted actual monastic life.

## III. The Colonial Rupture — Burma as Ground Zero

### The Structural Conditions

The transformation begins in colonial Burma under British administration. Several conditions converge:

**1. Destruction of the traditional saṅgha-state relationship**: The British deposed King Thibaw in 1885, ending the monarchy that had legitimated and regulated the Burmese saṅgha. The *thathanabaing* (head of the Burmese monastic order) lost state backing. Monastic discipline loosened. The traditional system of village-based monastic education was disrupted by colonial schooling. Buddhism faced an institutional crisis.

**2. Protestant missionary critique**: Christian missionaries attacked Burmese Buddhism as superstitious, passive, world-denying, and socially ineffective. The critique required a response that could demonstrate Buddhism's rational, activist, morally serious character.

**3. The Pāli Text Society and textual availability**: Rhys Davids's PTS (founded 1881) was making Pāli canonical texts available in romanised editions and English translation for the first time. Burmese reformers could now access "original Buddhism" through these editions — a Protestant-textual move that bypassed the traditional commentarial and oral transmission system.

**4. Print culture and lay education**: Burmese lay literacy and print culture were expanding. Texts that had been the province of monastics could now circulate among educated laypeople.

### Ledi Sayadaw (လေဒီဆရာတော်, 1846–1923) — The Pivotal Figure

Ledi Sayadaw is the single most important figure in the genealogy of modern meditation-centred Buddhism, and he is almost entirely absent from popular accounts of "mindfulness."

Ledi was a brilliant Abhidhamma scholar and prolific author — he wrote extensively in Burmese vernacular (not just Pāli) specifically to reach lay audiences. His doctrinal intervention was this:

**The traditional soteriological path required a supporting infrastructure** — a functioning saṅgha, a Buddhist king, a stable society in which the Dhamma flourished. That infrastructure was being destroyed by British colonialism. In the absence of the traditional conditions, **laypeople needed to practice vipassanā directly**, because the opportunity for liberation might otherwise be lost entirely.

This is a **crisis theology**. Ledi's opening of vipassanā to laypeople was not a democratic reform but an emergency measure — a response to what he perceived as the imminent extinction of the conditions for liberation.

His specific doctrinal move: he argued that **vipassanā** (insight meditation) could be practised without the full development of **samatha** (calm-abiding) — that "dry insight" (*sukkha-vipassanā*) could lead to liberation without the jhānas (meditative absorptions). This shortcut was not without Pāli textual precedent, but it was controversial and represented a significant departure from the dominant commentarial tradition (Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga* presupposes samatha as the foundation).

Ledi taught meditation to laypeople directly, produced manuals in Burmese vernacular, and created a network of lay meditation teachers. He was also a Burmese nationalist — his meditation movement was explicitly framed as **cultural resistance to British colonisation**, a way of preserving the Buddhist heritage against colonial erosion.

### U Narada (1868–1955) and Mahasi Sayadaw (မဟာစည်ဆရာတော်, 1904–1982)

U Narada developed Ledi's approach into a more systematised technique. His student **Mahasi Sayadaw** then transformed it into the globally exported form.

Mahasi's innovations:

- **The "noting" technique** (*labeling*): meditators mentally note each arising phenomenon — "rising, falling" for the breath, "hearing, seeing, thinking" for sense contacts — creating a continuous, discursive, labeling awareness of present-moment experience
- **Intensive retreat format**: the *satipaṭṭhāna* retreat, conducted in silence with multiple daily interviews with the teacher, compressing what might take years of gradual training into weeks of intensive practice
- **Systematic interview and verification**: Mahasi developed a standardised progression of insight knowledges (*vipassanā-ñāṇa*) against which students' reports were checked — creating what Sharf calls the "rhetoric of experience" in institutional form

Sharf's critique is directly applicable here: the noting technique **produces** certain kinds of reportable experience. Students learn to perceive in Abhidhammic categories because the technique's labeling procedure is saturated with Abhidhammic ontology (the three characteristics, the arising and passing of phenomena). What presents itself as unmediated observation of bare experience is actually **theory-laden perception** trained into the body through intensive practice.

Mahasi's centre in Rangoon (established 1949, the year after Burmese independence — the nationalist-political timing is not coincidental) became the training ground for teachers who exported the technique globally: **S.N. Goenka** (to India and then worldwide), **Joseph Goldstein, Sharon Salzberg, Jack Kornfield** (American Insight Meditation), and eventually the entire Western vipassanā movement.

---

## IV. The Thai Forest Tradition — A Parallel but Distinct Genealogy

### Ajahn Mun (อาจารย์มั่น ภูริทัตโต, 1870–1949)

Simultaneously with the Burmese development, a distinct meditation revival was occurring in northeastern Thailand and Laos. Ajahn Mun Bhūridatta revived the **dhutaṅga** (ascetic practice) tradition of forest-dwelling monks, emphasising rigorous discipline, wandering, and an approach to meditation that differed significantly from Mahasi's noting technique.

Ajahn Mun's practice was **samatha-weighted** — developing deep samādhi states (jhāna) as the foundation for insight, the reverse of Mahasi's dry-insight shortcut. His approach also included **visionary and psychic dimensions** (communication with devas, past-life memories, encounters with forest spirits) that are largely suppressed in the Western transmission of his lineage.

His students — **Ajahn Chah, Ajahn Lee, Ajahn Fuang** — produced the Thai Forest Tradition, which reached the West primarily through **Ajahn Chah's** teaching of **Ajahn Sumedho** and the establishment of Amaravati and Chithurst monasteries in Britain.

The Thai Forest Tradition presents itself as a recovery of ancient wandering-ascetic practice against the corruption of urban, ritual-focused Thai temple Buddhism. This **primitivist authenticity claim** — back to the forest, back to the original bhikkhu — is structurally identical to Protestant return-to-scripture moves, as several scholars have noted.

### The Nationalist Dimension

Ajahn Mun's movement also carried nationalist freight — emerging in the context of Thai state-building, the standardisation of Thai Buddhism under **Rama IV (Mongkut)** and **Rama V (Chulalongkorn)**, and the threat posed by both Western colonialism (Thailand was not colonised but was under intense pressure) and the Westernisation of Thai Buddhism through the **Dhammayut reform movement** that Mongkut himself had initiated as a monk.

The Dhammayut reform movement is itself a crucial node: Mongkut's reform of Thai Buddhism — emphasising textual fidelity, rational interpretation, and rejection of "superstitious" practices — is a **royal-nationalist Buddhist modernism** that precedes and parallels the Burmese developments, and that restructures Thai institutional Buddhism in ways that make the subsequent meditation revival both possible and necessary as a counter-movement.

---

## V. Sri Lanka — The Anagarika Dharmapāla Node

McMahan's account of Dharmapāla is well-known, but the specifically **meditation-related** dimensions deserve attention.

Dharmapāla's Buddhism was not particularly meditation-centred — it was activist, nationalist, and ethics-centred. But his reconstruction of Buddhism as a rational, experiential, personally transformative religion **created the conceptual space** within which meditation could later become central.

His specific contributions to the genealogy:

- **Disembedding Buddhism from ritual and communal practice** — his Buddhism is individualist, ethicised, and rational in ways that make meditation (as individual interior practice) the natural next step
- **The Maha Bodhi Society** (founded 1891) — his project of recovering Buddhist sacred sites in India created the infrastructure of pilgrimage and lay Buddhist identity that would later support meditation retreats
- **The "Buddhism is not a religion but a way of life"** formulation — this move, recurrent in Buddhist modernism, positions Buddhism as compatible with secular scientific rationalism and frames meditation as psychological technology rather than ritual practice

The connection between Dharmapāla's Buddhist modernism and the subsequent global vipassanā movement runs through **Nyanatiloka Mahāthera** (German-born, ordained in Sri Lanka) and the **Island Hermitage** on Polgasduwa, which became a conduit for Western engagement with Theravāda meditation from the early 20th century.

---

## VI. The American and European Reception — Three Channels

By the 1960s–70s, the meditation genealogy splits into several distinct streams that eventually converge in the Western mindfulness movement:

### Channel 1: The Insight Meditation Society (IMS) — Mahasi Lineage in America

**Joseph Goldstein, Sharon Salzberg, and Jack Kornfield** all trained in Asia in the early 1970s — Goldstein and Salzberg with Mahasi-lineage teachers (S.N. Goenka, Anagarika Munindra), Kornfield with Ajahn Chah in Thailand. They founded IMS in Barre, Massachusetts in 1975.

Critical features of the IMS transmission:

- **Decontextualisation**: the monastic and cultural context of Mahasi's technique was largely stripped away. The intensive retreat format was preserved; the Abhidhammic doctrinal framework was "backgrounded" but not eliminated
- **Psychologisation**: Kornfield's clinical psychology training shaped how insight experiences were framed — increasingly in therapeutic rather than soteriological terms
- **Democratisation**: no requirement of ordination, extended study, or teacher-student relationship in the traditional sense — technique-transmission replaces lineage-transmission

### Channel 2: Zen — The D.T. Suzuki to Philip Kapleau Pipeline

The Zen channel runs through D.T. Suzuki's writings (1920s–1950s), **Philip Kapleau's** *The Three Pillars of Zen* (1965) — the first Western book to present Zen as a practical meditation manual rather than a philosophical curiosity — and **Shunryu Suzuki's** *Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind* (1970).

**Shunryu Suzuki** (1904–1971) is particularly important: his presentation of *zazen* (seated Zen meditation) as something anyone can do, without extensive doctrinal preparation, without koan curriculum, without the full apparatus of Japanese Rinzai or Sōtō training, is the Zen parallel to Mahasi's democratisation of vipassanā. His formulation — "in the beginner's mind there are many possibilities; in the expert's mind there are few" — encodes exactly the Romantic-experientialist valorisation of immediacy over learned technique that Sharf critiques.

### Channel 3: The Tibetan Diaspora — Chögyam Trungpa and the Shambhala Transmission

**Chögyam Trungpa Rinpoche** (1939–1987), who arrived in the West in 1970 after leaving Scotland for the United States, created a distinct channel that brought Vajrayāna meditation (particularly *śamatha* and *vipaśyanā* in the Tibetan formulation, plus *dzogchen* and *mahāmudrā*) into Western cultural contexts with extraordinary creative energy.

Trungpa's transmission is important for this genealogy because:

- He refused to sanitise — he taught with wine glass in hand, engaged with Western psychology (co-founded Naropa Institute with the explicit goal of dialogue between Buddhist and Western psychology), and explicitly addressed Western psychological patterns as the material of meditation practice
- He coined **"spiritual materialism"** — the concept that Western practitioners use meditation to aggrandise rather than dissolve the ego — which is an internal critique of the Western meditation movement from within that movement
- **His lineage** (through **Pema Chödrön** primarily) has become enormously influential in popular mindfulness culture, often without the Vajrayāna doctrinal context

---

## VII. The Secularisation Node — MBSR and the Clinical Turn

### Jon Kabat-Zinn and the Laundering of Technique

**Jon Kabat-Zinn** (b. 1944) studied with Korean Zen master **Seungsahn** and with Mahasi-lineage teachers. In 1979 at the University of Massachusetts Medical School he developed **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction** (MBSR) — a structured eight-week programme using mindfulness meditation techniques for chronic pain and stress.

The MBSR development involves several critical moves:

**1. Deliberate decontextualisation**: Kabat-Zinn explicitly removed Buddhist terminology, doctrinal framing, and soteriological goals. "Mindfulness" (*sati*) was operationalised as "paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and non-judgmentally" — a definition that strips out the *recollective* dimension of *sati* (as Sharf notes), the ethical framework, the goal of liberation, and the community context.

**2. Medical legitimation**: By locating MBSR in a hospital setting and subjecting it to clinical trial methodology, Kabat-Zinn secured a form of epistemological authority (randomised controlled trial evidence) that bypassed questions of doctrinal authenticity entirely. The question "is this real Buddhism?" becomes irrelevant when replaced by "does this reduce cortisol levels?"

**3. The "universal dharma" claim**: Kabat-Zinn has consistently argued that MBSR is not a decontextualisation of Buddhism but a recovery of the universal truth that Buddhism happened to discover — a perennialist move that McMahan diagnoses as structurally characteristic of Buddhist modernism.

**4. Second-order canonisation**: MBSR generated **Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy** (MBCT, Segal, Williams, Teasdale, 2002), **Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention**, **Acceptance and Commitment Therapy**, and an entire ecosystem of clinical mindfulness interventions. Each further generation of derivation further from the Buddhist source while retaining the meditation technique as core.

### The Neuroscience Alliance

Simultaneously with MBSR's clinical expansion, a scientific programme around meditation emerged through the collaboration of **Richard Davidson** (University of Wisconsin), **Francisco Varela** (phenomenology/neurophenomenology), and the **Mind and Life Institute** (founded 1987 through dialogue between the Dalai Lama and Francisco Varela).

This alliance produced the **contemplative neuroscience** field — fMRI studies of meditating monks, research on neuroplasticity and meditation practice, the "happiness research" associated with **Matthieu Ricard**. The effect on the genealogy:

- Meditation acquires the authority of neuroscientific measurement — a secular epistemic warrant that entirely displaces the traditional soteriological warrant
- The Dalai Lama's active participation naturalises the alliance and gives it a kind of traditional legitimacy — but Tibetan Buddhist institutions are not where the neuroscience actually leads; it leads to secular clinical programmes
- **Evan Thompson's** subsequent critique (*Waking, Dreaming, Being*, 2015) from within the neurophenomenology tradition — arguing that the neuroscience of meditation systematically fails to engage with the first-person phenomenological claims of the traditions — represents an internal self-correction the field has only partially absorbed

## VIII. The Full Genealogical Map

The genealogy is not a single line but a **braided structure**:

```

         ↓
Colonial Burma + British destruction of saṅgha-state nexus
         ↓
Ledi Sayadaw — crisis theology, lay vipassanā, dry insight
         ↓
U Narada → Mahasi Sayadaw — noting technique, intensive retreat
         ↓                              ↓
   S.N. Goenka              Goldstein/Salzberg/Kornfield

    franchised Goenka        → Western Insight Meditation
    centres)
         ↓                              ↓
  Parallel streams:              Kabat-Zinn 1979

      Kapleau)                   → global secular mindfulness
  Tibetan (Trungpa,

```

Every node in this structure involves:
1. A **selection** from the available tradition (what gets transmitted)
2. A **translation** across cultural and linguistic contexts
3. A **transformation** of institutional form (monastic → lay → clinical)
4. A **legitimation claim** (traditional authenticity / scientific evidence / therapeutic efficacy)
5. A **suppression** of what doesn't travel (cosmology, ethics, community, lineage obligation, the goal of liberation rather than wellbeing)

## IX. What the Genealogy Reveals

**1. There is no ancient unbroken tradition of lay meditation practice.** The direct lineage from Ledi Sayadaw to Kabat-Zinn is approximately 100 years old. The claim to ancient continuity is part of the legitimation rhetoric, not a historical description.

**2. The techniques have inventors.** Mahasi's noting method, Goenka's body-scan, Kabat-Zinn's MBSR operationalisation — these are innovations with dates and authors. They are not transmissions of timeless practice.

**3. Each transmission node involves political conditions.** Ledi's crisis theology responds to colonial destruction. Mahasi's Rangoon centre opens in 1949 in newly independent Burma. IMS emerges from the American counterculture. MBSR is enabled by the specific institutional structure of American academic medicine. None of these are accidental.

**4. The suppression of samatha is a modern choice.** The Mahasi-lineage prioritisation of dry insight vipassanā over jhāna-based samatha represents a specific and contested doctrinal decision — not the traditional Theravāda position. The Thai Forest and Tibetan traditions maintain samatha as foundational. Global mindfulness culture's focus on present-moment awareness without depth states reflects the Mahasi lineage's specific technical choice, not "what meditation is."

**5. The ethics were dropped.** In the Nikāyas, *sīla* precedes *samādhi* — ethical conduct is the ground of meditation. In MBSR and secular mindfulness, ethical training is absent. The McMahan-Sharf critique is vindicated: what reached the clinic is a technique extracted from a soteriological system, presented as a universal psychological tool.

#MahāyānaSūtraComposition #TextualPerformance #CanonFormationTheory
🔭 Lens: Sūtra as speech-act — Mahāyāna texts not as records of doctrine but as **performative enactments** that constitute their own authority, community, and soteriological field in the act of being recited, copied, and transmitted

## I. The Problem Both Scholars Address

Before mapping Gummer and Gómez separately, the shared problem:

Schopen's inscriptional silence argument — that Mahāyāna appears almost nowhere in Indian epigraphy until centuries after the texts' putative composition — creates a genuine puzzle. If Mahāyāna sūtras were being composed from roughly the 1st century BCE onward, where are the communities? Where are the donors? Where are the institutional traces?

Two inadequate responses dominated prior scholarship:

**The Council/Schism model**: Mahāyāna emerged from a specific sectarian split — the Mahāsāṃghika hypothesis — and represents a distinct institutional movement. This is now largely abandoned as insufficient; no single sectarian origin accounts for Mahāyāna's textual diversity.

**The Lay Reform model**: Mahāyāna was a lay-driven reform movement protesting monastic elitism. Hirakawa Akira's influential version — Mahāyāna originated in lay stūpa-cult communities — was precisely what Schopen's epigraphy undermined: stūpa donors are never identified as Mahāyānists in inscriptions.

Both Gummer and Gómez offer more sophisticated alternatives that take the **textuality of Mahāyāna seriously** — not as a problem to be explained away but as the actual locus of Mahāyāna's existence and efficacy.

---

## II. Natalie Gummer — Sūtra as Ritual Performance

### The Core Argument

Gummer's work, centred on her dissertation and subsequent articles on the *Sūtrālaṃkāra* and sūtra-recitation texts, develops what might be called a **ritual-performative theory of sūtra composition**. Her key move is to read Mahāyāna sūtras not as doctrinal treatises that happen to have a narrative frame, but as **ritual scripts** — texts whose meaning is inseparable from their performance in recitation, copying, and liturgical deployment.

The implications are substantial:

**1. The sūtra creates its own congregation.**
Many Mahāyāna sūtras contain explicit passages about their own merit — the *dharmabhāṇaka* (dharma-preacher) passages in texts like the *Lotus Sūtra*, *Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā*, and *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa* that enumerate the immeasurable merit of copying, reciting, teaching, and hearing the text. Gummer reads these not as self-promotional insertions but as **constitutive speech acts**: the text performing its own sacrality in the act of being recited.

This answers Schopen's inscriptional silence problem obliquely: if Mahāyāna's community is constituted through text-performance rather than through temple construction or stūpa donation, it would leave precisely the kind of thin archaeological trace that Schopen finds. The community is a **textual community** in Brian Stock's sense — constituted around the practice of a text rather than around a physical site.

**2. The *dharmabhāṇaka* as institutional node.**
The figure of the *dharmabhāṇaka* — the professional dharma-preacher who recites, copies, and transmits the sūtra — is, Gummer argues, the **institutional form** of early Mahāyāna. Not a monastic order, not a lay organisation, but a specialist role within the broader Buddhist world: the person who holds, performs, and transmits specific texts. This explains both the diversity of Mahāyāna (different dharmabhāṇakas transmitting different texts without central coordination) and its porosity (dharmabhāṇakas could operate within multiple sectarian contexts).

**3. Oral composition and written fixation.**
Gummer engages seriously with the question of how Mahāyāna sūtras were composed — rejecting the simple model of authored texts that were then transmitted. Her argument draws on work in oral-formulaic theory (Parry, Lord) and on the specific formulaic structures of Mahāyāna sūtras: the opening *evaṃ mayā śrutam* ("thus have I heard"), the assembly descriptions, the question-answer dialogues, the repeated doctrinal set-pieces. These are not literary ornaments but **compositional infrastructure** enabling oral recitation, improvisation within fixed parameters, and accretion over time.

The sūtra is not a text that was composed and then performed; it is a **performance tradition that was progressively textualised**.

### Gummer on the Prajñāpāramitā

Her most developed case study involves the Prajñāpāramitā literature — the vast "Perfection of Wisdom" corpus ranging from the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* (8,000 lines) to the *Śatasāhasrikā* (100,000 lines) to the condensed *Heart Sūtra* and *Diamond Sūtra*.

Standard accounts treat the shorter texts as original and the longer versions as expansions. Gummer complicates this: the longer texts contain extensive *dharmabhāṇaka* passages and elaborate ritual instructions for text-performance that the shorter versions lack or condense. This suggests the longer versions may preserve **earlier ritual-performative strata** even if the doctrinal core of the shorter texts is older. The expansion is not simply rhetorical inflation but **the sedimentation of performative occasions** into textual form.

The *Heart Sūtra* — the most condensed, the most liturgically deployed, the most globally recited Buddhist text — is from this perspective not a distillation of wisdom but the residue of a performance tradition compressed to its most portable, most repeatable kernel.

### Critique of Gummer

- Her argument depends heavily on a relatively small corpus of texts with explicit dharmabhāṇaka passages; it is not clear how well it extends to Mahāyāna sūtras without such passages (much of the Avataṃsaka literature, for instance)
- The oral-formulaic model, developed for Homeric epic, requires careful calibration for Sanskrit Buddhist contexts where written transmission and oral performance were simultaneous rather than sequential
- She sometimes comes close to making the text's self-description (its own merit-claims) the evidence for her theory of what it is — a potentially circular move

## III. Luis O. Gómez — Between Philology and Phenomenology

### The Positioning

Gómez occupies a distinctive methodological position in Buddhist Studies: trained as a rigorous philologist (his translations of the *Longer* and *Shorter Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtras* are models of the craft) but consistently attentive to what texts *do* rather than only what they *say*. His engagement with Mahāyāna sūtra composition runs through several essays and his landmark work on Pure Land.

### The Indra's Net Problem

Gómez has written penetratingly on what he calls the **compositional paradox** of Mahāyāna sūtras: they present themselves as the speech of the Buddha (*buddhavacana*), yet they were clearly composed centuries after the historical Buddha's death by persons who knew this. How does this work? What kind of authority-claim is being made, and how was it sustained?

His answer engages with Buddhist theories of **buddhavacana** themselves. The claim that a text is "the word of the Buddha" does not necessarily mean the historical Śākyamuni spoke these words at Vulture Peak or wherever. Within Mahāyāna doctrinal frameworks:

- **Dharmakāya**: the Buddha's ultimate body is identical with the Dharma itself — the truth of dependent origination, emptiness, Buddha-nature. A text that expresses this truth *is* the Buddha's speech, regardless of when it was composed.
- **Nirmāṇakāya and visionary transmission**: several Mahāyāna traditions hold that texts were received through visionary encounters with Buddhas and Bodhisattvas — Nāgārjuna's reported retrieval of the Prajñāpāramitā from the nāgas, Asaṅga's reported reception of the *Yogācārabhūmi* from Maitreya. These are not fabrications to be debunked but **legitimate epistemological claims** within a Buddhist philosophy of mind that takes visionary states as valid knowledge sources.

Gómez takes these claims seriously — not as literally true but as **internally coherent** within a Buddhist epistemological framework that Western philology simply brackets as methodologically inconvenient.

### On the *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa* and Conceptual Innovation

Gómez has engaged with the *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa* as a case of **philosophical composition through narrative performance**. The text's famous "thunderous silence" — Vimalakīrti's wordless answer to the question of non-duality, which Mañjuśrī praises as the truest expression — is not a doctrinal claim that can be extracted from the narrative. The *silence* only means what it means **within the performance of the text**: it requires an audience that has followed the preceding thirty-one verbal answers, has been built up to expect a verbal culmination, and then encounters the caesura.

This is Gummer's argument in philosophical register: the text's meaning is **performatively constituted**, not propositionally extractable. You cannot summarise what the Vimalakīrti teaches about non-duality without the text — the teaching *is* the text-in-performance.

### Gómez on Pure Land Sūtras and Imaginative Practice

His most extended compositional analysis concerns the *Sukhāvatīvyūha* texts — the sūtras describing Amitābha Buddha's Pure Land. His argument:

**The Pure Land sūtras are compositional records of visualisation practice.** The extraordinarily elaborate descriptions of Sukhāvatī — its jewelled trees, its lotus ponds, its fragrant winds, its absence of the three lower realms — are not cosmological geography but **guided meditation scripts**. They tell the practitioner what to visualise; they were composed through the progressive refinement of visualisation practice; and the longer, more elaborate versions preserve **more developed stages of the practice** rather than being simple expansions of a simpler original.

This connects to the *Pratyutpannasamādhi Sūtra* — arguably the earliest Mahāyāna sūtra, centred on the *pratyutpanna* samādhi of "Buddha-standing-before-one" — in which practitioners meditate continuously for 90 days until Amitābha Buddha appears before them. Gómez reads this as evidence that **visionary encounter with Buddhas through samādhi practice** was a generative source of Mahāyāna sūtra composition — the texts emerged from meditative states, not from literary invention.

This is a remarkable inversion of the usual story: rather than sūtras producing meditation practice, **meditation practice produced sūtras**.

### Gómez's Methodological Self-Awareness

What distinguishes Gómez from many Buddhist Studies scholars is his consistent reflection on the **epistemological position of the scholar**. In essays on methodology he has argued:

- The Western academic scholar who brackets Buddhist truth-claims as methodologically irrelevant is not occupying a neutral position but a **specifically secular, post-Enlightenment** one
- This position has genuine scholarly virtues (critical distance, historical contextualisation) but also genuine scholarly vices (systematic inability to understand what practitioners understand themselves to be doing)
- A fully adequate Buddhist Studies would require something like **phenomenological empathy** alongside critical-historical method — not belief, but the capacity to inhabit the intentional structure of Buddhist practice from the inside

This is a position between Sharf's constructivism (which Gómez finds too dismissive of practitioners' claims) and perennialism (which he finds philosophically naive). He doesn't fully resolve it — but naming it as an unresolved tension is itself a significant methodological contribution.

## IV. Triangulating Gummer and Gómez Against Schopen

The Schopen problem: inscriptional silence about Mahāyāna.

Gummer's answer: **Mahāyāna's community is textually constituted** — it leaves no inscriptional trace because it is not organised around stūpas and monuments but around text-performance events. The dharmabhāṇaka is the institutional node; the recitation occasion is the congregational form; the text's merit-claims are the soteriological warrant.

Gómez's answer: **Mahāyāna's origin is meditatively constituted** — the texts emerged from and for visionary-samādhi practice, which by definition leaves no archaeological trace. The absence from inscriptions reflects the private, interior, retreat-based character of the practice communities that generated these texts.

These answers are **complementary rather than competing**: some Mahāyāna texts (especially Prajñāpāramitā) fit Gummer's dharmabhāṇaka model; others (Pure Land, early *pratyutpanna* texts) fit Gómez's visionary-samādhi model. The diversity of Mahāyāna sūtra literature may reflect diversity in originating practice communities.

Both answers also **implicitly rehabilitate** what Schopen's critique delegitimises: the text. Schopen argued that relying on texts to reconstruct Buddhist practice is methodologically naive. Gummer and Gómez agree — but conclude not that texts should be abandoned in favour of epigraphy, but that texts should be read **differently**: as performative events and practice records rather than doctrinal treatises.

## V. Implications for the Broader Debate

### Against the Protestant-Textualist Reading in Both Directions

Schopen criticised Western Buddhist Studies for privileging text over material culture — a Protestant presupposition. But his corrective (privilege epigraphy and archaeology) risks replacing one form of evidentiary essentialism with another. Gummer and Gómez offer a third position: **texts are material culture**. A manuscript is a physical object handled in ritual; a recitation is a sonic event in a social space; a copying practice is a bodily and meritorious act. The text/material-culture binary dissolves when you read texts as Gummer does.

### Against Sharf's Constructivism — Partially

Sharf argues that Buddhist "experience" is always already scripted by doctrinal and institutional frameworks — there is no unmediated phenomenal given. Gómez partially agrees but insists this doesn't mean meditators' reports are **merely** institutional rhetoric. His visionary-composition hypothesis requires that something happens in deep samādhi that is not simply the reproduction of prior doctrinal content — that the meditative encounter with Amitābha in *pratyutpanna* samādhi is a **genuine cognitive event** (however culturally conditioned its form) and not purely performative self-description.

This is the most philosophically unresolved tension in the literature: between strong constructivism (Sharf) and weak constructivism (Gómez) about meditative experience. Evan Thompson's neurophenomenology enters here as a potential bridge — but that's the next node in the map.

### For the Taishō Problem

Gummer's analysis has direct implications for the Taishō's editorial decisions. If Mahāyāna sūtras are performance traditions that were progressively textualised, then the **Taishō's stabilisation of a single text** from multiple manuscript recensions is not just an editorial convenience but an **ontological violence**: it converts a living performance tradition into a fixed object, erasing the variability that is constitutive of the text's identity.

The *Heart Sūtra* in the Taishō is T. 251 — a single, paginated, fixed text. But the Heart Sūtra exists in multiple recensions (longer and shorter Sanskrit versions, multiple Chinese translations, Tibetan versions) that differ in ways that matter for both doctrinal and ritual analysis. The Taishō's T. 251 is Xuanzang's 7th-century Chinese translation — chosen as canonical by the editorial apparatus. Every subsequent recitation of the Heart Sūtra in East Asian Buddhist ritual is a performance of the Taishō's editorial choice, not of "the Heart Sūtra."

#PostCriticalBuddhism #EpistemicReconstruction #BuddhismAfterDeconstruction
🔭 Lens: Reconstruction under critique — what can be responsibly affirmed about Buddhist knowledge after McMahan's genealogy, Schopen's archaeology, Sharf's constructivism, and the canon-formation problems have been fully absorbed

---

## I. Stating the Problem Precisely

Five turns of critique have established:

- "Original Buddhism" is a 19th-century philological construction (Schopen, McMahan)
- Buddhist modernism is a colonial-Romantic hybrid, not a recovery (McMahan)
- Meditative experience is institutionally scripted, not phenomenologically transparent (Sharf)
- The primary dataset of East Asian Buddhist Studies carries Meiji nationalist freight (Taishō)
- Even the critique of these problems is conducted in Western theoretical idioms that reproduce the authority structures being critiqued
- Meditation-centred Buddhism is approximately 100 years old and has identifiable inventors
- Mahāyāna sūtras are performative events and practice records, not doctrinal treatises

The question is: **what survives this?**

Three inadequate responses must be avoided upfront:

**Naive restoration**: "The critique has gone too far; traditional Buddhism simply *is* valid." This ignores what the critique actually established — you cannot unknow the Taishō's politics or Ledi Sayadaw's innovation date.

**Nihilistic dissolution**: "Buddhism is just ideology all the way down; nothing epistemically significant remains." This overclaims from the critique — showing that a knowledge tradition is historically constructed does not show it is false or empty.

**Pragmatist deflection**: "It works therapeutically, so the epistemic questions don't matter." This is MBSR's implicit position and it is philosophically evasive — therapeutic efficacy and epistemic validity are orthogonal questions.

What is needed is a **post-critical epistemology** — one that has genuinely absorbed the critique and asks what forms of Buddhist knowing remain defensible, in what register, and with what qualifications.

---

## II. Georges Dreyfus and the Pramāṇa Tradition — Formal Epistemology as Resource

### What Pramāṇa Offers

The Buddhist **pramāṇa** tradition — valid cognition theory — developed from Dignāga (5th century CE) through Dharmakīrti (7th century) into an extraordinarily sophisticated epistemological apparatus. Dreyfus's *Recognizing Reality: Dharmakīrti's Philosophy and Its Tibetan Interpretations* (1997) is the primary scholarly entry point.

The pramāṇa framework asks: what constitutes a **valid source of knowledge** (*pramāṇa*)? Dharmakīrti's answer: **perception** (*pratyakṣa*) and **inference** (*anumāna*). Everything else — testimony, scripture, tradition — is epistemically derivative and requires grounding in one of these two.

This is significant because it means the Buddhist philosophical tradition **already contains an internal critical epistemology** that does not rely on the authority of tradition or scripture as a first-order warrant. The Buddha's teaching is not valid because he said it; it is valid if and only if it accurately describes the causal structure of experience (*arthakriyā* — causal efficacy as the criterion of the real).

### Dharmakīrti's Naturalism

Dharmakīrti's epistemology is in important respects **naturalistic**: knowledge is grounded in the causal relationship between cognising mind and its object. Valid perception is produced by a reliable causal process; valid inference tracks real causal regularities. This is not identical to Western scientific naturalism but is structurally compatible with it in ways that allow genuine dialogue.

Dreyfus traces how Tibetan interpreters of Dharmakīrti diverge sharply: the **Gelug school** (Tsongkhapa's tradition) reads Dharmakīrti as compatible with Mādhyamika metaphysics — ultimately real entities do not exist, but conventionally valid cognition tracks conventional regularities reliably. The **Sakya school** reads Dharmakīrti as committed to a form of external realism. These disagreements map onto live debates in contemporary philosophy of mind and epistemology.

### What This Offers Post-Critical Buddhist Epistemology

The pramāṇa tradition offers a **self-critical internal standard**: Buddhist knowledge claims can be evaluated against criteria generated within Buddhist philosophy itself, without either deferring to tradition authority or capitulating to Western secular epistemology as the sole arbiter.

Specifically:
- Claims about the nature of mind and experience can be evaluated through **systematic phenomenological investigation** checked against intersubjective verification — roughly what the Abhidharma attempted, with its detailed phenomenological taxonomy
- Claims about causal regularities (karma, dependent origination) can be evaluated through **inferential reasoning** about whether they accurately describe the structure of experienced reality
- Claims grounded solely in **scriptural authority** (*āgama*) are epistemically secondary — the tradition itself says so

This means McMahan's critique of Buddhist modernism's "perennialist experience" claim does not undermine Buddhist epistemology as such — it only undermines the specific, Western-Romantic form of experience-appeal that Buddhist modernism deploys. The pramāṇa tradition's account of valid perception is a far more rigorous epistemological framework than anything in the perennialist school.

## III. Evan Thompson — Neurophenomenology as Bridge

### *Waking, Dreaming, Being* (2015) — The Argument

Thompson's project attempts to hold together:

- **Husserlian phenomenology** — first-person analysis of the structure of experience
- **Cognitive science and neuroscience** — third-person analysis of brain-mind processes
- **Buddhist philosophy of mind** — particularly Yogācāra, Madhyamaka, and Indo-Tibetan analyses of consciousness

His central epistemological claim: **consciousness cannot be adequately understood from the third-person perspective alone**. The "hard problem of consciousness" — why there is subjective experience at all — is not solvable by neuroscience operating exclusively in the objectivist register. First-person methods are **methodologically necessary**, not merely supplementary.

This directly addresses Sharf's constructivism: Sharf argues that meditators' reports are always already scripted by institutional frameworks and therefore cannot serve as transparent evidence of underlying states. Thompson agrees that reports are theory-laden — but argues this does not mean there is **nothing** being reported beyond the institutional script. The methodological challenge is to develop rigorous first-person methods that can distinguish genuine phenomenological findings from mere doctrinal projection.

### The Neurophenomenological Programme

Following **Francisco Varela** (*The Embodied Mind*, 1991, with Thompson and Eleanor Rosch), Thompson argues for **mutual constraint** between phenomenological description and neuroscientific explanation: rigorous first-person accounts of meditative states should inform and constrain neuroscientific hypotheses, while neuroscientific findings should inform phenomenological analysis.

This is more epistemologically demanding than both the contemplative neuroscience programme (which tends to use self-report as a correlate rather than a constraint) and Sharf's constructivism (which abandons first-person evidence entirely).

**Concretely**: when a meditator trained in *śamatha* practice reports a specific progression of attentional stability — sustained attention → interrupted attention → moments of clarity → stable clarity — this phenomenological sequence should generate specific neuroscientific predictions (about default mode network suppression, attentional network recruitment, etc.) that can be tested. If the phenomenological sequence and the neural sequence correlate structurally, this is evidence that the meditator is tracking something real, not merely reciting a doctrinal script.

### Where Thompson Departs from Sharf

Sharf's strong claim: meditative experience reports are **primarily rhetorical** — they function to establish authority and validate lineage, not to accurately report phenomenal states.

Thompson's response: this may be true of the **institutional context** of report-giving (the teacher-student interview, the lineage-validation ceremony) without being true of the **phenomenal states themselves**. The fact that reports are institutionally deployed for authority-purposes does not entail that the states being reported are themselves institutionally constructed.

This is a philosophically important distinction. Sharf collapses the **report** and the **reported state** in a way that Thompson argues is not warranted. The sociology of knowledge about meditative experience (how reports function institutionally) is a different question from the phenomenology of meditative experience (what meditators are actually undergoing).

### Thompson's Limitations

- His engagement with Buddhist textual scholarship is sophisticated but not always historically current — he sometimes relies on translations and secondary sources that a specialist would qualify
- The neurophenomenological programme remains more programmatic than achieved — the mutual constraint between phenomenology and neuroscience has been demonstrated in limited domains (visual perception, basic attention) but not yet in the deeper samādhi states where Buddhist claims are most distinctive
- His account of **self and no-self** — probably the book's most original philosophical contribution — accepts a Buddhist-inflected minimal-self view without fully engaging the Abhidharma-Yogācāra debate about the precise nature of the mental stream (*santāna*) that the no-self doctrine is about

## IV. Analayo — Phenomenological Philology as Constructive Method

### The Position

**Bhikkhu Analayo** represents a different constructive response: rigorous philological scholarship combined with committed practice, producing what might be called **phenomenological philology** — using textual analysis to recover the experiential structure of early Buddhist practice, while using practice to inform textual interpretation.

His *Satipaṭṭhāna: The Direct Path to Realization* (2003) and *Perspectives on Satipaṭṭhāna* (2013) work through the early meditation texts in Pāli and Chinese with extraordinary care, comparing parallel versions across the Nikāyas and Āgamas to identify what is early, stable, and cross-traditional.

### What Analayo Recovers

Against Sharf's claim that "bare attention" is a 20th-century Burmese innovation, Analayo argues:

- The **structure** of *satipaṭṭhāna* practice as described across multiple independent textual traditions (Pāli Theravāda, Chinese Sarvāstivāda, Chinese Dharmaguptaka) is sufficiently consistent to be a reliable indicator of early practice
- The specific **innovations** of Mahasi Sayadaw (the noting technique, the dry-insight shortcut, the intensive retreat format) can be distinguished from this early structural core without the entire practice collapsing into 20th-century construction
- What remains after the critical sifting: a practice of **sustained, non-reactive awareness** directed systematically at body, feeling-tone, mind-states, and dhammas — not identical to MBSR's "present-moment awareness" but also not reducible to Abhidhammic doctrinal projection

This is post-critical reconstruction in action: using the critique (Sharf's historical debunking of specific techniques) to refine rather than abandon the practice framework.

### Methodological Transparency

Analayo's method is notable for its **explicit acknowledgment of the hermeneutical circle**: he is both a scholar and a practitioner, and he does not pretend these perspectives are separable. His practice informs which textual features seem phenomenologically significant; his scholarship disciplines his practice-based intuitions against alternative readings. Rather than treating this as a methodological contamination, he presents it as a form of **reflective equilibrium** — the same epistemological structure that Rawls uses in ethics and that Dreyfus uses in his Tibetan pramāṇa work.

## V. Jay Garfield and the Madhyamaka Option

### Why Madhyamaka is Epistemically Resilient

Of all Buddhist philosophical positions, **Madhyamaka** — Nāgārjuna's two-truths framework — is most resistant to the critiques surveyed in this conversation. Here is why:

McMahan's critique targets Buddhist modernism's **substantialist** moves: positing an essential Buddhist experience, a timeless Dharma, a perennial wisdom. Madhyamaka explicitly denies the existence of any such essence — **śūnyatā** (emptiness) means precisely that no phenomenon, including "Buddhism" or "meditative experience," has intrinsic existence independent of causal conditions and conceptual designation.

Schopen's critique targets the **privileging of text over material practice**. Madhyamaka, properly understood, has no investment in privileging any particular epistemic medium — texts, practices, and material objects are equally conventionally real and ultimately empty.

Sharf's critique targets **experiential essentialism** — the claim that meditation accesses a pre-conceptual, transhistorically valid experience. Madhyamaka's Prāsaṅgika wing (Candrakīrti, Tsongkhapa) explicitly denies that awakening involves access to a non-conceptual absolute — ultimate truth is **not** an experience of the Absolute but the recognition of the absence of intrinsic existence in all phenomena, including the experiencing subject.

**Jay Garfield's** work — particularly *The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way* (1995, translation and commentary on Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*) and his collaborative work with **Graham Priest** on dialetheism — makes Madhyamaka available to analytic philosophy while preserving its critical edge.

### The Two-Truths Framework as Epistemological Architecture

Garfield argues the two-truths distinction offers a **sophisticated epistemological architecture** that dissolves many apparent contradictions in Buddhist knowledge claims:

- **Conventionally**: Buddhist practices (meditation, ethics, ritual, community) are causally efficacious — they produce genuine transformations in practitioners' lives and relationships. This is evaluable empirically and phenomenologically.
- **Ultimately**: none of these practices, transformations, or the practitioners undergoing them have intrinsic existence. The post-critical Buddhist epistemology is itself empty of self-existence — it does not constitute a new foundation.

This means the genealogical critique (McMahan) is **conventionally valid**: yes, contemporary meditation practice has the historical origins Ledi Sayadaw and Mahasi gave it; yes, Buddhist modernism is a colonial-Romantic hybrid. But this conventional-historical truth does not undermine the conventional causal efficacy of the practices. The emptiness of intrinsic existence does not mean causal inefficacy — that is the Madhyamaka's most careful distinction.

### The Ethical Implication — Śāntideva

**Śāntideva's** *Bodhicaryāvatāra* (8th century) remains, after all the critique, a remarkably durable document — and Garfield's commentary on it (*A Guide to the Bodhisattva's Way of Life*, with Vesna Wallace) shows why.

Śāntideva's ethical argument does not depend on:
- A substantialist account of the self being harmed or benefited
- A perennialist account of meditative experience
- A canonical authority whose textual authenticity must be established
- A pre-critical account of Buddhist history

It depends on: **the logical structure of the relationship between self and other, given the absence of intrinsic self-existence**. If the self has no intrinsic boundary that distinguishes it from others (since it has no intrinsic existence), then the asymmetric privileging of self-interest over other-interest is without rational foundation. This is an **argument**, not an appeal to tradition or experience — and it survives the critique intact.

## VI. What Specifically Survives — A Positive Account

Drawing the strands together, here is what a post-critical Buddhist epistemology can responsibly claim:

### 1. The Phenomenological Programme — With Qualification

Systematic first-person investigation of mind and experience, conducted with rigorous method and intersubjective checking, **can produce reliable knowledge** about the structure of experience. This is not the perennialist claim that all meditators access the same pre-conceptual absolute. It is the more modest claim that:

- Attention can be trained
- Trained attention reveals features of experience not accessible to untrained attention
- Some of these features (impermanence of mental events, constructed nature of self-sense, conditioned arising of emotional states) are cross-culturally stable findings, not doctrinal projections

The **qualification**: Sharf is right that specific technique-generated experiences (Mahasi's insight knowledges, Zen kenshō) are heavily institutionally scripted. The post-critical position acknowledges this while maintaining that scripting does not exhaust what is happening. Thompson's mutual-constraint methodology is the appropriate corrective.

### 2. Dependent Origination as Structural Claim

*Pratītyasamutpāda* — the arising of phenomena in dependence on conditions — is not primarily a metaphysical doctrine requiring scriptural authority. It is a **structural claim about causality** that is:

- Formally precise in the Abhidharma and Madhyamaka elaborations
- Compatible with and in some formulations entailed by contemporary philosophy of causation
- Verifiable in principle through both phenomenological and empirical investigation
- Not dependent on the Taishō's editorial choices or Ledi Sayadaw's 20th-century innovations

Thich Nhat Hanh's **interbeing** and Garfield's **dependence-only ontology** are contemporary philosophical reformulations that preserve the structural insight while abandoning the cosmological elaborations that Schopen's archaeology shows were always contested and variable.

### 3. The No-Self Claim — Carefully Delimited

The *anātman* claim — that no phenomenon, including the person, has a substantial, independent, unchanging self — survives in the following form:

**Phenomenologically**: the sense of a unified, continuous, authorial self is not a transparent report of a metaphysical fact but a **constructed cognitive phenomenon** — this is supported by both careful introspection and by contemporary cognitive science (Metzinger's *Being No One*, Parfit's *Reasons and Persons*, Albahari's *Analytical Buddhism*)

**Ethically**: Śāntideva's argument that the self/other distinction, lacking metaphysical foundation, cannot ground asymmetric self-preference survives

**What does not survive uncritically**: the specific Abhidharmic account of what replaces the substantial self (the stream of momentary *dharmas*) — this is a theoretical construction that carries its own problems and requires the same critical treatment as any other Buddhist doctrinal elaboration

### 4. Ethics Without Metaphysical Foundation

Charles Goodman's *Consequences of Compassion* (2009) argues that **Buddhist ethics — specifically consequentialist-oriented forms grounded in the reduction of suffering** — does not depend on contested metaphysical commitments about karma, rebirth, or Buddha-nature. It depends on:

- The recognition that suffering is real (even on a minimalist, constructivist account)
- The recognition that actions have causal consequences for suffering (dependent origination)
- The cultivation of dispositions (*brahmaviharā*: loving-kindness, compassion, equanimity, appreciative joy) that reliably reduce suffering

This is a **post-critical Buddhist ethics** that survives McMahan's genealogy intact — not because it appeals to traditional authority but because it rests on claims that are independently evaluable.

### 5. Community and Practice as Constitutive — Not Merely Instrumental

Gummer's analysis of sūtra-as-performance has an epistemological implication that tends to be lost in the critique: **Buddhist knowledge is not primarily propositional knowledge held by individuals**. It is **practice knowledge** — knowing-how rather than knowing-that — embedded in communities, transmitted through embodied apprenticeship, and constituted in the performance of shared practices.

This means the post-critical epistemology cannot be solely a matter of salvaging correct propositions from the wreckage of deconstructed tradition. It requires attending to **what forms of community and practice** reliably transmit genuine transformation — a question that is simultaneously sociological, phenomenological, and ethical.

Alasdair MacIntyre's concept of a **tradition-constituted rationality** (*Whose Justice? Which Rationality?*, 1988) — which he developed in a Western context but which maps surprisingly well onto Buddhist epistemology — offers a framework: a living tradition is one that has the internal resources to identify its own inadequacies and correct them over time. Post-critical Buddhism is a tradition engaged in exactly this — the critique is internal, not external destruction.

## VII. What Does Not Survive

Being precise about the losses is as important as the reconstruction:

- **Perennialism**: the claim that all mystical traditions share a common experiential core — gone. McMahan and Sharf together establish this is a Romantic projection.
- **Textual essentialism**: the claim that "original Buddhism" can be recovered from canonical texts with scholarly precision — gone. Schopen's archaeology and the Taishō critique together establish this.
- **Experiential foundationalism**: the claim that meditation provides unmediated access to reality prior to conceptual construction — gone in its strong form. Thompson's weak constructivism is the post-critical ceiling.
- **Cosmological literalism**: rebirth in specific realms, cosmological Buddhas, celestial bodhisattvas as literally existing beings — these require a metaphysical commitment that the post-critical framework cannot sustain without independent argument. They may be preserved as **regulative ideals**, **practice objects**, or **skillful means** (*upāya*) — but not as straightforwardly factual claims.
- **The authority of lineage as such**: the mere fact of transmission through a recognised lineage is not an epistemological warrant. Lineage matters sociologically and practically — it structures the transmission of practice knowledge — but it does not guarantee correctness.

## VIII. The Residual Tension — And Why It Should Not Be Resolved

The deepest tension in post-critical Buddhist epistemology is between:

**The constructivist ceiling** (Thompson, Gómez's weak constructivism): Buddhist knowledge is genuine but always already shaped by the conceptual and institutional frameworks within which it is produced and transmitted — there is no view from nowhere, and the post-critical epistemology is itself subject to this condition.

**The transformative claim** (Analayo, Garfield, Śāntideva): Buddhist practice produces genuine transformation — not merely different doctrinal framing of unchanged experience, but actual modification of how mind and world are encountered. This transformation cannot be fully captured in propositional form; it requires something like wisdom (*prajñā*) — a knowing that is simultaneously cognitive, affective, and dispositional.

This tension is **productive rather than destructive**. It is roughly the tension between **second-order epistemology** (how do we know what we know?) and **first-order transformation** (what happens when practice works?). The post-critical position holds both without collapsing one into the other — maintaining the critical vigilance of the constructivist ceiling while refusing to let it preclude genuine inquiry into what transformation is and how it occurs.

This is, in a recognisably Buddhist register, a form of the **Middle Way**: between the essentialism of traditional authority and the nihilism of pure deconstruction — not as a compromise between them but as a path that the critique itself, followed rigorously enough, opens.
