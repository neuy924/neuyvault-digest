## English abstract

This review article examines the **Fo shuo Shousheng jing** 佛說壽生經 as a Chinese apocryphal or para-canonical ritual scripture rather than as an Indian-translated Buddhist sūtra. Preserved in CBETA as **X01n0024 壽生經** without a secure translator attribution, the text constructs authority through Buddhist sūtra-form while advancing a distinctly Chinese ritual logic of birth-debt, underworld treasury accounting, paper-money repayment, and merit transfer. ([archive2.cbeta.org][1]) Its central claim is that human beings borrow **壽生錢** before or at birth and must ritually repay this debt to avoid misfortune, poverty, illness, bad rebirth, and underworld punishment. The article argues that the text bureaucratises karma: salvation is imagined less as awakening than as fiscal clearance within a cosmic administration. By comparing the scripture with Daoist **受生債** traditions, Chinese funerary paper-money practice, and modern “Repaying the Loan for Life” rituals documented in Changshu, the article shows how Buddhist, Daoist, and popular religious systems overlap in the ritual management of fate, family anxiety, and post-mortem risk. ([asianethnology.org][2])

## Review article summary

**Core argument:**
**佛說壽生經** is best read not as early Buddhist doctrine, but as a **Chinese apocryphal or para-canonical ritual scripture**. It uses Buddhist sūtra-form—**佛說**, Buddha/Ānanda dialogue, bodhisattvas, recitation merit, mantra, and canonical closure—to authorise a ritual economy of **受生債 / 壽生錢**, underworld treasuries, paper-money repayment, and post-mortem account settlement. CBETA preserves the text as **X01n0024 壽生經**, but its catalogue entry gives no translator or author attribution, which strengthens the case for treating it cautiously as non-securely translated scripture rather than an Indian Buddhist sūtra. ## Key themes

**1. Scriptural authority as performance**
The text does not merely claim authority; it performs authority by sounding like a sūtra. Its form is Buddhist even when its ritual logic is heavily Chinese.

**2. Life converted into debt**
The most powerful conceptual move is the conversion of birth into a loan. This changes existential uncertainty into a repayable obligation.

**3. Karma bureaucratised**
The text does not abolish karma, but it reframes karmic consequence through treasury offices, documents, account-keeping, and ritual repayment.

**4. Anxiety made administrable**
Illness, poverty, litigation, accidents, bad rebirth, and ancestral unrest are not left as vague fate. They become symptoms of unsettled accounts.

**5. Buddhist branding over shared ritual infrastructure**
The Buddhist layer is not fake, but it is not the whole system. The deeper machinery belongs to the shared field of Chinese Buddhist, Daoist, and popular ritual practice.

**6. Religious economy without simple cynicism**
A dark reading sees monetised fear. A more balanced reading sees ritualised care, filial duty, grief management, and communal handling of uncertainty.

**佛説壽生經 / 佛說壽生經** is best understood as a **Chinese popular religious Buddhist-apocryphal scripture**, not a securely Indian-translated Buddhist sūtra.

It is associated with the ritual idea of **還壽生 / 還受生債 / 還庫錢**: the belief that before birth a person “borrowed” a life-debt or spirit-money debt from an underworld or celestial treasury, and that this debt should be ritually repaid, often by scripture-recitation and burning spirit money. Berezkin describes this wider “repaying the loan for life” ritual as traceable to Chinese popular religion and Daoist scriptural foundations, with Buddhist adoption later. 

The text itself claims Buddhist authority by presenting itself as “spoken by the Buddha,” and some versions attach it to **Xuanzang / 唐三藏**, but scholars treat it as **apocryphal**. Berezkin explicitly notes that the *Fo shuo shou sheng jing* is “an apocryphal text not found in the Chinese Buddhist canon,” despite its claim of association with Xuanzang. 

Its core message is very transactional: if one repays the life-loan, one may gain longevity, avoid disasters, improve fortune, help ancestors, and escape negative underworld consequences. Berezkin summarizes that it promises escape from eighteen disasters, malicious stars, improved rebirth fortune, and cleansing ancestral sins. 

The closest conceptual background is probably **Daoist 受生經 / shòushēng jīng** traditions, where each person receives a debt or allocation at birth from a celestial treasury, with amounts sometimes calculated by zodiac year or birth data. The “壽生” title also plays on **受生**, “receiving birth,” while making it sound like “longevity-life.” Berezkin notes Daoist scriptural bases dating around the twelfth to thirteenth centuries and explains the system of debts according to a twelve-year roster. 

So in plain terms: **it is a folk-Buddhist ritual text about repaying a pre-birth spiritual debt, closely tied to paper-money rites, longevity rituals, funerary practice, and Chinese religious economy. It should not be read as early Buddhist doctrine.**

**Critical takeaway:** it is useful evidence for **Chinese popular religion**, Buddhist–Daoist ritual borrowing, underworld bureaucracy, and merit-as-accounting logic; it is weak evidence for what “the Buddha taught.”

## The strongest thesis directions

**1. 佛說壽生經 as religious accounting**
The text translates birth, fortune, illness, and death into a debt-ledger model. This is probably the most analytically powerful frame.

**2. 佛說壽生經 as Buddhist branding over Daoist-popular infrastructure**
The Buddhist title, Buddha-frame, bodhisattva names, and sūtra ending give legitimacy to a ritual logic whose deeper structure is Daoist/popular Chinese: treasury, officials, astral fate, paper money, and underworld repayment.

**3. 佛說壽生經 as a technology of filial piety**
The ritual lets children materially demonstrate care for parents before or after death. It converts filial anxiety into a visible, communal, ritually auditable act.

**4. 佛說壽生經 as a popular alternative to elite Buddhist doctrine**
Instead of emptiness, non-self, or liberation from saṃsāra, it promises longevity, money, protection, rebirth advantage, and disaster avoidance. That tension is revealing.

**5. 佛說壽生經 as bureaucratic cosmology**
The cosmos is imagined like an imperial state: offices, documents, accounts, treasuries, officials, debts, petitions, and penalties.

## Preamble

The weakest assumption to avoid is that **佛說壽生經** is simply “a Buddhist sūtra with folk elements.” The better reading is sharper: it is a **Chinese ritual scripture that borrows Buddhist scriptural authority to legitimate a birth-debt repayment system**. Its world is not primarily early Buddhist karma, renunciation, or liberation; it is a bureaucratic cosmos of ledgers, treasury officials, paper-money repayment, astral fate, family debt, and post-mortem risk.

CBETA preserves the text as **X01n0024 壽生經**, one fascicle, but its catalogue line gives no translator or author attribution, unlike many translated Buddhist scriptures nearby in the same list. ([archive2.cbeta.org][1]) The text itself claims Buddhist authority through the title **佛說壽生經** and an opening connection to **唐三藏法師**, but that internal claim is exactly what needs scrutiny, not acceptance. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

## 1. Textual identification

**佛說壽生經** is a short Chinese ritual scripture preserved in CBETA as **No. 24 佛說壽生經**. The CBETA PDF states that the ebook is based on **CBETA 電子佛典集成 Version 2023.Q4**, and the text begins at “No. 24 佛說壽生經.” ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2]) The CBETA catalogue update lists **X01n0024 壽生經(1卷)〖〗**, with the attribution field blank. ([archive2.cbeta.org][1])

That blank attribution matters. A securely translated Indian Buddhist sūtra normally comes with a translator, dynasty, or transmission claim. This text has instead a **self-legitimating narrative frame**: it says that in Zhenguan 13, Tang Sanzang found this scripture while inspecting the canon. The relevant fragment is:

> 「唐三藏法師」
> “The Tang Tripiṭaka Master.”
> Function: invokes Xuanzang-like prestige.
> Register: Buddhist-authorising, but historically suspicious. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

This is a classic apocryphal move: the text does not merely teach; it first tells you why it deserves to be treated as scripture.

Modern scholarship on Chinese Buddhist apocrypha has shown that many Chinese Buddhist scriptures were not translations from Indian originals but were composed in China, often responding to Chinese social, ritual, and religious needs. Buswell’s *Chinese Buddhist Apocrypha* is explicitly framed around this problem: indigenous Chinese scriptures reshape how we understand “canon” as a fluctuating institution rather than a fixed container of Indian texts. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][3])

## 2. Close reading of key passages

### Opening authority frame

The title itself is already doing work:

> 「佛說壽生經」
> “The Scripture on Life/Longevity Spoken by the Buddha.”
> Function: places the text under direct Buddha-speech authority.
> Register: Buddhist canonical style. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

The problem is that the contents rapidly move into a distinctly Chinese bureaucratic and ritual economy. The Buddha-title gives authority; the body gives a Chinese debt-and-treasury cosmology.

### Birth-debt claim

The core doctrinal claim is compressed in the opening passage:

> 「各借壽生錢」
> “Each borrows life-money.”
> Function: defines birth as debt.
> Register: bureaucratic, popular-religious, not early Buddhist doctrinal vocabulary. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

The surrounding passage says people born in **南贍部洲** first borrow **壽生錢** from the **冥司**, the underworld administration, and are then urged to repay it. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2]) This is not karma as impersonal moral causality; it is karma translated into an account ledger.

### Repayment mechanism

The text links repayment to recitation and scriptural efficacy:

> 「金剛經壽生經」
> “The Diamond Sūtra and the Shousheng Scripture.”
> Function: folds the ritual into Buddhist recitation culture.
> Register: Buddhist surface, ritual-accounting function. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

The pairing with the **金剛經** is strategic. It borrows the prestige of a canonical Mahāyāna scripture and attaches the **壽生經** to its merit economy.

### Disaster catalogue

The text promises avoidance of **一十八般橫災**, including travel danger, storms, drowning, fire, bloodshed, childbirth danger, sudden death, epidemic illness, suicide, and litigation. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2]) This list is not metaphysical abstraction; it is a catalogue of ordinary social fear.

The rhetoric is direct: repay, and diffuse anxiety becomes manageable.

### 壽生 and 受生

The text itself gives the philological clue:

> 「又名受生經」
> “Also called the Scripture of Receiving Birth.”
> Function: exposes the title’s semantic hinge.
> Register: philological bridge between birth and longevity. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

This is important. **受生** means receiving birth, being born into embodied existence. **壽生** shifts the ear toward longevity, life-extension, and good fortune. The text appears to exploit both meanings: the debt belongs to birth, but the ritual payoff is life, longevity, protection, and prosperity.

### Treasury and official language

The text’s most revealing phrase is:

> 「庫官收付」
> “Treasury officials receive and disburse.”
> Function: turns salvation into administrative clearance.
> Register: bureaucratic-underworld, not early Buddhist. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

The text imagines a cosmic treasury in which debts can be recorded, paid, held, and used after death. It is a religious ledger system.

### Prosperity promise

The transactional logic culminates here:

> 「三世富貴」
> “Wealth and honour for three lives.”
> Function: converts ritual payment into multi-life benefit.
> Register: popular-religious prosperity logic. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

This is where the text departs most clearly from elite Buddhist soteriology. It is not primarily promising nirvāṇa; it is promising protection, wealth, bodily wholeness, good rebirth, and escape from hell.

## 3. Analysis by the five angles

## Angle 1 — Apocryphal authority

The text constructs authority in four layers.

First, the title formula **佛說** says the Buddha himself spoke it. Second, it uses a Buddha–Ānanda dialogue structure, which imitates Buddhist sūtra convention. Third, it invokes familiar Buddhist actors: bodhisattvas, Guanyin, heavenly dragons, and the closing formula **信受奉行**. Fourth, it ties itself to **唐三藏法師**, implying contact with Xuanzang’s canonical world. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

But the external bibliographic evidence is weak: CBETA’s catalogue line gives **壽生經(1卷)〖〗**, with no translator/author attribution. ([archive2.cbeta.org][1]) That does not alone prove forgery, but it strongly supports treating the text as **apocryphal or para-canonical** rather than as an Indian-translated sūtra.

Best label: **folk-Buddhist apocryphal ritual scripture**. “Pseudo-sūtra” is accurate but dismissive. “Buddhist scripture” is too generous unless qualified. “Ritual manual” captures function but misses its claim to sūtra-status.

## Angle 2 — 壽生 versus 受生

The text’s self-identification as **又名受生經** is the hinge. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

**受生** points to the moment of embodiment: receiving birth, entering human life, being assigned fate. **壽生** reframes the same debt as a matter of longevity and life-blessing. That shift is not trivial. It changes the emotional target:

**受生債** asks: “What debt did I incur by being born?”
**壽生錢** asks: “What payment secures my life, fortune, and longevity?”

[INFERENCE] The title shift likely makes the doctrine more ritually marketable. “Birth-debt” is metaphysical and ominous; “longevity-life money” is protective, hopeful, and suitable for birthdays, illness rituals, and pre-mortem repayment.

The Daoist parallel is explicit in **靈寶天尊說祿庫受生經**, which Daoist reference sources describe as a one-fascicle scripture of uncertain authorship, probably by Sui–Tang Daoists, teaching that beings are under heavenly and underworld jurisdiction and borrow **祿庫受生錢財** from the underworld before human birth. ([Dao Info][4])

## Angle 3 — Life as debt-accounting

The cosmology is brutally clear: to be born is to have borrowed.

Berezkin’s study of the Changshu ritual states that the ceremony is related to a belief in a **“Loan for Life,” contracted by each person at birth**, which must be repaid to the underworld treasury. He also notes that the foundations of the ritual are located in medieval Daoist scriptures, while its later form is associated with baojuan performance in the Lower Yangtze region. ([University of Leipzig][5])

This differs from classical Buddhist karma in three ways:

| Classical Buddhist karma                    | 壽生 / 受生 debt logic                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Moral causality across intention and action | Financial-bureaucratic liability                                         |
| No cosmic accountant is strictly required   | Underworld offices, treasuries, officials                                |
| Liberation means ending craving and saṃsāra | Relief means repayment, clearance, protection                            |
| Merit may support rebirth                   | Payment directly alters fortune, illness risk, and post-mortem condition |

This does not mean the text has no Buddhist content. It uses Buddhist recitation, merit, bodhisattvas, hell-avoidance, and rebirth vocabulary. But the deeper mechanism is **administrative settlement**, not awakening.

## Angle 4 — Buddhist–Daoist hybridity

The Buddhist elements are obvious: **佛說**, Buddha, Ānanda, bodhisattvas, Guanyin, Diamond Sūtra, mantra, and **信受奉行**. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][2])

The Daoist/popular elements are equally obvious: **冥司**, treasury officials, astral stars, birth allotments, ledgers, repayment, burning money, and the underworld fiscal system. The Daoist **靈寶天尊說祿庫受生經** gives a much fuller account of this structure: beings are linked to heaven and underworld, borrow **祿庫受生錢財**, and suffer poverty or misfortune when debts are unpaid. ([Wikisource][6]) It also assigns debts by zodiac/branch-linked treasuries and officials, a strongly bureaucratic-ritual structure. ([Chinese Text Project][7])

So “syncretism” is not wrong, but it is too soft. A better model is **layered ritual authority**:

1. **Daoist/popular infrastructure** supplies treasury, fate, astral officials, and repayment logic.
2. **Buddhist scriptural voice** supplies Buddha-authority, recitation merit, bodhisattvas, and hell/rebirth language.
3. **Local ritual specialists** operationalise the rite through paper money, documents, altars, and performance.

## Angle 5 — Ritual economy

The text converts metaphysical anxiety into a concrete payment ritual.

Berezkin’s Changshu fieldwork describes ritual assemblies involving scripture-telling, altars, ritual documents, ledgers, envelopes, boxes for repaying the loan, and a treasury that is burned at the end of the rite. He notes that ritual teams prepare spirit-money burned as symbolic repayment of the host’s otherworldly debt. ([JSTOR][8])

This fits the larger Chinese paper-money system. Fred Blake’s *Burning Money* is described as a study of the custom, practiced for a thousand years, of burning paper replicas of valuable things—especially money—for deceased family members, ancestors, demons, and divinities, with attention to the cultural logic of value. ([University of Leipzig][9])

The **壽生經** version is more specific than ordinary ancestral paper money. It is not merely “send money to the dead.” It is: settle a debt contracted at birth, recorded by unseen officials, affecting present fortune and future rebirth.

The rite therefore functions as:

| Function               | How it works                                                       |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Protection             | Avoids named disasters and illnesses                               |
| Longevity ritual       | Reframes debt repayment as life-extension                          |
| Filial care            | Descendants may sponsor repayment for parents or ancestors         |
| Funeral aid            | Money and scripture help the dead pass bureaucratic danger         |
| Spiritual insurance    | A present payment reduces uncertain future risk                    |
| Ritual service economy | Specialists mediate documents, recitation, burning, and legitimacy |

The darker reading is that the ritual monetises fear. The charitable reading is that it gives families a socially intelligible way to care for the living, dying, and dead when fate feels opaque. Both readings are plausible.

---

## 4. Comparative analysis

### Compared with Buddhist karma

Buddhist karma is not absent, but it is subordinated. The text mentions sin, hell, rebirth, and merit, but its operative logic is not “transform intention and wisdom.” It is “repay the account.” The difference is not cosmetic. It shifts religious agency from ethical cultivation to ritual clearance.

### Compared with Daoist 受生債 traditions

The Daoist comparison is very strong. **靈寶天尊說祿庫受生經** explicitly teaches that human beings borrow **祿庫受生錢財** from the underworld at the time of receiving human birth, and that repayment through ritual practice affects wealth, poverty, and future suffering. ([Dao Info][4]) Its text also describes twelve treasury officials and debt amounts assigned by birth category. ([Chinese Text Project][7])

That makes the Buddhist **佛說壽生經** look less like an independent Buddhist doctrine and more like a Buddhist appropriation or re-voicing of a Daoist/popular debt cosmology.

### Compared with Chinese funerary paper-money practice

Ordinary paper-money offerings send resources to ancestors or spirits. **壽生 / 受生** ritual adds a contractual layer: money is not merely support; it is repayment. The burning is not just offering; it is fiscal transfer. The ritual specialist is not just a chanter; they become a clerk, courier, auditor, and ritual accountant.

## 5. Red team review

**1. The Daoist-origin claim may be too strong.**
Berezkin says the ritual foundations are in medieval Daoist scriptures, but the available evidence may not prove a simple one-way derivation from Daoism to Buddhism. Popular religious motifs can circulate across Buddhist, Daoist, and local ritual worlds without a clean origin chain. ([University of Leipzig][5])

**2. “Apocryphal” can mislead.**
Calling the text apocryphal does not mean it was unimportant, insincere, or marginal in practice. Chinese Buddhist apocrypha often became powerful precisely because they addressed Chinese concerns more directly than translated scriptures. Buswell’s framing of apocrypha as a major field of Chinese Buddhist studies supports this caution. ([NTU Digital Library of Buddhist Studies][3])

**3. Ritual economy is not just crude monetisation.**
A purely cynical reading—“priests sell fear”—misses the social function. In practice, repayment rites can express filial piety, care for ageing parents, mourning, birthday celebration, communal performance, and anxiety management. Berezkin’s Changshu study treats the ritual as a complex performance system, not merely payment extraction. ([University of Leipzig][5])

**4. The text’s Buddhism should not be dismissed entirely.**
The Buddhist layer is not decorative only. Recitation, merit transfer, bodhisattva invocation, hell avoidance, and rebirth are genuinely Buddhist-shaped practices in Chinese religious life. The point is not “fake Buddhism,” but **Chinese Buddhism functioning inside a shared ritual marketplace**.

---

## Conclusion

**佛說壽生經** reveals a Chinese religious world where birth, fate, illness, poverty, death, and rebirth can be imagined through **credit, debt, treasury, officials, repayment, and ritual documentation**. Its Buddhist surface is real but not sufficient. The text’s deeper grammar is bureaucratic and transactional: human life begins with a loan, misfortune signals unsettled accounts, and ritual specialists help convert paper money, scripture recitation, and merit into cosmic repayment.

It does **not** tell us much about early Buddhism or Indian Buddhist doctrine. It tells us a great deal about Chinese popular religion, Buddhist apocryphal authority, Daoist fiscal cosmology, paper-money practice, and the ritual management of family anxiety.

**Review-article thesis:**
*佛說壽生經 is best read as a Chinese apocryphal ritual scripture in which Buddhist sūtra-form legitimates a Daoist-popular economy of birth-debt, underworld treasuries, paper-money repayment, and merit transfer. Its importance lies not in doctrinal orthodoxy, but in showing how Chinese religious communities transformed life itself into an auditable debt and salvation into ritual account-settlement.*

## Confidence

**High** that the text is best treated as Chinese apocryphal / para-canonical ritual scripture rather than an Indian-translated Buddhist sūtra.
**High** that its core logic is birth-debt repayment through underworld treasury accounting.
**Moderate** on the precise historical direction of influence between Daoist 受生 texts, Buddhist 壽生 framing, and local folk practice.
**Low-to-moderate** on dating the Buddhist **佛說壽生經** itself without a manuscript history.
