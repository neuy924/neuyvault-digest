## Ethnic Differences in Chemotherapy Toxicity Profiles: Asia vs. West

Substantial trial-based evidence demonstrates that ethnicity is a significant modifier of chemotherapy toxicity, driven primarily by pharmacogenomic differences in drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters. The field of **pharmacoethnicity** captures both genetic (enzyme polymorphisms, allele frequencies) and non-genetic (BMI, comorbidities, healthcare access) contributors to these disparities.[1][2][3][4]

***

## Overarching Mechanisms

The major pharmacogenomic drivers include differential frequencies of:[2][1]
- **UGT1A1 alleles**: *\*28* predominates in Caucasians/Africans; *\*6* predominates in East Asians — both reduce glucuronidation of SN-38 (irinotecan active metabolite)
- **DPYD variants**: Different loss-of-function alleles cause DPD deficiency across ethnicities, affecting 5-FU catabolism
- **CYP3A5 expression**: ~70% of African Americans express CYP3A5 vs. ~20% Caucasians, altering vincristine/taxane metabolism
- **CYP3A4/2B6 polymorphisms**: More prevalent in African Americans, affecting cyclophosphamide activation

## Key Clinical Implications

- **The JCOG 9511 vs. SWOG S0124 divergence** (irinotecan/cisplatin in SCLC) is a landmark example: the same regimen produced a survival benefit in Japanese patients but not North Americans, driven by different UGT1A1 allele distributions and platinum sensitivity.[9][11]
- **Docetaxel dosing** in Japan is approved at 60 mg/m², while Western practice uses 75–100 mg/m², yet Japanese patients still experience more severe neutropenia — illustrating that dose reduction alone does not fully resolve ethnic toxicity differences.[18]
- **Paclitaxel in Black women** with breast cancer warrants consideration of switching to docetaxel given the prospective EAZ171 trial data showing substantially lower TIPN rates.[28][30]
- **DPD testing** before 5-FU/capecitabine is mandated in Europe (EMA) for four DPYD variants relevant to Caucasians, but African-specific variants (e.g., Y186C) are not routinely captured, creating a gap for Black patients.[25][50]
- A major limitation across this evidence base is **underrepresentation of minorities** in pivotal trials — African Americans and Asians each represent <10% of many Western trial populations, meaning standard dosing recommendations are derived predominantly from Caucasian pharmacokinetic data.[3][4]

## Ethnic Differences in Toxicity of TKIs and Other Targeted Therapies

The evidence base for TKIs is now substantial, with a landmark 2025 systematic review covering 73 inter-ethnic efficacy and safety analyses across 15 TKIs showing that Asian patients have a consistently distinct toxicity signature for several agents — most notably crizotinib, brigatinib, pazopanib, and sunitinib. The pattern is characterised by increased hematologic toxicity, hepatotoxicity, and skin reactions in Asian patients, versus predominant GI toxicity in non-Asian patients.[1][2]

## Key Clinical Callouts

- **The T-DM1 thrombocytopenia gap is clinically critical**: a 10-fold difference in grade ≥3 TCP (20% Asians vs. 2% non-Asians) without any pharmacokinetic explanation — implying platelet biology differences that dose adjustment alone cannot predict.[30]
- **Osimertinib ILD in Japanese patients** (12.3% vs. 4% globally) is embedded in Japanese FDA-equivalent labelling, mandating monitoring within the first 4 weeks.[8]
- **Imatinib under-dosing in African patients** is a pharmacogenomically plausible but clinically under-studied issue — PBPK models suggest standard 400 mg may be subtherapeutic in this population, yet prospective dose-optimisation data remain sparse.[39]
- **No dosing label changes have yet been implemented** for any TKI based on ethnicity alone — a significant gap given the evidence base.[1]
- The 2025 systematic review across 15 TKIs concluded that **"no TKI prescribing information currently includes population-specific dosing recommendations"** despite consistent signals in Asian subgroups.[1]
