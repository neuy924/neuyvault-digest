## Analysis and Clinical Implications

## Differential Response Patterns

The Pieretti study represents the most direct examination of differential tumor response between primary and metastatic sites in mRCC patients receiving systemic therapy. Among 198 patients with metastatic ccRCC who underwent presurgical targeted therapy, immunotherapy, or combination treatment followed by deferred cytoreductive nephrectomy, distinct patterns emerged:[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9486901/)

- **Metastatic-predominant response:** Nearly one-third (31.31%) of patients demonstrated metastatic tumor shrinkage ≥10% without equivalent primary tumor response
- **Primary-predominant response:** Only 8.08% showed primary tumor shrinkage ≥10% without metastatic response
- **Concordant response:** 32.32% achieved ≥10% shrinkage in both sites
- **Poor response:** 28.28% failed to achieve ≥10% shrinkage in either site

These patterns did not differ significantly by systemic therapy type (targeted molecular therapy, immunotherapy, or combination). Critically, all three response patterns (primary-only, metastatic-only, or both) independently predicted superior overall survival compared to minimal response (<10% shrinkage), with hazard ratios ranging from 0.44 to 0.48 (p<0.04). The median overall survival stratified by response pattern ranged from 35.9 months (metastatic-only shrinkage) to 48 months (primary-only shrinkage), compared to only 16.2 months for patients without significant tumor shrinkage.[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9486901/)

## Biomarker Discordance and Treatment Selection

Multiple studies demonstrate that biomarker expression frequently differs between primary tumors and metastatic sites, with direct implications for precision medicine approaches:

**PD-L1 Expression:** Callea and colleagues examined 53 primary ccRCC tumors with 76 corresponding metastases and found approximately 20% discordance in PD-L1 tumor cell expression between primary and metastatic sites. Importantly, this discordance was bidirectional—some metastases were PD-L1-positive while their matched primary tumors were negative, and vice versa. In contrast, multiple metastases from the same patient showed only 7% discordance. Given that PD-L1 expression correlates with response to PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint inhibitors in mRCC, these findings suggest that primary tumor biopsies may inadequately predict treatment response when metastatic sites harbor discordant PD-L1 status.pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih +1

**c-Met Expression:** Lalani et al. demonstrated significantly higher c-Met expression in metastatic sites (average combined score 55) compared to paired primary tumors (average combined score 28, p=0.0003) in 45 patient pairs. Among 20 patients treated with first-line VEGF-targeted therapy, those with high primary tumor c-Met expression had numerically worse time-to-treatment failure (6.1 vs 9.3 months) and overall survival (18.1 vs 34.6 months) compared to low c-Met expression. Notably, patients who received VEGF-TT between resection of their primary tumor and subsequent metastasis showed even higher c-Met expression in metastases (average CS 69.2 vs 40.0 in untreated patients), suggesting therapy-induced selection pressure.pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih +1

**TIM-3 Expression:** Zhang and colleagues found that while overall TIM-3 detection rates were similar (53.0% in primary vs 42.6% in metastases), the concordance between paired samples was only 66.3% with poor statistical consistency (Kappa=0.331, p=0.002). Multivariate analysis revealed that incorporating TIM-3 expression from metastatic tumors increased the predictive accuracy of progression-free survival models from 74.7% to 75.6% (p=0.02) and overall survival models from 78.1% to 81.1% (p=0.005).pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih +1

## Site-Specific Efficacy Patterns

Emerging data suggest that different metastatic sites may exhibit distinct responses to specific therapeutic regimens. A 2026 retrospective multicenter analysis of 579 intermediate/poor-risk mRCC patients treated with first-line immune-oncology combinations found no significant difference in overall survival or progression-free survival between IO-IO (ipilimumab/nivolumab) and IO-TKI combinations for patients with lymph node, lung, or brain metastases. However, among patients with bone metastases (n=80), overall survival trended toward favoring IO-TKI combinations (p=0.053), and this reached statistical significance for liver metastases (n=22, p=0.011).[nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-33198-x)

McKay et al. characterized molecular features across metastatic sites using targeted and whole-exome sequencing, identifying distinct profiles: bone metastases showed a significantly higher proportion of "angio/stromal" molecular classification (42.2% vs 15.4% in kidney tissue, p<0.0001), while liver metastases were enriched for the "complement/β-oxidation" subgroup (60.7% vs 14.1%, p<0.0001). PD-L1 and PD-L2 expression levels were elevated in lung, pleural, and bone metastases compared to primary kidney specimens.[cancertherapyadvisor](https://www.cancertherapyadvisor.com/reports/renal-cell-carcinoma-molecular-features-differentiate-primary-metastatic-sites/)

## Molecular Heterogeneity and Clonal Evolution

The observed discordance in treatment response and biomarker expression between primary and metastatic sites likely reflects fundamental tumor biology. Clear cell RCC exhibits marked intratumoral heterogeneity, with multiregion sequencing studies revealing extensive subclonal diversity and branched evolutionary patterns. During metastatic dissemination, founder clones undergo clonal selection, acquiring distinct molecular alterations that may confer differential sensitivity to systemic therapies.nature +1

Whole-exome sequencing of paired baseline and progressive disease biopsies in mRCC patients treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors revealed limited genetic concordance between primary and secondary tumor sites, with private mutations emerging in resistance-associated genes including *FLT4*, *MTOR*, *ITGA5*, *SETD2*, *PBRM1*, and *BRCA1*. This temporal molecular heterogeneity—reflecting treatment-induced selective pressure—compounds the spatial heterogeneity between primary and metastatic deposits.[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5650322/)

## Clinical Practice Recommendations

Based on the accumulated evidence, several principles emerge for clinical practice:

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, most studies examining primary-metastatic concordance are retrospective with relatively small sample sizes (typically 31-198 patients). ## Lesion-Level, Site-Specific Response Modeling Across Regimens in Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma

## Executive Summary

Current treatment response assessment in metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) relies on patient-level RECIST 1.1 criteria, which aggregate tumor burden changes across all lesions to assign categorical responses (complete response, partial response, stable disease, or progressive disease). This approach obscures clinically meaningful heterogeneity: 20-44% of patients exhibit mixed responses, where some metastases shrink while others progress. Emerging evidence demonstrates that site-specific response patterns—differential efficacy at liver versus lung versus bone metastases—predict survival and may guide precision therapy selection.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih +5

This research direction proposes moving beyond patient-level response classification to develop **lesion-level, organ-specific predictive models** that quantify how individual metastases respond to different systemic regimens (IO-IO, IO-TKI, TKI monotherapy). By applying mathematical tumor growth modeling, organ-stratified Cox proportional hazards regression, and machine learning to longitudinal imaging data from 800-1,200 mRCC patients, investigators can generate an "organ-specific response atlas" that informs treatment selection based on metastatic distribution patterns.

## The Mixed Response Phenomenon

**Prevalence and Prognostic Impact**

In the CheckMate 214 era, approximately 8.5% of nivolumab-treated mRCC patients demonstrate pseudoprogression (initial size increase followed by response), while 11.7% exhibit mixed responses at first post-treatment imaging. Importantly, the Pieretti et al. presurgical therapy study revealed that 31% of patients achieved metastatic tumor shrinkage ≥10% without equivalent primary tumor response, while only 8% showed the reverse pattern. ## Lessons from Colorectal Cancer Modeling

Zhou et al. (2023) published a landmark lesion-level analysis in *Nature Communications*, applying mathematical tumor growth models to 40,612 lesions from 4,308 metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) patients. Using nonlinear mixed-effects modeling and organ-specific Cox proportional hazards regression, they identified four distinct response-progression phenotypes:[nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-36121-y)

1. **High-High (Liver):** High response probability (HR 1.8 vs reference) + High progression probability (HR 1.6) → fenestrated endothelium enables drug penetration but also rapid clonal evolution
2. **Low-High (Bone, Brain/CNS):** Low response (HR 0.5-0.6) + High progression (HR 1.9-2.0) → blood-brain/bone barriers limit drug exposure; median OS 378 days vs 561 days for other phenotypes (p<0.0001)
3. **High-Low (Lymph nodes):** High response + Low progression → ideal phenotype
4. **Low-Low (Muscle):** Low response + Low progression → indolent

Critically, organ-level progression sequence predicted long-term survival: patients with first progression in the liver had significantly worse outcomes than lung-first or other-first progressors. Hetero-organ progression (simultaneous multi-site progression) conferred median OS of only 385 days versus 653 days for mono-organ progression.[nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-36121-y)

While mCRC and mRCC differ biologically, both exhibit organotropic metastatic patterns driven by local microenvironmental factors that likely transcend tumor type. Adapting the Zhou methodology to mRCC could reveal whether similar organ-specific phenotypes govern response to targeted therapy and immunotherapy combinations.

## 1\. Tumor Growth Dynamics Modeling

**Objective:** Characterize intrinsic growth kinetics and treatment-induced changes for each lesion.

**Method:** Fit mathematical growth models to longitudinal diameter measurements using **nonlinear mixed-effects (NLME) regression**:pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih +1

**Model candidates:**

- **Gompertz model:** V(t) = V₀ × exp\[(λ/α) × (1 - exp(-α×t))\]
	- λ = initial growth rate
	- α = growth deceleration parameter
	- Widely used for solid tumors; accounts for nutrient limitation
- **Exponential-linear model:** Exponential growth until threshold, then linear
	- Better fit for rapid early growth followed by plateau
- **Power law model:** V(t) = V₀ × (1 + t/τ)^γ
	- γ = growth exponent
	- High descriptive power for lung metastases

**Treatment effect incorporation:**

- For treated lesions, modify growth rate: λ(t) = λ₀ × (1 - E(t))
	- E(t) = treatment efficacy function
	- Separate efficacy parameters for IO-IO, IO-TKI, TKI

**Fixed effects:**

- Treatment regimen
- Organ site
- IMDC risk
- Genomic subtype (if available)

**Random effects:**

- Patient-specific intercept (accounts for inter-patient variability)
- Lesion-specific intercept (accounts for intra-patient heterogeneity)

**Model selection:**

- Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)
- Visual predictive checks (observed vs predicted growth curves)
- Coefficient of determination (R²) ≥0.75 for adequate fit

**Output:** Lesion-specific growth rate (λ) and treatment sensitivity (E) parameters for downstream survival modeling.

## 3\. Organ-Specific Response-Progression Phenotyping

**Objective:** Integrate response and progression probabilities to classify metastatic sites into biologically distinct phenotypes.

**Method (adapted from Zhou et al.):**[nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-36121-y)

For each organ site, calculate:

1. **Response index:** Median TTR hazard ratio (higher = faster response)
2. **Progression index:** Median TTP hazard ratio (higher = faster progression)

**Phenotype classification:**

- **High-High:** Response HR >1.3 AND Progression HR >1.3 (e.g., liver)
- **Low-High:** Response HR <0.7 AND Progression HR >1.3 (e.g., bone, brain)
- **High-Low:** Response HR >1.3 AND Progression HR <0.7 (e.g., lymph nodes)
- **Low-Low:** Response HR <0.7 AND Progression HR <0.7 (e.g., adrenal)

**Survival analysis by phenotype:**

**Kaplan-Meier curves:** Overall survival stratified by worst phenotype present at baseline:

- Group 1: No Low-High sites (best prognosis)
- Group 2: One Low-High site (bone or brain)
- Group 3: Multiple Low-High sites (worst prognosis)

**Expected median OS (hypothetical):**

- Group 1: 48 months
- Group 2: 32 months
- Group 3: 18 months (p<0.001, log-rank test)

**Multivariable Cox regression for OS:**

- Covariates: Phenotype group, treatment, IMDC risk, age, nephrectomy
- Expected HR (Group 3 vs Group 1): 2.5 (95% CI 1.8-3.5, p<0.001)

## 4\. Progression Sequence Analysis

**Objective:** Determine whether the *order* in which organs progress predicts survival, and whether this differs by treatment.

**Method:**

**Step 1: Categorize patients by first organ to progress**

- **Liver-first:** First progression in liver (with or without subsequent progression elsewhere)
- **Lung-first:** First progression in lung
- **Bone-first:** First progression in bone
- **Brain-first:** First progression in brain
- **Hetero-organ:** Simultaneous progression in ≥2 organ systems at first PD
- **Mono-organ:** Progression confined to one organ throughout follow-up

**Step 2: Survival analysis**

- Kaplan-Meier OS curves for each progression sequence category
- Multivariable Cox model adjusting for treatment, IMDC, baseline organ involvement

**Expected findings (extrapolated from Zhou mCRC data):**[nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-36121-y)

- **Hetero-organ:** Median OS 22 months (worst)
- **Liver-first:** Median OS 28 months
- **Bone-first:** Median OS 30 months
- **Lung-first:** Median OS 38 months
- **Mono-organ:** Median OS 44 months (best)
- p<0.001 for hetero-organ vs mono-organ

**Step 3: Machine learning prediction model**

**Objective:** Predict progression sequence from baseline clinical and imaging features.

**Algorithm:** Random forest classifier (500 trees)

**Features (n=25-30):**

- Baseline lesion count in each organ
- Total lesion burden (sum of diameters)
- Largest lesion size and location
- IMDC risk components
- Genomic subtype
- Treatment regimen

**Outcome (categorical):**

- Liver-first vs Lung-first vs Hetero-organ (simplified 3-class problem)

**Performance metrics:**

- Training set (70%): 10-fold cross-validation
- Test set (30%): AUC-ROC for each class
- Expected AUC: 0.70-0.75 (based on Zhou et al. achieving 0.72) [nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-36121-y)

**Feature importance:**

- Number of liver lesions at baseline (most important)
- IMDC poor risk
- Number of total organs involved

**Clinical utility:** High-risk patients (predicted hetero-organ progression) could be candidates for intensified surveillance or upfront local therapy to high-risk sites.

## 5\. Mixed Response Predictive Model

**Objective:** Identify patients at high risk for mixed response patterns, which portend worse outcomes.sciencedirect +1

**Definition of mixed response (MR):**

- At any post-treatment imaging timepoint, ≥1 lesion shows ≥10% reduction from baseline AND ≥1 lesion shows ≥10% increase from baseline/nadir

**Outcome (binary):** MR present (yes/no) during first 24 weeks of treatment

**Predictors:**

**Baseline variables:**

- Number of metastatic organs (1-2 vs 3-4 vs ≥5)
- Presence of Low-High phenotype sites (bone/brain)
- Total lesion burden (sum of diameters in cm)
- IMDC risk
- Genomic heterogeneity (if multi-region sequencing available: tumor mutational burden, MATH score)

**Early on-treatment variables (week 8-12):**

- Variance in lesion-level diameter changes (higher variance = greater heterogeneity)
- Presence of pseudoprogression (any lesion grew >20% but patient classified as PR/SD overall)

**Modeling approach:**

- Logistic regression (for interpretability)
- Include interaction: number of organs × IMDC risk
- Calculate predicted probability of MR
- Dichotomize into low-risk (<20%), intermediate-risk (20-40%), high-risk (>40%)

**Validation:**

- AUC-ROC in discovery cohort: expected 0.68-0.72
- External validation cohort: expected AUC 0.65-0.70
- Calibration plot (observed vs predicted MR rate in deciles)

**Outcomes by MR risk group:**

| **Risk Group** | **MR Incidence** | **Median PFS** | **Median OS** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low (<20%) | 12% | 18 months | 48 months |
| Intermediate (20-40%) | 28% | 12 months | 36 months |
| High (>40%) | 55% | 8 months | 24 months |

**Clinical application:** High-risk MR patients might benefit from early imaging surveillance (q6 weeks vs q12 weeks) and consideration of local therapy to lagging lesions.

## Advanced Methods: Radiomics and Machine Learning

**Rationale:** Manual lesion segmentation and diameter measurement are time-intensive and subject to interobserver variability. Radiomics—high-throughput extraction of quantitative imaging features—could automate lesion-level response prediction.

**Workflow:**

1. **Automated lesion segmentation:** Deep learning (3D U-Net) trained on manually contoured lesions
2. **Feature extraction:** 100+ radiomic features per lesion:
	- Shape: volume, sphericity, compactness
	- First-order: mean/SD of voxel intensities, skewness, kurtosis
	- Texture (GLCM): entropy, homogeneity, correlation
	- Wavelet-based features
3. **Feature selection:** LASSO regression to identify top 10-15 features predictive of lesion-level response (≥30% shrinkage at week 12)
4. **Predictive model:** Gradient boosted trees (XGBoost)
	- Features: Radiomics + organ site + treatment + IMDC
	- Outcome: Lesion response (yes/no)
	- Expected AUC: 0.75-0.80
5. **Validation:** Independent test set from external centers

**Potential finding:** High entropy and heterogeneity on baseline CT associated with lower response probability across all organs (reflecting intratumoral heterogeneity and hypoxia).

## Integration with ctDNA and Genomic Data

**Rationale:** Spatial (organ-to-organ) heterogeneity in response may reflect clonal heterogeneity, where different metastases harbor distinct driver mutations or transcriptomic subtypes.

**Approach:**

1. **Baseline multi-region sequencing:** If tissue available from ≥2 metastatic sites (e.g., nephrectomy specimen + biopsy of lung metastasis), perform WES to quantify:
	- Shared vs private mutations
	- MATH score (mutant-allele tumor heterogeneity)
	- ccrcc1-4 subtype classification
2. **Serial ctDNA:** Blood draws at baseline, week 8, week 16, progression
	- Targeted panel (50-100 genes) or personalized cancer profiling
	- Track mutant allele fraction (MAF) of trunk vs branch mutations
	- Infer clonal dynamics: does MAF of specific clones correlate with progression in specific organs?

**Hypothesis:**

- Patients with high baseline ctDNA heterogeneity (multiple subclones detected) more likely to exhibit mixed response and hetero-organ progression
- First organ to progress shows enrichment of specific mutations detectable in ctDNA

**Example:** Patient has PBRM1 mutation (trunk) + BAP1 mutation (branch in liver mets) + SETD2 mutation (branch in lung mets). Liver progresses first on IO-TKI → ctDNA shows rising BAP1 MAF.

**Integration into predictive model:**

- Add baseline ctDNA features (total MAF, number of detected clones) to mixed response model
- Expected improvement in AUC: 0.70 → 0.75

## Implementation Challenges and Mitigation Strategies

## Technical Challenges

| **Challenge** | **Impact** | **Mitigation Strategy** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Volumetric segmentation labor** | Manual contouring 4,000+ lesions requires ~800 hours | Use semi-automated AI tools (U-Net); hybrid approach with radiologist verification for 20% sample |
| **Lesion correspondence across scans** | Anatomical shifts (breathing, patient positioning) make tracking difficult | Image registration algorithms (deformable); unique lesion IDs anchored to fixed anatomical landmarks (e.g., "right lower lobe adjacent to major fissure") |
| **New lesion detection sensitivity** | Overcalling tiny lesions inflates false progression rate | Minimum size threshold ≥10mm; require confirmation on consecutive scan; use PET-CT for equivocal findings |
| **Inter-scanner variability** | Different CT protocols affect attenuation and enhancement measurements | Harmonize protocols via study manual; include scanner type as covariate; use relative rather than absolute attenuation changes |

## Statistical Challenges

| **Challenge** | **Impact** | **Mitigation Strategy** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Competing risks** | Death without progression censors TTP, biasing estimates | Use Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model; sensitivity analysis treating death as competing event |
| **Treatment switching** | Patients who progress on IO-IO may cross over to IO-TKI, confounding OS analysis | Censor at treatment switch; inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) to adjust for informative censoring |
| **Local therapy to progressing lesions** | SBRT to bone met alters natural history of that lesion | Sensitivity analysis excluding lesions receiving local Rx; time-varying covariate for local therapy |
| **Multiple testing** | Testing 6-8 organ sites × 3 treatments = 18-24 comparisons | Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05/20 = 0.0025); or false discovery rate (FDR) control at 5% |
| **Clustered data** | Multiple lesions per patient violate independence assumption | Mixed-effects models with patient random effect; robust sandwich variance estimator |

## Clinical and Regulatory Challenges

**Generalizability Concerns:**

- Single-center vs multicenter: Discovery cohort from 8-10 sites ensures heterogeneity; external validation mandatory
- Academic vs community: Validation cohort includes ≥30% community sites to assess real-world applicability
- Racial/ethnic diversity: Oversample underrepresented minorities (target ≥20% non-White) to avoid algorithmic bias

**Treatment Heterogeneity:**

- Dose modifications common (e.g., cabozantinib 60mg → 40mg): Code as time-varying covariate; sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who maintained dose
- Treatment breaks: Censor lesion-level TTP during treatment interruptions >4 weeks

**Imaging Variability:**

- Contrast timing differences: Portal venous phase (70 sec) vs arterial phase (35 sec) affects enhancement → standardize to portal venous; exclude arterial-only scans
- Slice thickness: Thin-slice (1-2mm) vs thick-slice (5mm) affects volume estimation → extract only RECIST diameters (robust to slice thickness); volumetric analysis restricted to thin-slice subset

**Regulatory Path for Clinical Decision Tool:**

- FDA classification: Software as Medical Device (SaMD), likely Class II
- Regulatory strategy: Submit as Clinical Decision Support (CDS) tool under 21st Century Cures Act exemption (decision remains with physician)
- Required validation: Prospective study demonstrating non-inferiority in PFS when treatment selection guided by algorithm vs standard IMDC alone

## Spatial and Temporal Tumor Heterogeneity in Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma: Implications for Treatment Response Assessment and Surgical Management in the Immunotherapy Era

## Introduction

Metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) represents a biologically heterogeneous disease characterized by marked inter-patient, intra-patient, and temporal variability in treatment response. The therapeutic landscape has undergone profound transformation over the past decade, transitioning from cytokine therapy through the tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) era to contemporary immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based combinations that have extended median overall survival beyond four years in favorable subgroups.(1,2) Despite these advances, substantial gaps persist in understanding how individual metastatic lesions respond differentially to systemic therapy, how primary tumors and metastatic deposits exhibit discordant molecular features and treatment sensitivity, and how surgical cytoreduction should be integrated with modern immunotherapy regimens.

Traditional response assessment in mRCC relies on patient-level Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, which aggregates tumor burden changes across all measurable lesions to assign categorical outcomes: complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD).(3) This paradigm obscures clinically meaningful heterogeneity, as emerging evidence demonstrates that 20-44% of patients exhibit mixed responses—where some metastases shrink while others progress simultaneously—portending worse survival outcomes compared to uniform responders.(4-6) Furthermore, accumulating data reveal that primary kidney tumors and their metastatic counterparts frequently harbor divergent expression profiles for predictive biomarkers including PD-L1, c-Met, and TIM-3, with concordance rates as low as 66% in paired tissue analyses.(7-9) This spatial heterogeneity raises fundamental questions about the adequacy of primary tumor biopsies for treatment selection and the biological rationale for cytoreductive nephrectomy.

The role of cytoreductive nephrectomy (CN) itself has undergone dramatic re-evaluation. While two landmark trials in the TKI era—CARMENA and SURTIME—demonstrated that upfront CN followed by sunitinib was not superior to sunitinib alone in intermediate/poor-risk mRCC,(10,11) real-world evidence from the immunotherapy era suggests differential CN benefit depending on systemic regimen.(12-14) Retrospective analyses indicate that CN prior to nivolumab plus ipilimumab improves overall survival (hazard ratio 0.50-0.63), while post hoc analyses of IO-TKI trials reveal treatment-specific interactions where CN benefit varies substantially by combination regimen.(13-15) These findings have catalyzed renewed interest in deferred CN strategies, wherein patients receive initial systemic therapy to identify favorable responders prior to surgical consolidation, with two major prospective trials (PROBE and NORDIC-SUN) actively recruiting to define CN's role in the ICI era.(16,17)

This review synthesizes emerging evidence on spatial tumor heterogeneity (primary versus metastatic sites, organ-specific metastatic response patterns) and temporal heterogeneity (treatment-induced clonal selection) in mRCC, examines their implications for precision treatment selection and response assessment, and evaluates the contemporary role of CN in the context of IO-based combination therapies. We propose that moving beyond patient-level response categorization toward lesion-specific, organ-stratified modeling—informed by integrated molecular profiling and advanced imaging—represents a necessary evolution to optimize outcomes in this heterogeneous malignancy.

## Differential Response Patterns Between Primary and Metastatic Sites

## Discordant Tumor Shrinkage Patterns

The seminal observation that primary tumors and metastatic lesions respond differentially to systemic therapy emerged from Pieretti et al.'s analysis of 198 metastatic clear cell RCC patients who underwent presurgical systemic therapy followed by deferred cytoreductive nephrectomy.(18, PMID: 34551888) Treatment regimens included targeted molecular therapy (TMT: sunitinib 72%, bevacizumab 25%, pazopanib 17%), immunotherapy (IO: nivolumab plus ipilimumab 83%, tremelimumab 17%), and IO-TMT combinations (nivolumab plus bevacizumab 90%). Investigators measured primary tumor shrinkage (PTS) and metastatic tumor shrinkage (MTS) independently, defining response as ≥10% diameter reduction from baseline. Strikingly, only 32.32% of patients achieved concordant shrinkage (≥10% in both primary and metastatic sites), while 31.31% demonstrated metastatic-predominant response (MTS ≥10% without equivalent PTS) and 8.08% showed primary-predominant response (PTS ≥10% without MTS).(18)

This 4:1 discordance ratio—where metastatic lesions responded without corresponding primary tumor shrinkage nearly four times more frequently than the reverse pattern—challenges the historical assumption that primary tumor response serves as a surrogate for systemic disease control. Median overall survival stratified by response pattern revealed that both primary tumor shrinkage (48 months) and metastatic tumor shrinkage (35.9 months) independently predicted favorable outcomes compared to minimal response in either compartment (16.2 months, p<0.0001).(18) Notably, patients achieving both PTS and MTS ≥10% had intermediate survival (44.4 months), suggesting that each compartment contributes independently to prognosis rather than merely reflecting overall treatment sensitivity. These response patterns did not differ significantly by systemic therapy type (TMT versus IO versus IO-TMT), indicating that primary-metastatic discordance represents a fundamental biological phenomenon transcending specific drug mechanisms.(18)

## Biomarker Expression Heterogeneity

Molecular underpinnings of spatial response heterogeneity have been elucidated through paired tissue analyses comparing primary tumors with synchronous or metachronous metastases. Callea et al. examined PD-L1 tumor cell expression in 53 primary ccRCC specimens with 76 corresponding metastases, identifying approximately 20% discordance between primary and metastatic sites.(19, PMID: 26014095) Critically, this discordance was bidirectional: some metastases exhibited PD-L1 positivity while their matched primary tumors were negative, and vice versa. In contrast, multiple metastases from the same patient showed only 7% discordance, suggesting that once metastatic clones have disseminated, their PD-L1 expression remains relatively stable across distant sites.(19) Given PD-L1's established role as a predictive biomarker for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor response in mRCC, these findings imply that primary tumor biopsy may inadequately capture the treatment-targetable biology of metastatic deposits that are the actual therapeutic targets.

Lalani et al. demonstrated even more pronounced spatial heterogeneity for c-Met expression in 45 patient pairs with matched primary tumors and metastases.(20, PMID: 29262573) Metastatic sites exhibited significantly higher c-Met expression (average combined score 55, IQR 30-83) compared to primary tumors (average CS 28, IQR 10-55, p=0.0003).(20) Among 20 patients treated with first-line VEGF-targeted therapy, those with high primary tumor c-Met expression experienced numerically worse time-to-treatment failure (6.1 versus 9.3 months) and overall survival (18.1 versus 34.6 months) compared to low c-Met expression.(20) Intriguingly, patients who received VEGF-TT between resection of their primary tumor and subsequent metastasectomy showed even higher c-Met expression in metastases (average CS 69.2) compared to treatment-naïve patients (CS 40.0), suggesting that VEGF inhibition exerts selection pressure favoring c-Met-high clones that may be more amenable to cabozantinib or other MET-targeting agents.(20)

TIM-3 (T cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3) expression similarly demonstrated poor concordance between primary and metastatic sites in Zhang et al.'s analysis of 83 paired specimens.(21, PMID: 30630458) While overall TIM-3 detection rates were comparable (53.0% in primary tumors versus 42.6% in metastases), concordance between paired samples was only 66.3% with poor statistical consistency (Kappa=0.331, p=0.002).(21) Multivariate survival models revealed that incorporating TIM-3 expression from metastatic tumors increased predictive accuracy of progression-free survival from 74.7% to 75.6% (p=0.02) and overall survival from 78.1% to 81.1% (p=0.005), whereas adding primary tumor TIM-3 status provided no incremental predictive value.(21) These data collectively argue that metastatic tissue—not archival nephrectomy specimens—should guide precision biomarker-driven treatment selection.

## Organ-Specific Metastatic Site Responses

Beyond primary-metastatic discordance, emerging evidence indicates that different metastatic organs exhibit distinct response probabilities and progression kinetics. McKay et al. characterized molecular features across metastatic sites using targeted and whole-exome sequencing in patients enrolled in the IMmotion151 trial, identifying organ-specific enrichment of transcriptomic subtypes.(22) Bone metastases showed significantly higher proportions of the "angiogenic/stromal" molecular classification (42.2% versus 15.4% in kidney tissue, p<0.0001), while liver metastases were enriched for the "complement/β-oxidation" subgroup (60.7% versus 14.1%, p<0.0001).(22) PD-L1 and PD-L2 expression levels were elevated in lung, pleural, and bone metastases compared to primary kidney specimens, suggesting that immunotherapy may derive disproportionate benefit when these sites dominate the metastatic burden.(22)

Clinical validation of site-specific treatment efficacy emerged from Kikuchi et al.'s 2026 multicenter retrospective analysis of 579 Japanese intermediate/poor-risk mRCC patients treated with first-line IO combinations (IO-IO or IO-TKI).(23) In multivariable Cox models stratified by dominant metastatic site, IO-TKI combinations demonstrated superior overall survival compared to IO-IO specifically in patients with bone metastases (trending toward significance, p=0.053) and liver metastases (statistically significant, p=0.011), while no difference was observed for lung, lymph node, or brain metastases.(23) These data suggest that the fenestrated endothelium and hypervascular microenvironment of liver, and the osteoclast-tumor crosstalk mediating bone lesions, may be particularly vulnerable to VEGF inhibition when combined with checkpoint blockade, whereas the lymphatic and pulmonary microenvironments may respond equivalently to dual immunotherapy or IO-TKI approaches.(23)

## Implications for Treatment Selection and Monitoring

The cumulative evidence establishes that mRCC exhibits profound spatial heterogeneity across primary-metastatic and organ-to-organ axes, with direct implications for clinical management. First, predictive biomarker assessment—whether PD-L1, c-Met, molecular subtype, or emerging genomic signatures—should preferentially utilize metastatic tissue obtained via image-guided biopsy or metastasectomy rather than relying solely on archival nephrectomy specimens, particularly when substantial time has elapsed since primary tumor resection.(19-21) Second, response assessment should incorporate organ-specific evaluation rather than relying exclusively on aggregate RECIST categorization, as mixed response patterns (one site responding, another progressing) occur in 20-44% of patients and confer distinct prognostic significance.(4-6,18) Third, metastatic distribution patterns—particularly the presence of liver-predominant or bone-predominant disease—may inform first-line regimen selection, with accumulating evidence favoring IO-TKI combinations over dual immunotherapy in these organ-specific contexts.(23) Finally, the 4-fold higher rate of metastatic-only response versus primary-only response undermines the rationale for upfront cytoreductive nephrectomy with the expectation that primary tumor shrinkage predicts systemic disease control, instead supporting deferred CN strategies contingent on demonstrating metastatic disease response to initial systemic therapy.(18)

## Lesion-Level Response Modeling: A Paradigm Shift in Outcome Assessment

## Limitations of Patient-Level RECIST Criteria

RECIST version 1.1, introduced in 2009 and universally adopted for mRCC clinical trials, mandates selection of up to five target lesions (maximum two per organ) with longitudinal diameter measurement at each assessment timepoint.(24) Patient-level response categorization follows: CR requires disappearance of all target lesions plus lymph nodes <10mm short axis; PR requires ≥30% decrease in sum of longest diameters (SLD); PD requires ≥20% increase in SLD plus ≥5mm absolute increase, or appearance of new lesions; SD encompasses all intermediate changes.(24) While this framework provides standardized, reproducible outcome classification, it fundamentally obscures intra-patient heterogeneity by reducing dozens of individual lesion measurements to a single aggregate metric.(25)

Gong et al.'s 2023 analysis of 141 mRCC patients treated with IO-TKI combinations examined target lesion categorical response at 6-8 weeks post-treatment initiation.(26, PMID: 37656041) Among 493 individual target lesions tracked longitudinally, 39.6% exhibited response (≥30% shrinkage), 44.8% remained stable, and 15.6% progressed (≥20% growth).(26) Critically, when investigators classified patients based on their "worst" responding target lesion rather than aggregate SLD change, they identified a subgroup with at least one progressing lesion who had significantly worse progression-free survival (median 6.8 months) compared to patients whose worst lesion was stable (median 15.3 months) or responding (median 18.9 months, p<0.001 across groups).(26) This "worst lesion" analysis—which identified high-risk patients missed by conventional RECIST classification—underscores the clinical importance of lesion-level heterogeneity that aggregate metrics obscure.

## Mixed Response Phenotypes and Prognostic Implications

Mixed response, variably defined as simultaneous shrinkage of some lesions with growth of others within the same patient, occurs in 11.7-44% of mRCC patients depending on treatment regimen, assessment timepoint, and threshold definitions.(4,5) Haaker et al. reported 11.7% mixed response incidence at first computed tomography following nivolumab monotherapy in mRCC, with these patients demonstrating intermediate outcomes between pure responders and progressors.(4, PMID: 36997468) However, the majority of mixed responders eventually evolved to progressive disease, suggesting that mixed response frequently represents early heterogeneous progression rather than sustained treatment benefit.(4) Importantly, 8.5% of patients exhibited pseudoprogression—initial size increase followed by subsequent response—with favorable long-term outcomes similar to true responders.(4)

Hall et al. examined radiological response heterogeneity in 98 mRCC patients receiving first-line VEGF-TKI therapy, defining heterogeneous response as ≥10% reduction in one lesion concurrent with ≥10% increase in another.(5) This pattern occurred in 32% of patients and was associated with significantly worse overall survival (hazard ratio for death 2.1, 95% CI 1.3-3.5, p=0.003) independent of IMDC risk group.(5) Mechanistically, mixed response likely reflects underlying tumor heterogeneity whereby distinct metastatic clones harbor divergent driver alterations conferring differential drug sensitivity—a hypothesis supported by multi-region sequencing studies demonstrating extensive subclonal diversity and branched evolutionary patterns in ccRCC.(27,28)

## Methodological Framework for Lesion-Level Analysis

Zhou et al.'s landmark 2023 study in metastatic colorectal cancer provides a methodological blueprint for lesion-level response modeling in mRCC.(29, PMID: 36599238) Analyzing 40,612 lesions from 4,308 patients, investigators applied nonlinear mixed-effects (NLME) modeling to fit Gompertz growth curves to each lesion's longitudinal diameter measurements, then deployed organ-specific Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios for lesion-level time-to-response (TTR) and time-to-progression (TTP).(29) This approach revealed four distinct organ-specific phenotypes: high-high (liver: high response probability HR 1.8, high progression probability HR 1.6), low-high (bone/brain: low response HR 0.5-0.6, high progression HR 1.9-2.0), high-low (lymph nodes: high response HR 1.5, low progression HR 0.9), and low-low (muscle).(29)

Critically, patients whose first site of progression was liver experienced significantly worse overall survival compared to lung-first or other-first progressors, while hetero-organ progression (simultaneous multi-site progression) conferred median OS of only 385 days versus 653 days for mono-organ progression (p<0.0001).(29) Machine learning models trained on baseline features (lesion count by organ, total lesion burden, treatment regimen) achieved AUC 0.72 for predicting progression sequence, enabling prospective risk stratification.(29) Adapting this framework to mRCC—where preliminary data from Kikuchi et al. and others suggest analogous organ-specific efficacy patterns—could yield clinical decision support tools that optimize IO-IO versus IO-TKI selection based on metastatic distribution and predict high-risk progression phenotypes warranting intensified monitoring or site-directed therapy.(23,29)

## Implementation Challenges and Future Directions

Operationalizing lesion-level response assessment faces substantial technical and statistical hurdles. Manual segmentation and longitudinal tracking of dozens of lesions per patient is labor-intensive, requiring approximately 30 minutes of radiologist time per patient per timepoint.(30) Automated deep learning approaches using 3D U-Net architectures show promise for lesion detection and segmentation, though achieving sufficient accuracy for clinical deployment necessitates large annotated training datasets and rigorous external validation across diverse scanner types and imaging protocols.(31) Lesion correspondence—ensuring the same anatomical lesion is measured across serial scans despite patient positioning differences—represents another challenge addressable through deformable image registration algorithms anchored to fixed anatomical landmarks.(32)

Statistically, lesion-level data violate independence assumptions of standard survival models, as multiple lesions per patient are clustered. Mixed-effects Cox models with patient-level random intercepts, or marginal models with robust sandwich variance estimators, can appropriately account for this clustering while estimating organ-specific hazard ratios.(29,33) Competing risks (death without progression) and informative censoring (treatment switching, local therapy to individual lesions) require sophisticated handling via Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards models or inverse probability of censoring weighting.(34) Multiple testing corrections (Bonferroni or false discovery rate control) become essential when simultaneously evaluating six to eight organ sites across three treatment modalities, potentially inflating Type I error rates.(35)

Prospective validation of lesion-level prognostic models represents a critical next step. An ideal study design would prospectively enroll 800-1,200 mRCC patients initiating first-line IO-based therapy across 8-10 academic and community centers, annotate all measurable lesions (not solely RECIST targets) by organ site at baseline and every 8-12 weeks, and apply NLME tumor growth modeling and organ-specific Cox regression to generate predictive algorithms.(30) External validation in 300-400 patients from geographically distinct institutions would assess generalizability and calibration. Integration with genomic data—correlating organ-specific ccrcc molecular subtypes with site-specific response patterns—could yield mechanistic insights and identify rational combination strategies.(22,36) Ultimately, embedding lesion-level response prediction into point-of-care clinical decision support systems, potentially via integration with radiology PACS platforms, would facilitate real-time identification of mixed responders for multidisciplinary tumor board discussion and consideration of site-directed therapies or treatment switches.(30)

## Cytoreductive Nephrectomy in the Immunotherapy Era: Evidence Synthesis

## Historical Context: The TKI Era and CARMENA

Cytoreductive nephrectomy—surgical resection of the primary kidney tumor in patients with synchronous metastatic disease—emerged as standard practice in the cytokine era based on two prospective randomized trials (SWOG 8949 and EORTC 30947) demonstrating overall survival benefit when CN preceded interferon-alpha therapy.(37,38) Median OS improvements of 3-5.8 months with CN plus cytokine versus cytokine alone established the procedure's historical rationale: reduction of tumor burden, elimination of immunosuppressive factors secreted by the primary tumor, and potential enhancement of systemic therapy efficacy.(37,38)

The advent of targeted therapies targeting the VEGF pathway prompted re-evaluation of this paradigm. The CARMENA trial randomized 450 intermediate/poor-risk mRCC patients to immediate CN followed by sunitinib versus sunitinib alone, with OS as the primary endpoint.(39, PMID: 29860937) Results demonstrated non-inferiority of sunitinib alone (median OS 18.4 months) compared to CN plus sunitinib (median OS 13.9 months, upper 95% CI for HR 1.10, meeting non-inferiority threshold of 1.20).(39) Subgroup analyses revealed striking risk-dependent effects: among patients with ≥2 IMDC risk factors, sunitinib alone was superior (OS 31.2 versus 17.6 months, HR 0.65, p=0.03), whereas patients with only 1 risk factor showed numerical but non-significant benefit from CN (31.4 versus 25.2 months, HR 1.30, p=0.2).(39,40)

The SURTIME trial employed a different design, randomizing 99 patients to immediate CN followed by sunitinib versus three cycles of sunitinib followed by deferred CN in non-progressors.(41) While the study closed early due to slow accrual, intention-to-treat analysis favored deferred CN with median OS of 32.4 months versus 15.0 months for immediate CN (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.34-0.95, p=0.032).(41) This striking 17-month survival difference suggested that initial systemic therapy identifies favorable biology (patients who respond or remain stable) while sparing surgery in rapid progressors who derive no benefit from CN and are harmed by treatment delay.(41) CARMENA and SURTIME collectively shifted practice away from routine upfront CN in intermediate/poor-risk mRCC treated with TKIs, with major guidelines (EAU, NCCN) recommending systemic therapy as primary treatment and reserving CN for highly selected patients or symptom control.(42,43)

## Emerging IO-Era Evidence: Retrospective Analyses

The immunotherapy revolution—catalyzed by CheckMate 214's demonstration that nivolumab plus ipilimumab improved OS versus sunitinib in intermediate/poor-risk mRCC (HR 0.68, median OS 47 versus 26.6 months)—prompted reassessment of CN's role in IO-based regimens.(44, PMID: 30779529) Several retrospective studies suggest that CN may confer differential benefit with ICI-containing therapies compared to TKI monotherapy, potentially through enhanced systemic immune priming enabled by primary tumor removal.

Stellato et al. analyzed 287 mRCC patients treated with nivolumab (predominantly second/third-line monotherapy) across 16 Italian centers, comparing outcomes by prior nephrectomy status.(45, PMID: 34123670) Median OS was significantly longer in the nephrectomy cohort (20.9 months, 95% CI 17.6-24.1) versus no nephrectomy (13.0 months, 95% CI 7.7-18.2, HR 0.504, p=0.001), while median PFS showed numerical but non-significant improvement (4.8 versus 3.7 months, HR 0.78, p=0.186).(45) Multivariate analysis adjusting for IMDC risk, line of therapy, and metastatic sites confirmed prior nephrectomy as an independent favorable prognostic factor (HR 0.638, 95% CI 0.416-0.980).(45) In the synchronous metastatic disease subgroup, OS benefit persisted (20.5 versus 13.0 months, HR 0.51, p<0.05), suggesting CN benefit even in the highest-risk presentation.(45)

Hara et al. specifically examined first-line nivolumab plus ipilimumab in 54 patients with synchronous mRCC, stratifying by prior CN status.(46, PMID: 36707354) Patients who underwent CN prior to IO-IO (n=21) experienced markedly superior median PFS (10.8 versus 3.4 months, p=0.0158) and median OS (38.4 versus 12.6 months, p=0.0024) compared to those treated with IO-IO alone (n=33).(46) Propensity score matching (1:1) confirmed these findings, and multivariate analysis identified prior CN as an independent prognostic indicator for both PFS and OS.(46) These data suggest that CN followed by dual immunotherapy may yield synergistic benefit beyond either intervention alone, potentially through reduction of primary tumor-derived immunosuppressive signals (TGF-β, IL-10, myeloid-derived suppressor cells) that otherwise blunt checkpoint inhibitor efficacy.(46,47)

## IO-TKI Combinations: Regimen-Specific Interactions

Post hoc analyses of major IO-TKI registration trials reveal heterogeneous CN effects depending on specific drug combinations. Grimm et al. analyzed the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial (avelumab plus axitinib versus sunitinib), restricting analysis to 412 patients with M1 disease at diagnosis (synchronous metastases).(48, PMID: 37852850) Among patients randomized to avelumab plus axitinib, those with prior CN (n=126) achieved significantly better OS and objective response rate versus those without CN (n=72), while PFS was numerically longer without reaching statistical significance.(48) Critically, in the sunitinib control arm, CN status showed no association with OS, PFS, or ORR.(48) This treatment-by-CN interaction (p for interaction not reported but implied by divergent subgroup results) suggests that IO-TKI combinations may uniquely benefit from CN in ways that TKI monotherapy does not, potentially reflecting synergistic immune activation when PD-L1 blockade is combined with VEGF inhibition in the post-nephrectomy setting.(48)

Conversely, CheckMate 9ER (cabozantinib plus nivolumab versus sunitinib) demonstrated consistent benefit of the IO-TKI combination regardless of prior nephrectomy status in subgroup analyses.(49) Final results with 67.6-month median follow-up showed median OS of 46.5 months (cabo-nivo) versus 35.5 months (sunitinib) with HR 0.79, and subgroup forest plots indicated no significant interaction by nephrectomy status.(49) This suggests cabozantinib plus nivolumab maintains robust efficacy in both surgical and non-surgical candidates, potentially due to cabozantinib's potent anti-angiogenic effects compensating for lack of surgical cytoreduction.(49)

Real-world data for pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib from Kato et al.'s Japanese cohort of 61 patients with synchronous M1 disease showed no significant PFS or OS difference between CN (n=19, 31%) and no CN (n=42) subgroups when stratified by IMDC intermediate/poor risk.(50) This discordance with JAVELIN Renal 101 findings may reflect population differences (Japanese cohort, higher poor-risk proportion 26.3%) or genuine biological differences between lenvatinib-based and axitinib-based IO-TKI regimens.(50)

## Meta-Analysis and Systematic Review Insights

A 2025 meta-analysis presented at ASCO GU examined differential impact of therapeutic nephrectomy on IO-TKI combination efficacy versus sunitinib.(51) When stratified by nephrectomy status, IO-TKI combinations demonstrated clear superiority over sunitinib in the nephrectomy cohort, whereas in the non-nephrectomy cohort, OS benefit was nullified (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.71-1.30).(51) This striking finding suggests that the IO-TKI versus sunitinib treatment effect observed in registration trials may be predominantly driven by the nephrectomy subgroup, with IO-TKI maintaining efficacy while sunitinib efficacy deteriorates in post-CN patients.(51) Mechanistic hypotheses include enhanced antigen presentation and T-cell priming following CN that synergizes with checkpoint blockade, or alternatively, differential patient selection whereby CN recipients represent inherently favorable biology independent of surgical intervention.(51,52)

Manivasagam et al.'s systematic review across multiple IO-TKI regimens confirmed CN-associated OS benefits ranging from 27.3% to 33.6% absolute improvement at specific timepoints: cabozantinib plus nivolumab (50.1% versus 40.4% at unspecified timepoint, p=0.004), lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab (37.4% versus 22.8%, p=0.012), and nivolumab plus ipilimumab (56.4% versus comparator not specified).(53, PMID: 40807168) These consistent benefits across regimens contrast with historical TKI-era data, further supporting CN's resurgent role in the IO era.(53)

## Timing Considerations: Upfront Versus Deferred CN

Meagher et al. analyzed CN timing in 118 mRCC patients receiving either immune checkpoint inhibitors or TKIs, comparing outcomes based on whether systemic therapy was initiated before or after CN.(54, PMID: 38521546) Patients who received systemic therapy before CN demonstrated significantly better outcomes (HR 0.67, p=0.039) compared to those undergoing CN-first approach.(54) Furthermore, among patients who underwent CN, IO-based therapy was associated with superior survival versus TKI therapy (OS rate at 30 months ~50% for CN plus IO versus 23% for IO alone).(54) These findings align with SURTIME's deferred CN results, suggesting that initial systemic therapy serves multiple functions: early metastatic disease control, identification of favorable responders who merit surgical consolidation, and avoidance of surgery in rapid progressors with intrinsically resistant biology.(41,54)

The biological rationale for deferred CN extends beyond patient selection. Treatment-induced changes in the primary tumor microenvironment—including increased CD8+ T cell infiltration, PD-L1 upregulation, and vascular normalization—may render post-treatment nephrectomy specimens more immunologically "hot" and facilitate subsequent metastatic disease control.(55,56) Conversely, immediate CN may deprive ICI combinations of neoantigens released during therapy-induced tumor cell death, potentially blunting systemic immune priming.(57) Prospectively testing these hypotheses requires randomized trials incorporating correlative studies on pre- and post-treatment tumor specimens, currently underway in PROBE and NORDIC-SUN.(16,17)

## Prospective Trial Landscape and Future Research Directions

## Ongoing Randomized Controlled Trials

Two pivotal phase III trials are actively recruiting to definitively establish CN's role in the ICI era. The PROBE trial (NCT04510597) randomizes patients to IO-based therapy (nivolumab plus ipilimumab or IO-TKI combinations with axitinib) with or without cytoreductive nephrectomy, with overall survival as the primary endpoint.(16) All patients receive three months of pre-randomization IO-based therapy, ensuring initial disease control prior to CN consideration and excluding rapid progressors. This design directly tests whether adding CN to contemporary IO regimens improves survival in appropriately selected patients.(16)

The NORDIC-SUN trial (NCT03977571) employs a deferred CN design wherein patients with synchronous mRCC receive three months of nivolumab plus ipilimumab or IO-TKI combinations, then undergo randomization to deferred CN versus no surgery if they have ≤3 IMDC risk factors and resectable disease.(17, PMID: 38402173) Patients initially deemed ineligible undergo re-evaluation at six months for potential delayed randomization. The primary endpoint is overall survival, with integrated translational research examining predictive biomarkers of CN benefit including primary tumor molecular features, circulating immune cell populations, and ctDNA dynamics.(17) Results anticipated in 2026-2027 will inform evidence-based guidelines for CN patient selection and timing.

## Integration of Molecular Profiling and Liquid Biopsy

Precision oncology approaches incorporating comprehensive genomic profiling, transcriptomic subtyping, and longitudinal ctDNA monitoring hold promise for individualizing CN decisions. McKay et al.'s demonstration that bone metastases are enriched for angiogenic/stromal subtypes while liver metastases harbor complement/β-oxidation signatures suggests that metastatic distribution patterns may serve as surrogates for underlying biology.(22) If corroborated, patients with bone/liver-predominant disease harboring angiogenic subtypes might preferentially benefit from IO-TKI combinations and potentially from CN to debulk the primary angiogenic nidus, whereas lung-predominant disease with immune-enriched subtypes may respond equivalently to IO-IO without requiring surgical intervention.(22,36)

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) offers non-invasive assessment of tumor burden and clonal dynamics. Takemura et al. characterized mRCC patients by CN timing (upfront within 3 months before metastatic diagnosis versus deferred after systemic therapy initiation), demonstrating feasibility of ctDNA tracking across these cohorts.(58, PMID: 37914579) Hypothetically, patients demonstrating rapid ctDNA clearance on initial IO therapy—indicative of treatment-sensitive biology—may represent optimal CN candidates for surgical consolidation, whereas persistent or rising ctDNA despite therapy signals resistant clones unlikely to benefit from CN.(59,60) Prospective validation of ctDNA-guided CN algorithms could refine patient selection beyond clinicopathologic risk models.

## Site-Directed Therapy and Oligoprogression Management

The recognition of lesion-level heterogeneity and organ-specific treatment sensitivity raises the possibility of hybrid approaches combining systemic therapy with site-directed interventions. For patients exhibiting oligoprogression—progression at one or two sites while maintaining disease control elsewhere—stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) or metastasectomy to progressing lesions may extend systemic therapy duration without necessitating immediate regimen switch.(61,62) A 2023 analysis of mRCC patients receiving IO-TKI combinations found that 18% developed oligoprogression, with median time to subsequent systemic therapy of 12.8 months in those receiving local consolidation versus 4.2 months with immediate systemic change (HR 0.42, p=0.01).(63) Analogously, patients with "low-high" phenotype lesions (low response probability, high progression probability) at bone or brain sites might benefit from prophylactic SBRT concurrent with systemic IO-TKI, preventing symptomatic progression and hetero-organ progression patterns associated with poor survival.(29)

## Advanced Imaging and Radiomics

Functional imaging modalities including dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) and PET-CT with novel tracers offer opportunities to non-invasively assess lesion-level biology and treatment response beyond anatomic size criteria. DCE-MRI parameters (Ktrans reflecting vascular permeability, ve reflecting extravascular extracellular space) correlate with angiogenic gene expression signatures in ccRCC and predict response to VEGF-TKI therapy.(64,65) In principle, baseline DCE-MRI could identify which individual metastases harbor high angiogenic activity likely to respond to IO-TKI combinations, while low-angiogenic lesions might preferentially benefit from dual immunotherapy or warrant site-directed therapy.(64)

Radiomics—high-throughput extraction of quantitative imaging features from routine CT scans—enables computational phenotyping of individual lesions. Texture features (entropy, homogeneity, energy) correlate with intratumoral heterogeneity and hypoxia, both adverse prognostic factors.(66) A hypothetical lesion-level predictive model integrating baseline radiomics (100+ features per lesion), organ site, molecular subtype, and treatment regimen could achieve AUC 0.75-0.80 for predicting individual lesion response, enabling prospective identification of mixed responders and guiding site-directed interventions.(30,66) Validation requires multicenter datasets with standardized imaging protocols and harmonization of radiomic feature extraction methodologies.(67)

## Clinical Implications and Treatment Algorithm Recommendations

## Precision Biomarker Assessment Strategy

Current evidence supports a shift from primary tumor-centric to metastasis-centric biomarker assessment. For patients with accessible metastatic sites (lung nodules amenable to CT-guided biopsy, superficial lymph nodes, bone lesions suitable for core biopsy), tissue acquisition for PD-L1 immunohistochemistry, molecular subtyping (ccrcc1-4 classification), and comprehensive genomic profiling should be prioritized over reliance on archival nephrectomy specimens.(19-21) In cases where metastatic biopsy is unfeasible due to anatomic constraints or bleeding risk, liquid biopsy with ctDNA-based genomic profiling and circulating PD-L1 assessment may provide surrogate information, though correlations with tissue-based biomarkers remain imperfect and require further validation.(68,69)

When discordance exists between primary and metastatic biomarker profiles—as occurs in ~20-33% of paired analyses for PD-L1 and TIM-3—treatment selection should prioritize metastatic tissue findings as these reflect the biology of sites actually targeted by systemic therapy.(19,21) For example, a patient with PD-L1-negative primary tumor but PD-L1-positive lung metastases would be anticipated to derive meaningful benefit from checkpoint inhibition based on metastatic expression, whereas primary tumor status alone might erroneously classify them as poor immunotherapy candidates.(19)

## First-Line Regimen Selection Framework

Integrating spatial heterogeneity principles into first-line treatment selection yields the following evidence-based framework for intermediate/poor-risk mRCC:

**Liver-predominant or bone-predominant disease:** IO-TKI combinations (cabozantinib-nivolumab, lenvatinib-pembrolizumab, axitinib-pembrolizumab) preferred based on site-specific efficacy data showing superior outcomes versus IO-IO in these organ contexts (Level of evidence: retrospective comparative, Grade C).(23,48)

**Lung-predominant or lymph node-predominant disease:** Either IO-IO (nivolumab-ipilimumab) or IO-TKI combination acceptable, with choice guided by patient preferences regarding toxicity profiles, convenience (IV-IV versus PO-IV), and institutional experience (Level: retrospective comparative, Grade C).(23)

**Brain metastases:** IO-TKI combinations with known CNS penetration (cabozantinib-nivolumab) may be preferred, though prospective CNS-specific outcome data remain limited. Local therapy (SBRT, surgical resection) for symptomatic or large (>3 cm) lesions should be integrated with systemic therapy initiation (Level: expert opinion, Grade D).(70)

**Favorable-risk disease:** CheckMate 214 demonstrated no OS benefit of nivolumab-ipilimumab over sunitinib in favorable-risk patients (HR 1.19, 95% CI 0.77-1.85), and this subgroup was excluded from subsequent IO-TKI trials.(44) For favorable-risk patients, TKI monotherapy (pazopanib, sunitinib, cabozantinib) remains appropriate first-line treatment, with VEGF-TKI plus PD-1 combinations reserved for those who decline monotherapy or have specific contraindications (Level: RCT subgroup, Grade B).(44,71)

## Cytoreductive Nephrectomy Decision Algorithm

In the absence of definitive prospective trial results, the following represents a synthesis of current evidence for CN decision-making:

**Do NOT perform upfront CN in:**

- IMDC poor-risk with ≥2 risk factors (CARMENA subgroup: inferior outcomes with CN plus sunitinib versus sunitinib alone)(39,40)
- Patients with brain metastases, liver metastases, or bone metastases and ECOG PS ≥2 (IMDC database: CN benefit restricted to good PS, absence of visceral metastases)(72)
- Patients unlikely to tolerate major surgery due to comorbidities (cardiac, pulmonary, renal dysfunction) or expected survival <6 months

**Consider deferred CN (after 3-6 months initial IO-based therapy) in:**

- IMDC intermediate-risk with 1-2 risk factors, especially if metastatic disease limited to lung/lymph nodes and patient exhibits CR/PR/SD on first restaging scan (SURTIME paradigm: deferred CN superior to upfront in responders)(41)
- Synchronous mRCC with symptomatic primary tumor (hematuria, pain, paraneoplastic syndrome) that has demonstrated response or stability on initial IO therapy (retrospective IO-era data: OS benefit observed)(45,46)
- Patients achieving excellent metastatic disease control (near-CR) where CN may facilitate treatment discontinuation and prolonged therapy-free interval (emerging real-world experience, Level: case series, Grade D)(73)

**Upfront CN may still be reasonable in:**

- IMDC intermediate-risk with only 1 risk factor, low metastatic burden (≤2 sites, all pulmonary), excellent PS (ECOG 0), and highly symptomatic primary tumor where surgery would be performed for palliation regardless (CARMENA subgroup: numerical benefit trend in 1-risk factor cohort)(40)
- Patients with T3b-T4 primary with renal vein/IVC thrombus where thrombotic complications risk outweighs systemic therapy delay (specialized surgical centers, Level: expert opinion, Grade D)(74)

All CN decisions should be made in multidisciplinary tumor boards incorporating medical oncology, urology, radiology, and pathology input, with explicit consideration of metastatic distribution patterns, molecular features when available, patient preferences, and institutional surgical expertise.(42,43)

## Response Assessment and Surveillance Modifications

Recognition of mixed response patterns and their adverse prognostic significance warrants enhanced surveillance strategies. Patients with ≥3 metastatic organs, bone plus liver involvement, or IMDC poor-risk should undergo imaging every 8 weeks for the first 6 months (versus standard 12-week intervals) to enable early detection of mixed response or hetero-organ progression.(5,26) When mixed response is identified—defined operationally as ≥1 lesion with ≥30% shrinkage concurrent with ≥1 lesion with ≥20% growth—multidisciplinary discussion should address:

1. **Site-directed therapy:** SBRT or metastasectomy to 1-3 progressing lesions if other sites remain controlled
2. **Treatment intensification:** Dose escalation of TKI component (if initially reduced), addition of second agent in TKI monotherapy recipients, or transition from IO-IO to IO-TKI
3. **Regimen switch:** For patients progressing on IO-TKI, switch to alternative IO-TKI (e.g., cabozantinib-nivolumab to lenvatinib-pembrolizumab) or emerging agents (HIF-2α inhibitor belzutifan in VHL-mutant disease)
4. **Clinical trial enrollment:** Particularly for novel triplet combinations, antibody-drug conjugates, or biomarker-selected approaches

Beyond RECIST, attenuation change on CT should be systematically documented for TKI-treated patients, as ≥40 HU decrease predicts progression-free survival with 75% sensitivity and 100% specificity even without size reduction (Size and Attenuation CT criteria).(75) For immunotherapy recipients, iRECIST modifications—requiring confirmation of progression on two consecutive scans ≥4 weeks apart to exclude pseudoprogression—should be applied when feasible.(76)

## Conclusions and Future Paradigm

Metastatic renal cell carcinoma exemplifies the challenges and opportunities of precision oncology in heterogeneous malignancies. The convergence of spatial heterogeneity (primary versus metastatic sites, organ-specific response patterns), temporal heterogeneity (treatment-induced clonal selection), and regimen-specific interactions (differential CN benefit by systemic therapy) necessitates a paradigm shift from patient-level aggregate response assessment toward granular, lesion-specific, organ-stratified treatment strategies.

Emerging evidence establishes that primary tumors and metastatic deposits frequently harbor discordant predictive biomarker expression (PD-L1, c-Met, TIM-3 concordance rates 66-80%), with metastatic tissue more accurately reflecting treatment-targetable biology.(19-21) The 4-fold higher incidence of metastatic-only response versus primary-only response (31% versus 8%) fundamentally challenges assumptions that primary tumor shrinkage serves as a reliable surrogate for systemic disease control.(18) Organ-specific analyses reveal that liver and bone metastases derive greater benefit from IO-TKI combinations than IO-IO, while lung and lymph node sites respond equivalently, suggesting that metastatic distribution patterns should inform first-line regimen selection.(23,48)

Lesion-level response modeling—pioneered in metastatic colorectal cancer and demonstrating that hetero-organ progression confers median OS of only 385 days versus 653 days for mono-organ patterns—offers a methodological blueprint for mRCC.(29) Prospective studies annotating all measurable lesions (not solely RECIST targets) by organ site, applying nonlinear mixed-effects tumor growth modeling and organ-specific Cox regression, and developing machine learning classifiers to predict progression sequences could yield clinical decision support algorithms optimizing treatment selection and surveillance intensity.(29,30) Integration with radiomics, functional imaging, and circulating tumor DNA monitoring would further refine these models.

The cytoreductive nephrectomy controversy, seemingly resolved by CARMENA's demonstration of no benefit in the TKI era, has been reopened by retrospective IO-era data suggesting treatment-specific interactions wherein CN improves outcomes with ICI-containing regimens.(39,45,46,48) Meta-analyses indicating that IO-TKI superiority over sunitinib requires nephrectomy—with HR 0.96 (no OS benefit) in the non-nephrectomy cohort—have profound implications for interpretation of registration trials and treatment guidelines.(51) Prospective trials (PROBE, NORDIC-SUN) testing deferred CN strategies in IO-treated patients will provide Level 1 evidence, while correlative studies examining immune microenvironment changes post-nephrectomy may elucidate mechanisms.(16,17)

The ultimate vision is a precision medicine ecosystem for mRCC wherein treatment algorithms integrate: (1) metastatic tissue-based biomarker assessment (PD-L1, molecular subtype, genomic alterations), (2) metastatic distribution patterns dictating IO-IO versus IO-TKI selection, (3) baseline ctDNA burden and dynamics predicting treatment sensitivity, (4) lesion-level response heterogeneity at first restaging triggering site-directed interventions, (5) deferred CN in appropriately selected responders to consolidate disease control, and (6) continuous refinement via machine learning models trained on real-world data. Such an approach promises to extend median OS from current benchmarks (40-50 months with optimal therapy) toward the 60-72 month range observed in exceptionally responding subgroups, while minimizing futile treatments and surgery-associated morbidity in those with intrinsically resistant disease.(44,49,73)

The coming decade will likely witness clinical implementation of these principles through pragmatic platform trials incorporating adaptive randomization based on early response patterns, integration of imaging-genomic correlates into treatment algorithms, and embedded quality-of-life assessments ensuring that intensified multimodal strategies yield meaningful patient-reported benefit alongside radiographic and survival endpoints. For this paradigm shift to succeed, nephrology/urology, medical oncology, radiology, pathology, and computational biology disciplines must collaborate closely, transcending traditional specialty silos to realize the full potential of precision medicine in metastatic renal cell carcinoma.
