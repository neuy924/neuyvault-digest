## Abstract

Oncology drug access in the European Union is characterised by a fundamental structural discontinuity: European Medicines Agency marketing authorisation is centralised and uniform, while all subsequent pricing, health technology assessment, and reimbursement decisions are national in scope and sovereign in character. This paper synthesises the regulatory, economic, and institutional architecture governing oncology drug access across EU member states from 2020 to 2026, with detailed analysis of Ireland and Greece. It examines how Ireland's National Centre for Pharmacoeconomics applies cost-effectiveness thresholds with oncology-specific flexibility, how managed access protocols function as conditional reimbursement instruments under clinical uncertainty, and how confidential commercial agreements decouple published from effective drug prices. At the EU level, the analysis addresses the legal boundaries of pricing intervention under TFEU Article 168(7), the scope and limits of the HTA Regulation 2021/2282 Joint Clinical Assessment, and the mechanics of voluntary cross-national negotiation through BeNeLuxA. Comparative analysis across all 27 member states reveals a persistent three-tier access hierarchy: high-income Western states with rapid automated access; mid-tier states with functional but slow HTA systems; and Eastern and smaller peripheral states facing structural manufacturer deprioritisation, limited HTA capacity, and reimbursement rates below 35%. Greece represents a transitional case — a recently established HTA system with improving throughput but deteriorating time-to-access trends, constrained by a legislated dependency on prior reimbursement in reference countries. The paper argues that the EU access gap is not primarily a regulatory failure but a compound product of pricing architecture, launch sequencing strategy, HTA capacity disparity, and the fundamental tension between pharmaceutical innovation incentives and national health system affordability constraints.

## Summary

The conversation traced the pathway from EMA marketing authorisation to patient access across EU member states, with Ireland and Greece as focal cases. EMA approval confers market authorisation only — reimbursement is entirely national, creating systematic access inequity. Ireland's pathway runs through NCPE HTA, HSE MMP negotiation, and managed access protocols; typical oncology timelines run 18–30 months. NCPE's €45,000/QALY threshold flexes substantially upward under end-of-life, orphan, and unmet-need criteria, with confidential commercial agreements routinely closing the gap between published and effective ICERs. EU law constrains process (Transparency Directive) but not price, leaving member states full sovereign negotiating latitude subject to ERP interdependencies and free movement obligations. BeNeLuxA operationalises joint negotiation through consensus-based mandate, single-table negotiation, and national implementation of a shared framework — Ireland participates as a price-taking but leverage-gaining member. Across the EU, access stratifies sharply: Germany and Sweden achieve reimbursement within 100 days at near-100% rates; Cyprus, Latvia, Lithuania, and Malta take over three years with rates below 31% or zero. Greece, operating a post-2018 HTA system with a structural 5-country prerequisite trigger, improved from 47% reimbursement in 2020 to 71% by 2024 but faces a worsening time trend despite capacity gains.

# EU/Ireland Oncology Drug Reimbursement Post-EMA Approval | #T1 #T2 #T3 | 🔭 [health-policy] |

---

## EMA Approval → Reimbursement: The Gap

EMA approval grants **market authorisation** (MA) — it does NOT guarantee access or reimbursement. These are entirely separate processes, national in scope.

---

## EU-Level: What Happens After EMA Approval

### HTA Regulation (EU 2021/2282) — Joint Clinical Assessment (JCA)
Since **January 2025**, oncology drugs undergo mandatory **Joint Clinical Assessment** at EU level via **EUnetHTA 21** (now coordinated by ECBH).

- Produces a **joint clinical report**: relative effectiveness vs. comparator, evidence certainty, clinical added benefit
- **Not binding** on national reimbursement decisions
- Replaces duplicated clinical evidence reviews across member states
- **Pricing & reimbursement remain 100% national**

---

## National Reimbursement: Each Member State Decides

Each country runs its own:
- HTA (health technology assessment)
- Economic evaluation (cost-effectiveness, budget impact)
- Price negotiation
- Coverage/reimbursement listing decision

Typical timeline: **12–24 months** post-MA, often longer for oncology.

---

## Ireland: Specific Reimbursement Pathway

### Key Bodies
| Body | Role |
|---|---|
| **HPRA** | Regulatory (mirrors EMA for national MAs) |
| **NCPE** (National Centre for Pharmacoeconomics) | HTA — clinical + economic evaluation |
| **HSE Medicines Management Programme (MMP)** | Reimbursement recommendation |
| **HSE Executive/Minister** | Final listing decision |
| **CervicalCheck/NCCP** | Oncology-specific clinical input |

---

### Ireland Step-by-Step

**1. EMA Grants Marketing Authorisation**
↓
**2. Manufacturer submits to HSE/NCPE**
- Dossier: clinical evidence, economic model, budget impact
- Can now reference JCA report (since 2025)

**3. NCPE Conducts HTA**
- Pharmacoeconomic review: ICER vs. willingness-to-pay threshold (~€45,000/QALY, flexible for oncology)
- Oncology: End-of-life premium, unmet need weighting applied
- Outcome: **Recommend / Recommend with managed access / Do not recommend**
- Timeline: ~6 months (can extend)

**4. HSE MMP Review**
- Commercial negotiation with manufacturer
- Confidential pricing/rebate agreements common
- Budget impact assessment

**5. Managed Access / Compassionate Use (parallel track)**
- **Community Drug Schemes** (GMS, DPS, LTI)
- **Managed Access Protocol (MAP)** for high-cost oncology — conditional coverage with data collection
- **HSE Drugs Payment Scheme** for interim access

**6. Reimbursement Listing Decision**
- HSE CEO/Board or Minister for Health (>€5M budget impact)
- Listed under appropriate scheme

**7. Hospital/Cancer Centre Access**
- NCCP (National Cancer Control Programme) issues clinical guidance
- Drug added to formularies at designated cancer centres

---

### Ireland Oncology-Specific Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Managed Access Protocol (MAP)** | Time-limited coverage pending real-world data |
| **Orphan/rare cancer pathway** | Streamlined for small populations |
| **Compassionate use / named patient** | Pre-reimbursement access via manufacturer |
| **Cancer Drugs Fund equivalent** | Ireland lacks a formal CDF (unlike England's NICE CDF); HSE uses MAPs instead |

---

### Typical Total Timeline: Ireland

```
EMA Approval → NCPE submission:     1–6 months
NCPE HTA review:                    6–12 months
HSE negotiation + decision:         3–6 months
─────────────────────────────────────────────
Total (best case):                  ~12 months
Total (typical oncology):           18–30 months
```

---

### Key Contrast: Ireland vs. Other EU States

- **Germany (AMNOG)**: Automatic reimbursement at launch, then G-BA HTA → negotiated price at 12 months
- **France (HAS)**: SMR/ASMR rating → price negotiation with CEPS
- **Ireland**: No automatic access; full HTA required first; slower than DE/FR; no equivalent of NICE's CDF

### NCPE ICER Threshold Flexibility in Oncology | #T1 #T2 #T3 | 🔭 [health-economics] |

---

## Baseline: Ireland's Standard WTP Threshold

**~€20,000–€45,000/QALY** — informal range, never legislatively fixed.

NCPE uses **€45,000/QALY** as the de facto ceiling for standard conditions. This is soft — oncology routinely exceeds it and still gets recommended.

---

## Factors That Unlock Threshold Flexibility

### 1. End-of-Life (EoL) Criteria
Modelled on NICE's EoL premium. NCPE applies upward flexibility when:
- Life expectancy <24 months without treatment
- Treatment extends life ≥3 months (absolute gain)
- Small eligible population

**Effect**: ICERs up to **€75,000–€100,000+/QALY** have been accepted under EoL framing.

---

### 2. Orphan / Ultra-Orphan Designation
EU orphan designation (<5 in 10,000 prevalence) triggers explicit flexibility:
- Budget impact inherently small → cost-effectiveness less decisive
- NCPE may accept ICER with heavy uncertainty if unmet need is high

**Effect**: Effectively no hard ceiling for ultra-orphan; decision becomes multi-criteria.

---

### 3. Severity of Condition / Unmet Need
NCPE's framework incorporates:
- **Absolute QALY shortfall** (vs. age-matched population)
- **Proportional QALY shortfall**
- High shortfall → higher acceptable ICER

Oncology almost always scores high on both metrics.

---

### 4. Managed Access / Conditional Recommendation
When ICER is high but uncertain (wide CIs, immature OS data — common in oncology):
- NCPE recommends conditional on **MAP with registry/outcomes data**
- Effectively defers hard cost-effectiveness verdict
- Manufacturer often accepts confidential rebate to get within acceptable range

---

## Real-World Examples

| Drug | Indication | Published ICER | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Olaparib** (Lynparza) | BRCA+ ovarian, 1L maintenance | >€100,000/QALY | Recommended via MAP + confidential pricing |
| **Pembrolizumab** (Keytruda) | Multiple oncology indications | Consistently >€80,000/QALY base case | Multiple positive recs; EoL + unmet need applied |
| **Nusinersen** (Spinraza) | SMA (not oncology but benchmark) | >€500,000/QALY | Eventually reimbursed — ultra-orphan, severity ceiling irrelevant |
| **Ribociclib** (Kisqali) | HR+ HER2- breast cancer | ~€70,000–90,000/QALY | Positive rec; EoL criteria marginal, unmet need decisive |
| **Axicabtagene** (Yescarta) | DLBCL (CAR-T) | >€150,000/QALY | Recommended under MAP; budget impact managed via small eligible N |
| **Venetoclax** (Venclyxto) | AML, CLL combinations | Variable; some >€100,000 | Positive recs; confidential rebates assumed to close gap |

---

## The Confidential Rebate Effect

Published ICERs are **pre-rebate**. Almost all oncology reimbursements involve:
- Confidential commercial agreements (CCAs) with HSE
- Effective net price reduces true ICER substantially
- NCPE models the **list price ICER** but recommendation accounts for "scope for commercial negotiation"

So a published ICER of €120,000/QALY may reflect a true negotiated ICER of €50,000–60,000/QALY.

---

## NCPE's Explicit Multi-Criteria Framework

When cost-effectiveness alone doesn't justify, NCPE weighs:

```
Clinical added benefit (vs. comparator)
Magnitude of unmet need
Disease severity / QALY shortfall
Availability of alternatives
Budget impact (absolute €, not just ICER)
Strength / maturity of evidence
Equity considerations (rare/paediatric)
```

No published scoring weights — qualitative synthesis. This opacity is both criticised (predictability) and defended (flexibility).

---

## Practical Implication for Manufacturers

| Scenario | Strategy |
|---|---|
| ICER €45–75k, EoL criteria met | Submit standard; expect positive rec |
| ICER €75–120k, strong unmet need | MAP + CCA; pre-negotiate with HSE MMP |
| ICER >€120k, large population | Almost certainly requires major rebate |
| ICER >€120k, ultra-orphan | Argue multi-criteria; budget impact dominant argument |
| Immature OS data | Accept MAP; real-world data collection built in |

# Drug Price Negotiation Space Within EU Law | #T1 #T2 #T3 | 🔭 [regulatory-economics] |

---

## Foundational Legal Position

EU law **explicitly excludes** pharmaceutical pricing and reimbursement from harmonisation.

**TFEU Article 168(7)**: Member states retain full competence over health system organisation and financing, including drug pricing.

**Transparency Directive 89/105/EEC** (now modernised): Governs *process* only — timelines, transparency, appeals. Does **not** constrain what price a state sets.

→ **Result**: Enormous national flexibility. EU law creates procedural guardrails, not price floors or ceilings.

---

## What EU Law Actually Constrains

### Transparency Directive Obligations
- Pricing/reimbursement decisions must be made within **90 days** (180 for new active substances requiring HTA)
- Decisions must be reasoned and in writing
- Appeals mechanism must exist
- Criteria must be published in advance

### Free Movement (TFEU Art. 34–36)
- Cannot ban parallel imports without justification
- Cannot use pricing policy as disguised trade barrier
- **Parallel trade is legal** — creates de facto price floor pressure

### State Aid Rules (TFEU Art. 107)
- Preferential pricing for domestic manufacturers = potential state aid violation
- Rarely litigated in pharma context but theoretically constraining

### Patent / SPC Framework
- Compulsory licensing permitted under TRIPS Article 31 / EU Regulation 816/2006
- Rarely used in EU (Thailand/India model); political resistance high
- **Belgium 2023 (Spinraza/nusinersen)**: threatened compulsory licence → Biogen settled at undisclosed price. Landmark precedent.

---

## The Real Negotiation Architecture

### 1. Confidential Commercial Agreements (CCAs) / Managed Entry Agreements (MEAs)

Legally unregulated at EU level. Member states design their own:

| MEA Type | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple price-volume** | Rebate if volumes exceed threshold | Common across all MS |
| **Outcomes-based** | Rebate if clinical outcomes not met in real-world | Italy (AIFA), increasingly Ireland |
| **Expenditure caps** | Manufacturer pays excess above budget ceiling | France (CEPS clause de sauvegarde) |
| **Indication-specific pricing** | Different net price per oncology indication | Germany post-AMNOG, Italy |
| **Installment/annuity** | Gene therapy amortised over years | Emerging; Zolgensma precedent |

CCAs are **confidential** — legally protected as commercial secrets under most national law. This creates the **parallel trade problem**: rebated prices in low-price MS leak via parallel imports to high-price MS.

---

### 2. External Reference Pricing (ERP)

Not mandated by EU law but practised by ~24/27 member states.

- Countries peg their price to a basket of other MS prices
- Creates **systemic interdependency**: price in one country affects 20+ others
- Manufacturers strategically sequence launches (**launch sequencing**) to hit high-price markets first
- **EU law does not prohibit ERP** — but it creates de facto constraint on what any single MS can negotiate

**Ireland's basket**: Typically references UK, France, Germany, Denmark, Netherlands, Austria, Finland, Belgium, Sweden, Luxembourg.

---

### 3. Voluntary / Compulsory Price Cuts

**Legal instruments used:**
- Statutory price reductions (e.g., Germany's 7% mandatory rebate for non-NAS generics)
- Clawback mechanisms (Ireland: IPHA agreements with industry body)
- Reference pricing for off-patent medicines
- Tendering (hospital sector, generics/biosimilars)

**IPHA Framework Agreement (Ireland)**:
- Negotiated between HSE and Irish Pharmaceutical Healthcare Association
- Sets discount structures, payback thresholds, generic substitution rules
- Renewed periodically; legally a commercial agreement, not statute

---

### 4. EU-Level Collective Negotiation: BeNeLuxA / Valletta

Not EU law — **voluntary intergovernmental cooperation**:

| Group | Members | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| **BeNeLuxA** | BE, NL, LU, AT, IE (observer→member) | Joint horizon scanning, some joint negotiation |
| **Valletta Declaration group** | MT, CY, SI, HR, EE, LV, LT, PT | Small MS pooling negotiating power |
| **Nordic group** | SE, NO, DK, FI, IS | Information sharing, some joint procurement |

**Ireland joined BeNeLuxA** formally — allows joint negotiation posture without surrendering sovereign reimbursement decision.

**Legal basis**: Intergovernmental MOU. No EU treaty basis required. Confidentiality of joint outcomes maintained.

---

### 5. HTA Regulation 2021/2282 — Pricing Implications

JCA produces **clinical** report only. Explicitly:
- **Article 34**: Pricing and reimbursement decisions excluded from JCA scope
- Member states cannot be forced to accept JCA conclusions in their economic evaluations
- But: shared clinical evidence base → reduces manufacturer ability to present different evidence packages to different MS

**Indirect pricing effect**: If JCA rates added benefit as "low" or "none," manufacturer's negotiating leverage weakens across all MS simultaneously. This is the real pricing impact of JCA.

---

## Compulsory Licensing: The Nuclear Option

Permitted under:
- TRIPS Article 31
- Doha Declaration
- EU Regulation 816/2006 (export to developing countries — narrow)
- **Each MS's national patent law**

**Conditions (under TRIPS/national law)**:
- National emergency / extreme urgency, OR
- Public non-commercial use, OR
- Anti-competitive practices remedy

**EU political reality**: No MS has executed a compulsory licence for a patented oncology drug against manufacturer will — Belgium came closest (2023). Legal risk to single market credibility, industry investment deterrence arguments dominate politically.

---

## Practical Negotiation Leverage Points

```
Manufacturer leverage:          State leverage:
─────────────────────           ──────────────────
Patent monopoly                 Monopsony purchasing power
Regulatory data exclusivity     Non-reimbursement = zero revenue
First-mover in indication       Reference pricing cascade threat
EoL / unmet need framing        Parallel import exploitation
Orphan designation              Compulsory licence threat (credible?)
                                BeNeLuxA joint negotiation
                                Delay / managed access conditionality
                                Budget impact cap / outcomes clause
```

---

## Summary: How Much Room?

| Dimension | Flexibility |
|---|---|
| Set any list price | ✅ Full (MS cannot block market entry, only reimbursement) |
| Refuse reimbursement entirely | ✅ Full sovereign right |
| Negotiate confidential net price | ✅ Full; no EU floor or ceiling |
| Impose outcomes-based rebates | ✅ Full |
| Compulsory licence | ✅ Legal but politically near-unused in EU |
| Reference other MS prices (ERP) | ✅ Full; creates systemic constraint |
| Collective negotiation across MS | ✅ Via voluntary agreements |
| Harmonise pricing EU-wide | ❌ Legally prohibited under TFEU 168(7) |
| Block parallel imports | ❌ Prohibited (free movement) |

# BeNeLuxA Joint Negotiation Mechanics | #T1 #T2 #T3 | 🔭 [health-policy] |

---

## What BeNeLuxA Is

Voluntary intergovernmental collaboration launched **2015** (BE, NL, LU → +AT 2016, +IE 2019).

**Legal basis**: Bilateral/multilateral MOUs between health ministries. No EU treaty instrument. No supranational authority created.

**Core insight**: Small-to-mid markets individually have weak negotiating leverage. Pooled, they represent ~30M people and combined pharmaceutical budgets that manufacturers cannot ignore.

---

## Governance Structure

```
Political level:    Ministers of Health (strategic direction)
        ↓
Operational level:  National HTA/reimbursement body representatives
        ↓
Working groups:     Horizon scanning | Joint assessment | Negotiation
        ↓
Secretariat:        Rotating; no permanent body
```

No binding voting mechanism — **consensus required** for joint actions. Any member can opt out of any specific negotiation without leaving the collaboration.

---

## The Process: Step by Step

### Phase 1: Horizon Scanning (Joint)
- Members share intelligence on pipeline drugs 12–24 months pre-launch
- Identify candidates suitable for joint negotiation
- Criteria: high budget impact, unmet need, expected high price, similar eligible populations across members
- Oncology and rare diseases dominate candidate lists

### Phase 2: Candidate Selection
- Consensus decision on whether to proceed jointly
- Not all drugs are suitable — if one MS has divergent epidemiology, patient numbers, or existing treatment landscape, joint approach may not work
- Each MS retains right to proceed nationally if joint process stalls

### Phase 3: Joint HTA / Evidence Review
- Pre-JCA (pre-2025): members shared national HTA reports, avoided duplication
- Post-JCA (2025+): EU JCA report forms shared evidence base; BeNeLuxA adds country-specific elements
- **Clinical assessment increasingly harmonised; economic models remain national**

### Phase 4: Joint Negotiation Mandate
Each MS's competent body (HSE for IE, RIZIV/INAMI for BE, ZIN for NL, etc.) agrees:
- Common negotiation objectives
- Acceptable price range (confidential)
- Non-negotiable conditions (outcomes clauses, registries, etc.)
- Designated lead negotiator or joint team

### Phase 5: Negotiation with Manufacturer
- Manufacturer faces **single negotiating table** representing all members
- Joint negotiation team presents unified position
- Manufacturer submits single dossier (adapted per country for budget impact)
- Sessions typically in Brussels or via video
- **Confidential throughout** — NDA signed by all parties

### Phase 6: Outcome
Three possible results:

| Outcome | Mechanics |
|---|---|
| **Full joint agreement** | Single framework deal; each MS implements nationally via its own legal instrument (reimbursement listing, CCA) |
| **Partial agreement** | Subset of members reach deal; others negotiate bilaterally using joint process intel |
| **Failure** | Members revert to national processes; joint process still provided intelligence advantage |

### Phase 7: National Implementation
- **Critical**: Each MS must implement through its own national legal process
- Ireland: HSE listing decision, Minister sign-off if >€5M
- Belgium: RIZIV/INAMI reimbursement committee recommendation → Royal Decree
- Netherlands: ZIN advice → Ministry decision → GVS listing
- The **net price is identical or near-identical** across members (purchasing power adjustments sometimes applied)
- Each MS signs its own CCA with manufacturer — same commercial terms, separate legal documents

---

## What Gets Negotiated

```
List price:              NOT negotiated (manufacturer sets; MS cannot block)
Net price/rebate:        PRIMARY negotiation object
Outcomes clauses:        Increasingly standard in oncology
Volume/expenditure caps: Common
Registry requirements:   Real-world data collection conditions
Indication scope:        Which licensed indications are covered
Re-assessment triggers:  When deal is reviewed (new data, new indications)
Access conditions:       Prescriber restrictions, hospital-only, etc.
```

---

## Confidentiality Architecture

- All negotiated net prices **confidential** — protected under national commercial secrecy law
- Members agree not to disclose to other EU states outside the group
- This creates tension with ERP: other MS reference **list prices**, not BeNeLuxA net prices
- Manufacturers accept confidentiality as condition of doing business
- **Problem**: If net price leaks, ERP cascade can collapse the manufacturer's pricing architecture across 20+ countries

---

## Ireland's Specific Position

Ireland joined as **observer 2016 → full member 2019**.

**Why Ireland fits BeNeLuxA:**
- Small market (~5M population) — individually weak leverage
- High-income, willing-to-pay
- Similar HTA rigour to BE/NL
- NCPE methodology compatible with ZIN/KCE approaches

**Ireland's operational role:**
- HSE MMP participates in joint negotiation teams
- NCPE shares HTA outputs with KCE (Belgium) and ZIN (Netherlands)
- Ireland often a **price-taker** within the group — BE/NL drive negotiation given larger budgets
- Irish implementation: HSE CCA mirrors BeNeLuxA framework terms

**Practical outcome for Ireland**: Access to drugs at prices it could not have negotiated bilaterally. Estimated **20–40% net price improvement** vs. solo negotiation (no published figure; industry estimates).

---

## Limitations and Criticisms

| Issue | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Slow process** | Consensus requirement delays; manufacturers can play members against each other |
| **Opt-out fragmentation** | If NL or BE proceed nationally, IE loses leverage |
| **No enforcement** | Manufacturer can walk away; no legal compulsion to negotiate jointly |
| **Confidentiality vs. transparency** | Patients/clinicians don't know real prices; budget accountability opaque |
| **Post-deal divergence** | Members implement at different speeds; access timelines still vary |
| **JCA interaction unclear** | How JCA clinical reports integrate with BeNeLuxA economic work still being operationalised |
| **Manufacturer adaptation** | Large manufacturers now have dedicated BeNeLuxA response teams; information asymmetry partially restored |

---

## Comparison to Other EU Collaborative Models

| Group | Joint negotiation? | Binding? | IE member? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BeNeLuxA** | Yes, for selected drugs | No | ✅ Yes |
| **Valletta** | Information sharing + some joint | No | ❌ No |
| **Nordic** | Mainly horizon scanning | No | ❌ No |
| **HERA procurement** (vaccines/crisis) | Yes | Partially | ✅ Via EU |
| **EU HTA (JCA)** | Clinical only, not pricing | Mandatory clinical | ✅ Via EU |

---

## Trend Direction

BeNeLuxA is expanding scope:
- **More oncology and ATMP** (advanced therapy medicinal products) joint negotiations
- Exploring **joint horizon scanning integration with JCA**
- Pressure to add more members (potential: PT, DK)
- Long-term political question: does this evolve toward formal EU pricing coordination — which would require TFEU amendment?

# Oncology Drug Approval & Access Across EU: 2020–2026 | #T1 #T2 #T3 | 🔭 [comparative-health-policy] |---

## The Core Access Divide

EMA approval eliminates regulatory disparities across EU/EEA, but access diverges sharply at all subsequent stages — reimbursement rates and timelines vary enormously, with Central and Eastern Europe consistently trailing Western Europe, and clinical use showing up to 10-fold differences across countries.

---

## Country-by-Country Comparison

### Tier 1: Fastest Access

**Germany**
- AMNOG: automatic reimbursement at launch → G-BA benefit assessment at 12 months → negotiated price
- Time to reimbursement: ~100 days or less
- Reimbursement rate for high-benefit breast/lung cancer medicines: 100%
- Only country achieving the 180-day Transparency Directive timeline for 100% of medicines
- No HTA gate before access; benefit assessment informs price only
- Manufacturers price freely at launch → perennial ERP anchor problem for other MS

**Sweden**
- Time to reimbursement: ~100 days or less
- TLV (Tandvårds- och läkemedelsförmånsverket) HTA: cost-utility analysis, QALY-based
- NT Council for hospital oncology drugs: national recommendation layer on top of TLV
- Early access via compassionate use well-established
- High reimbursement rate; consistent top-tier performer

---

### Tier 2: Mid-Range Access

**France**
- HAS (Haute Autorité de Santé): SMR (medical benefit) + ASMR (added benefit vs. comparator) ratings
- ASMR I–IV → price negotiation with CEPS; ASMR V → generic-referenced price
- Early access: ATU (now AAP — Autorisation d'Accès Précoce) for serious/unmet need; funded pre-reimbursement
- Average time to reimbursement after EMA approval: ~7–11 months in EU5
- 51% of medicines reimbursed within 180 days
- France has higher uptake of newer oncology medicines partly due to shorter MA-to-reimbursement timelines

**Netherlands**
- ZIN (Zorginstituut Nederland): cost-effectiveness assessment; explicit €80,000/QALY threshold (higher for severe disease)
- Sluice procedure for hospital drugs >€2.5M budget impact: mandatory HTA before inclusion
- Reimbursement rate: 92% for high-benefit indications
- 29% reimbursed within 180 days — process sound but slower than Germany/Sweden
- Active BeNeLuxA participant; joint negotiations improve net prices

**Austria**
- HEK (Heilmittel-Evaluierungs-Kommission) assessment
- Hauptverband (HVB) negotiation with manufacturers
- Austria maintains higher uptake of newer oncology medicines with shorter timelines
- BeNeLuxA member; benefits from joint negotiation
- No strict QALY threshold; relative effectiveness + budget impact dominant criteria

**Belgium**
- RIZIV/INAMI: KCE clinical assessment + reimbursement commission recommendation
- Class 1 (therapeutic advance) → full reimbursement negotiation; Class 3 (no added value) → generic pricing
- Only 3% reimbursed within 180 days — process notoriously slow despite high-income status
- Temporary reimbursement (tijdelijke terugbetaling) for urgent oncology access
- BeNeLuxA driver; strong negotiating posture

**Italy**
- AIFA: two-step process — technical-scientific commission (CTS) + pricing/reimbursement committee (CPR)
- Law 648/96: allows hospital use of non-reimbursed drugs for serious conditions (oncology safety net)
- 648 List + Innovative Drug Fund (Fondo Farmaci Innovativi) for high-value oncology
- HTA review time: ~504 days for oncology — among slowest EU5
- Outcomes-based MEAs (registro AIFA): highly developed performance-based agreements; payment-by-result standard
- Regional variation significant: northern Italy access faster than south

**Spain**
- AEMPS + IPT (Informe de Posicionamiento Terapéutico): joint clinical positioning report before regional reimbursement
- CIPM (Comisión Interministerial de Precios) price negotiation
- HTA review time: ~713 days for oncology — slowest EU5
- Hospital formulary decisions then devolve to 17 autonomous communities → massive subnational variation
- Real-world access to same reimbursed drug can vary 6–12 months between regions

---

### Tier 3: Slower/Structural Challenges

**Ireland**
- NCPE HTA + HSE MMP negotiation (detailed in prior exchanges)
- No automatic access; full HTA required before reimbursement
- BeNeLuxA member; MAP mechanism for oncology
- Typical total timeline: 18–30 months
- No CDF equivalent (unlike England)

**Poland**
- MZ (Ministry of Health) central decision; no independent HTA agency — AOTMiT advises
- Drug Programme (Program Lekowy) system: indication-specific, protocol-bound access
- Median time to reimbursement: ~27 months
- Reimbursed proportion of EMA-approved indications: 19–64% range across CEE
- Budget constraint dominant; many drugs denied or delayed indefinitely
- Off-label hospital access exists but inconsistent

**Czechia**
- SÚKL (State Institute for Drug Control): HTA + reimbursement
- Reimbursed proportion: up to 64% — highest in CEE
- Relatively functional HTA system; better than regional peers
- Early access via temporary supply authorisation

**Hungary**
- NEAK (National Health Insurance Fund): reimbursement decisions
- Median time to reimbursement: ~37 months — among slowest in CEE
- Gyógyszerterápiás Tanács (Drug Therapeutic Council) advises
- Individual patient application system for non-reimbursed drugs creates named-patient burden

### Tier 4: Severely Constrained Access

**Cyprus, Latvia, Lithuania**
- Time from EMA marketing authorisation to reimbursement: over three years
- Reimbursement rates: Cyprus and Latvia at 31% for high-benefit indications
- Small markets; manufacturers deprioritise submissions
- Named-patient/compassionate use primary access route
- Valletta group membership provides some collaborative leverage

**Malta**
- Reimbursement rate: 0% for sampled high-benefit breast/lung cancer indications
- Smallest EU market; manufacturers frequently do not submit
- Government procurement on case-by-case basis
- Significant out-of-pocket or medical tourism burden

## Greece: Detailed Profile

### HTA System Background
Greece's formal HTA system launched **January 2018** under Law 4633/2019 — relatively recent by EU standards, established post-austerity crisis.

**Key structural feature — the 5-country prerequisite:**
Under Article 22 of Law 4633/2019, patented medicines are subject to HTA in Greece only if already reimbursed in five other countries with an HTA process from a defined list: Austria, Belgium, France, Germany, Denmark, Spain, Netherlands, Italy, Portugal, Sweden, and Finland.

This effectively means Greece is a **second-wave market by design** — manufacturers must establish reimbursement elsewhere first.

### Process Steps (Greece)
1. EMA approval
2. Manufacturer waits for 5-country reimbursement trigger
3. Pricing application → Ministry of Health Price Bulletin
4. HTA Committee (under MoH) clinical assessment
5. Price negotiation
6. Minister of Health decision
7. Addition to Positive List

### Timeline Data
Median times from regulatory approval to HTA application: 420 days (NAS) and 457 days (orphan drugs); from HTA application to reimbursement: 228 days (NAS) and 417 days (orphan drugs).

Clinical data assessment duration: 114 days (NAS excluding orphans), 203 days (orphan drugs). Price negotiation time: 60 days (NAS) and 170 days (orphan drugs).

### Performance Trend (2020–2024)
The reimbursement rate for novel cancer medicines (EMA-approved 2019–2022) rose from 47% in 2020 to 71% in 2021, remaining above the EU average since then. Average reimbursement time was 568 days in 2024, close to the EU average of 559 days. However, Greece's reimbursement time has increased every year over the past four years while the EU average has remained stable.

### HTA Capacity Improvement
HTA recommendations increased from 60 (first period 2018–2020) to 641 (third period), while the backlog of medicines pending HTA decreased from 89 (January 2020) to 8 (February 2023).

The median clinical assessment time for NAS almost halved from 207 days in the early period to 114 days by 2023.

## 2020–2026 Trend Direction

The EMA's conditional approval framework is evolving substantially through 2026 in response to advancing precision oncology, real-world evidence methodologies, and regulatory harmonisation.

Key shifts across the period:
- **JCA (Jan 2025)**: shared clinical evidence base reduces duplication; early impact still being operationalised
- **Orphan single-arm trial surge**: proportion of single-arm trials underpinning EMA approvals rose from 28.6% to 53.6% between 2017 and 2022 — increases HTA uncertainty burden on all MS
- **Greece trajectory**: improving but slowing — system capacity better; manufacturer submission lag worsening
- **CEE**: persistent structural gap despite GDP growth; political will variable
