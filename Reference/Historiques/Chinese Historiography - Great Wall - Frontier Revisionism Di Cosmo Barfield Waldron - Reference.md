## Abstract (English)

This conversation traces the historiography of China's northern frontier from the Warring States period through the Tang dynasty, using the scholarship of Nicola Di Cosmo, Thomas Barfield, and Arthur Waldron as primary interlocutors. Beginning with Di Cosmo's revisionist argument that Warring States-era walls were offensive instruments of territorial expansion rather than defensive reactions to nomadic aggression, the discussion extends his framework across all seven major Warring States polities, demonstrating that wall-building was a universal technology of Chinese inter-state competition deployed on both inter-Chinese and steppe frontiers. The conversation then examines Barfield's shadow empire model — the claim that steppe nomadic polities were structurally dependent on extracting surplus from centralized Chinese states — and Waldron's argument that the Great Wall as a unified ancient structure is largely a Ming-era construction and modern nationalist myth. These three frameworks are shown to address different dynasties and different questions while collectively dismantling the received narrative of the wall as a timeless boundary between civilization and barbarism. The discussion concludes with Di Cosmo's paleoclimate research program, which adds environmental drivers — specifically East Asian Monsoon variability — to the political-military framework, and subjects the Barfield model to the Uyghur Khaganate (744–840 CE) as a stress test. The Uyghur case confirms the extraction mechanism Barfield describes while breaking his assumptions of nomadic mobility, dyadic structure, and ecological stability, pointing toward a more complex multi-causal model in which climate, political economy, internal social structure, and contingent political events interact to produce steppe imperial rise and collapse.

## Narrative Summary

The conversation began with a biographical sketch of Nicola Di Cosmo and a chapter-by-chapter walkthrough of *Ancient China and Its Enemies* (2002), establishing the book's central argument: that Chinese states were active agents of northern expansion, not passive victims of nomadic aggression. The wall and the nomad were reframed from the outset — the supposed boundary between civilization and barbarism was a construction, not a fact of nature.

From there the conversation drilled into Di Cosmo's **Great Wall revisionism** — his claim that the Warring States walls were offensive instruments of territorial consolidation, built after conquests to hold gained land, not defensive reactions to nomadic pressure. Scholarly reception was mapped: the argument entered mainstream historiography but coexists with rival frameworks rather than having routed them.

This opened a three-way comparison of **Di Cosmo, Barfield, and Waldron** — the three scholars who between them dismantle every layer of the popular Great Wall myth. Di Cosmo showed the early walls were offensive; Barfield showed the nomads weren't simply aggressive raiders but rational extractors operating a parasitic-symbiotic system; Waldron showed the wall as a unified ancient structure is largely a Ming-era construction and modern nationalist projection. Each addressed a different dynasty and a different question, but together they demolished the received narrative.

The Barfield discussion unpacked the **shadow empire model** in detail: nomads can't tax mobile populations, so they extract from sedentary China; strong Chinese state produces strong steppe empire; collapse of one produces collapse of the other; the Mongols are the exception that proves the rule. Khazanov's foundational claim about nomadic non-autarchy was identified as Barfield's theoretical bedrock. Criticisms were noted — Barfield over-relies on Chinese dynastic histories, treats nomadic politics as entirely reactive, and leaves no room for internal steppe dynamics.

The **Warring States wall survey** generalized Di Cosmo's logic. Chu's Fangcheng (facing Qi and Jin), Qi's Long Wall (facing Chu), and Wei's inner walls (facing Qin and Zhao) confirmed that wall-building was a universal technology of Chinese inter-state competition — the nomadic frontier was simply one more arena where the same tool was deployed. The sequence was always the same: campaign, conquest, wall to consolidate. Zhao under King Wuling and Yan after Qin Kai's campaigns were the closest to Di Cosmo's specific model; Chu and Qi had nothing to do with nomads at all.

The thread then turned to **Di Cosmo's climate work** — the later research program (culminating in the forthcoming 2026 Springer volume on the Ordos) that added environmental drivers to the political-military framework. The Ordos sits on the East Asian Monsoon's northern limit; precipitation variability flips the land between farmable and pastoral. Wall location tracked this ecological boundary over centuries, moving north when moisture permitted agriculture and retreating south when aridity reasserted pastoral dominance. The political decisions Di Cosmo had analyzed in 2002 were shown to operate within climatic constraints he hadn't fully theorized at the time.

The **Uyghur Empire (744–840 CE)** became the test case for the climate thesis and simultaneously the stress-test for Barfield. The paleoclimate paper (Di Cosmo et al., *Journal of Interdisciplinary History*, 2018) found a 68-year drought running through most of the empire's second half — and found that this drought did *not* cause the collapse. The empire survived it by diversifying into agriculture, urbanism, and Silk Road intermediation. What killed the Uyghurs was a succession crisis in 839, elite defection to the Kyrgyz, and a single devastating raid on Ordubaliq in 840, compounded by plague and famine. Climate set structural brittleness; politics pulled the trigger.

The Uyghur case was then evaluated against **Barfield's shadow empire model** in detail. The extraction mechanism fit perfectly: silk-for-horses, An Lushan dependency, collapse when Tang weakened. But the Uyghurs broke three of Barfield's implicit assumptions: they sedentarized (Ordubaliq as a real city of 33 km²); they inserted the Sogdians as a powerful third-party commercial intermediary, making the structure triadic rather than dyadic; and they adopted Manichaeism as a state ideology, creating a literate cosmopolitan identity that transformed the social structure his model treats as fixed. Di Cosmo's climate work added a fourth break: Barfield treats steppe ecology as a constant background; paleoclimate data shows it was a variable that shaped nomadic adaptive strategies and therefore their political resilience.

---

## Major Themes

**1. Agency inversion.** Across all topics, the conversation consistently reversed the vector of historical causation. Chinese states were expansionary actors, not defensive victims. Walls were tools of conquest, not shields against it. Nomadic empires were rational extractors, not irrational raiders. Steppe empire formation was partly provoked by Chinese state-building, not independent of it.

**2. Methodological pluralism.** Three distinct methodological registers converged: political-military history (Di Cosmo 2002, Waldron), political economy (Barfield, Khazanov), and environmental history / paleoclimatology (Di Cosmo 2014–2026). Each register illuminated different aspects and exposed the blind spots of the others.

**3. The wall as index.** The Great Wall — or rather the ensemble of walls — functioned throughout as a diagnostic object. Its location, timing, and function indexed ecological boundaries, state capacity, inter-state competition, factional politics, and nationalist myth-making simultaneously. No single explanation sufficed; the wall demanded multi-causal analysis.

**4. Model stress-testing.** Each major framework was subjected to a case that strained it. Di Cosmo's offensive thesis was tested against non-northern walls (Chu, Qi). Barfield's shadow empire model was tested against the Uyghurs. The climate thesis was tested against the paradox of a 68-year drought that didn't cause collapse. In each case the model survived but required qualification.

**5. The nomad as historical subject.** Running beneath everything was a sustained effort to restore nomadic peoples as agents with internal politics, economic strategies, adaptive repertoires, and ideological lives — not merely as external pressure on Chinese civilization.

## Bio

Nicola Di Cosmo is an Italian historian specializing in historical relations between China and Inner Asia from prehistory to the modern era, with particular emphasis on early interactions between China and steppe nomads, nomadic empires, Mongol history, Manchu history, and the integration of climatological and palaeoscientific data into historical analysis.

He earned his PhD from the Department of Uralic and Altaic Studies at Indiana University in 1991, was a research fellow at Cambridge (1989–92), a Rockefeller Fellow and lecturer at Indiana (1992–93), an assistant professor at Harvard (1993–97), and taught at the University of Canterbury (1998–2003). In 2003 he was appointed Luce Foundation Professor in East Asian Studies at the Institute for Advanced Study.

His major works include *Ancient China and Its Enemies*, *Manchu-Mongol Relations on the Eve of the Qing Conquest*, and *Venezia e i Mongoli*. His current research focuses on connections between climate, environment, and politics in the history of steppe empires.

## Chapters

The book is organized into four parts of two chapters each:

> **Part I** — The Steppe and the Northern Zone  
> **Part II** — Chinese Perceptions and Early Contacts  
> **Part III** — The Xiongnu Empire and Han Warfare  
> **Part IV** — Sima Qian and the Historiography of the North

---

### Ch. 1 — The Steppe Highway: Nomadism as a Eurasian Phenomenon

Di Cosmo surveys major theories that have tried to explain the rise of the nomadic way of life, concluding that no single key exists. The emergence of nomadism is explained as a combination of environmental, technological, and social factors — advances in horse-riding and possibly chariot use were crucial in allowing a shift from part-time pastoral economy to full-scale nomadic life. This chapter situates China's frontier problem within a vast Eurasian context, so the reader understands that nomadism was not a local aberration but a continental development.

---

### Ch. 2 — Bronze, Iron and Gold: Nomadic Cultures on China's Northern Frontier

Di Cosmo emphasizes that "one cannot see, in the Northern Zone as a whole, a linear evolutional continuum." Instead, several parallel pastoral societies were likely emerging in northwestern, north-central, and northeastern sub-zones, interacting among themselves and with semi-pastoral and sedentary neighbors. Archaeological evidence is synthesized to map the material evolution of frontier cultures — their metallurgy, weaponry, and burial practices — across roughly 900–300 BCE.

---

### Ch. 3 — Beasts and Birds: Early Chinese Perceptions of Northern Peoples

Di Cosmo breaks from the tradition of simplistic selected citations that portrayed Chinese attitudes toward northern peoples as monolithic racism. His careful reading of received texts shows that different — sometimes contradictory — doctrines co-existed: proponents of aggressive conquest were opposed by supporters of peaceful policy, and harsh statements about the aliens' "bestiality" did not prevent Chinese states from trading, allying, and intermarrying with neighbors. This chapter demolishes the Han-period retrospective view that "Barbarian = Other" was always a fixed Chinese ideological position.

---

### Ch. 4 — Walls and Horses: First Historical Contacts Between Horse Nomads and Chinese States

This chapter examines the Warring States period (453–221 BCE), when Chinese polities first confronted mounted steppe raiders in systematic ways. Di Cosmo offers a revisionist argument about the Great Wall — he does not hesitate to defy generations-old theories in his explanation for the construction of the Great Wall, reframing it not as a purely defensive reaction to nomadic threat but as part of an aggressive state-building and territorial projection strategy by Chinese states competing with each other and with the frontier.

---

### Ch. 5 — Those Who Draw the Bow: The Rise of the Xiongnu Nomadic Empire

Di Cosmo gives a new interpretation for the formation of the Xiongnu empire. Rather than treating it as an organic tribal confederation, he argues that Xiongnu state formation was catalyzed partly by the pressure and stimulus of Chinese imperial unification under Qin — a relational model where the Chinese empire and the steppe empire co-produced each other. This is one of the book's most original contributions.

---

### Ch. 6 — From Peace to War: China's Shift from Appeasement to Military Engagement

Di Cosmo traces Han dynasty policy from the *heqin* (peace-and-kinship) appeasement strategy of the early Han — sending silk, grain, and imperial brides northward — to the aggressive military campaigns of Emperor Wu (r. 141–87 BCE). This chapter covers China's shift from appeasement to military engagement, analyzing the economic, ideological, and political pressures that eventually made the costly wars against the Xiongnu politically viable and ideologically necessary.

---

### Ch. 7 — In Search of Grass and Water: Ethnography and History of the North in the *Shiji*

Di Cosmo scrutinizes the way in which the great Chinese historian Sima Qian portrayed the Xiongnu empire in his *Records of the Grand Historian* (c. 99 BCE), the first written narrative of the northern nomads in Chinese history. Di Cosmo reads it not as neutral reportage but as a constructed ethnographic text, asking what Sima Qian chose to include, exclude, and frame — and why.

---

### Ch. 8 — Taming the North: The Rationalization of the Nomads in Sima Qian's Historical Thought

Chinese cultural definitions are explained as the expression of political goals — for example, the need to cast enemies in a negative light — and the result of historical processes. Di Cosmo shows that Sima Qian's portrayal of the Xiongnu as savage and irrational served a specific ideological function within Han statecraft: it legitimized military expansion while also grappling honestly with the Xiongnu's formidable power. This chapter is essentially an exercise in historiographical deconstruction before the term was fashionable.

## The Traditional View (What Di Cosmo Overturns)

The standard interpretation — from Owen Lattimore onward — held that the walls were a **defensive reaction**: pastoral nomads raided sedentary farmers, Chinese states panicked, and walls went up to keep barbarians out. The wall marked an ecological and cultural boundary that already existed; it just formalized it in stone and rammed earth. Nomadic aggression was the cause; the wall was the effect.

---

## Di Cosmo's Revisionist Argument

The construction of walls on China's northern frontier has traditionally been viewed as marking worsening relations with pastoral nomadic cultures in the north. Contrary to this view, Di Cosmo argues that the walls were actually part of a campaign for territorial expansion by the Chinese states of the Warring States period.

The core inversion: **the wall was an offensive instrument, not a defensive one.** Three interlocking claims support this:

**1. The walls preceded large-scale nomadic threat.**
The early walls — built by Zhao, Yan, and Qin in the 4th–3rd centuries BCE — predate the Xiongnu confederacy. If the Xiongnu didn't yet exist as a unified military threat, the walls couldn't have been a response to them. Di Cosmo uses archaeological stratigraphy and textual dating to make this case.

**2. Walls enabled projection, not just protection.**
Di Cosmo challenges long-held views on the Great Wall's purpose, asserting it protected nomadic territories from Chinese expansion. The walls functioned as forward operating bases — garrisoned outposts from which Chinese military power could be projected into the steppe. They secured supply lines, anchored colonization of newly seized land, and kept Chinese armies in the field farther north than would otherwise be sustainable.

**3. The walls were a tool of inter-state competition, not Sino-nomadic conflict.**
As the central states expanded into the lands of nomadic and semi-nomadic peoples, they built walls not to stop raiders but to consolidate territorial gains against *each other* and against frontier peoples they were actively displacing. The walls marked conquered territory, not a line of fear.

---

## Implication

The Xiongnu confederacy, rather than being the *cause* of wall-building, may partly be an *effect* of it. Chinese state expansion and wall-projection into the steppe disrupted and compressed nomadic groups, creating the political pressure that accelerated Xiongnu unification under Modu Chanyu (c. 209 BCE). Wall and empire are thus co-produced — each side's consolidation provoked the other's.

This view of a radical difference between the ecological, economic, and political forms of organization on both sides of the Chinese walls is nowadays heavily criticized as ahistorical and generalizing — archaeology has recently contributed important data from the region north of the Great Wall(s), indicating that the subsistence of semi-sedentary people was diverse and included agriculture — further undercutting the old "hard border between two worlds" narrative that the defensive-wall thesis depended on.

## Reception: Broadly Accepted, Selectively Contested

The argument landed as one of the more **durable** revisionist moves in the field — it entered Wikipedia's standard article on the Great Wall's history, appears in textbooks, and is now treated as a credible alternative to the defensive thesis. But it sits within a contested landscape of frontier theories, and not everyone accepted every element.

---

### What Stuck

The walls were built far to the north and included traditionally nomadic lands, indicating the northward expansions of the three northern states and their desire to safeguard recent territorial acquisitions. This theory is supported by the archaeological discovery of nomadic artifacts within the walls, suggesting the presence of pre-existing or conquered barbarian societies.

That archaeological point — nomadic material culture *inside* the wall line — was particularly hard to dismiss. It's empirical, not merely interpretive, and gave Di Cosmo's thesis concrete grounding that the older purely-textual defenses of the defensive thesis could not match.

Di Cosmo has pointed to an important shift in Chinese military strategy during the Warring States Period — this framing, foregrounding strategy over reaction, was widely adopted.

---

### The Barfield Tension

The main intellectual competitor isn't a direct rebuttal but a rival model. Thomas Barfield's *Perilous Frontier: Nomadic Empires and China 221 B.C. to AD 1757* drew large-scale attention from scholars to nomadic-sedentary relations, prompting renewed debate about why ancient nomads chose to invade agricultural areas and what decisive factors determined those relations.

Barfield's framework emphasized nomadic extortion — raids as an economic strategy to extract Chinese goods — which implies *nomadic initiative* drove the relationship. Di Cosmo's model reverses the vector: Chinese expansion drove nomadic consolidation. The two models aren't fully incompatible (one can apply to different periods or scales), but they pull in opposite directions on the question of agency.

Mongolian historian Sechin Jagchid argued that nomads launched large-scale invasions of China only when China used its walls to shut off trade with them — from that perspective, walls were only a small and occasionally counterproductive part of Chinese strategy. Jagchid's reading partially converges with Di Cosmo (walls had non-defensive functions) but for entirely different reasons.

---

### The Waldron Contrast

Waldron's *The Great Wall of China: From History to Myth* (1990), predating Di Cosmo, had already argued the "Great Wall" as a continuous entity was largely a Ming-era construction and nationalist myth — so Waldron cleared the ground by decoupling early walls from the later iconic structure. Waldron argued that every Chinese regime had to answer the question of whether and where to draw the line between steppe and China proper — a more politically contingent reading than Di Cosmo's structural-expansionist one. Waldron saw wall-building as a failure mode, a fallback when other strategies collapsed; Di Cosmo sees early walls as active tools of conquest. These aren't in direct conflict — they address different dynasties and different walls — but the framing diverges.

---

### The Remaining Soft Spot

It is entirely possible, as Western scholars like Di Cosmo and Lattimore suggest, that nomadic aggression against the Chinese in coming centuries was partly caused by Chinese expansionism during this period — but "entirely possible" is the operative hedge. Critics note the evidential base for Warring States wall chronology remains incomplete, and that proving walls *preceded* serious nomadic military threat (rather than coincided with early stages of it) requires dating precision that the archaeology doesn't always deliver. Di Cosmo's own caution about leaving questions open was noted by reviewers as a strength, but it also meant the thesis was framed probabilistically, not definitively.

---

### Bottom Line

The argument is now **mainstream enough to be the standard "revisionist" position** taught and cited — it displaced the naive defensive reading — but it coexists with Barfield's model rather than having routed it. The field treats early wall construction as offensive/expansionist (Di Cosmo), later wall elaboration as politically contingent (Waldron), and the whole Sino-nomadic dynamic as economically symbiotic and mutually constitutive (Barfield/Jagchid). Di Cosmo's contribution was establishing the first plank.

## Thomas Barfield

### Bio

A social anthropologist who received his PhD from Harvard, Barfield is currently a professor at Boston University. He conducted ethnographic fieldwork with nomads in northern Afghanistan in the mid-1970s, as well as shorter periods of research in Xinjiang, China, and post-Soviet Uzbekistan. His work on nomads is grounded in lived fieldwork, not just texts — which shapes his model's anthropological logic. In 2006 he was awarded a Guggenheim Fellowship leading to *Afghanistan: A Cultural and Political History* (2010). His more recent *Shadow Empires* (2023, Princeton) extends the framework from China to a global theory of empire.

---

### The Extortion Model

The core claim: the nomadic empires of the Xiongnu, Mongols, and Uyghurs were "shadow empires" — secondary formations rising and falling in tandem with the primary empire, China. Unable to tax their own too-mobile and pasturing population, they lived on extracting wealth from China.

The structural logic has three parts:

**1. Nomads can't fund states internally.** Pastoral economies don't generate taxable surplus. You can't run an empire on sheep. This idea — that nomad societies were non-autarchic and hence dependent on trading with or raiding neighboring agricultural peoples — is derived from A.M. Khazanov, who made this the central thesis of his 1984 book *Nomads and the Outside World*. Barfield builds on Khazanov and politicizes it.

**2. Raiding vs. conquest is a rational choice.** Imperial nomad confederacies arose in Mongolia and extorted resources from China rather than attempting to conquer it. Why? Conquest means administering a sedentary population — hard, expensive, alien work that destroys the nomadic social structure. Extortion (tribute, heqin gifts, border market access) gives you the goods without the governance burden. Barfield argues their relationship with the Chinese was as much symbiotic as parasitic, and that they understood their dependence on a strong and settled Chinese state.

**3. Strong China → strong steppe empire; weak China → steppe fragmentation.** This is the counterintuitive payoff. Barfield sees the existence of strong Chinese states and real steppe empires as linked. Leaders of steppe empires could only maintain their states by extorting and raiding resources from a centralized Chinese state. When China fragments — Three Kingdoms, end of Han — the steppe empires collapse too, because the extraction target disappears. The shadow has no substance without the body casting it.

**The Mongols as exception.** The best-known steppe empire, that of the Mongols under Temujin and his successors, was an anomalous exception to the 2,000-year pattern. Typical steppe empires were interested in extortion (or tribute, or gifts, depending on who tells the story), not direct rule. Barfield's model is specifically built to explain why the Mongols were *weird*, not typical.

---

### Main Criticism

Barfield has been accused of forcing history to meet his theory. He appears to make a great deal of interpretation, relying mostly on official dynastic histories of China translated into English — essentially taking his fieldwork experience among Afghan pastoralists and trying to imagine what 2,000 years of tribes may have been thinking. The deeper critique: nomadic state formation is treated as entirely reactive to China, leaving no room for internal steppe dynamics, environmental drivers, or inter-tribal politics as independent causes.

## Arthur Waldron

### Bio

Arthur Waldron (born December 13, 1948) is an American historian. Since 1997, he has been the Lauder Professor of International Relations in the department of history at the University of Pennsylvania. He works chiefly on Asia, China in particular, often with a focus on the origins and development of nationalism, and the study of war and violence. Waldron studied Ming Dynasty history at Harvard, focusing on why relations between the sedentary Ming and the nomadic Mongols to the north often turned hostile.

---

### The Nationalist Myth Argument

Waldron's *The Great Wall of China: From History to Myth* (1990) makes two separable claims — one historical, one ideological.

**Historical claim — the Wall is Ming, not ancient.**
The notion of an ancient and continuously existing Great Wall, one of modern China's national symbols, is in fact a myth. Most of what we today call the Great Wall was built during the Ming dynasty (1368–1644). Earlier walls built by Warring States polities and Qin/Han were disconnected, locally varying earthworks with no physical continuity to what tourists visit today. The "Great Wall" as a single unified structure was essentially invented by the Ming.

**Why the Ming built it — internal politics, not ecology.**
Waldron suggests that domestic political conflict, not cultural or ecological factors, determined why and when defensive walls were built. Despotic, palace-reared Ming rulers, fearful of potential Mongol invasion, chose wall-building over trade or diplomatic relations. The wall was a factional compromise — hawks who wanted expensive military campaigns lost to a coalition that settled for expensive static fortifications. It was a second-best policy born of court politics, not strategic wisdom.

**The Maginot Line verdict.**
The Ming fortifications, like the French Maginot Line, proved ineffective: Manchu warriors entered China in 1644, captured Peking, and established the Qing dynasty. Waldron argues that every Chinese regime had to answer the question of whether and where to draw the line between steppe and China proper. The cosmopolitan model — Tang, Yuan, early Ming, early Qing — used active military campaigning and frontier posts but undertook little systematic wall-building. Wall-building was what regimes did when they'd given up on engaging the steppe.

**The ideological claim — modern myth-making.**
The notion of an ancient and continuously existing Great Wall has grown up and now obscured the historical record — even the maps which chart the Wall's position offer erroneous accounts of a phenomenon which has never been accurately surveyed. Waldron traces how the PRC (and earlier nationalist China) retroactively projected the Ming wall backward to Qin Shi Huangdi, creating a 2,200-year lineage of civilization-defining construction that justifies modern nationalism. The wall that Nixon called "great" in 1972 is a 16th-century structure rebranded as eternal.

---

### How All Three Relate

| Scholar | Wall's function | Who's driving? | Period of focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Di Cosmo | Offensive tool of Chinese expansion | Chinese states | Warring States / Qin / Han |
| Barfield | Extraction target that structured nomadic politics | Nomads (reactively) | 221 BCE–1757 CE |
| Waldron | Factional compromise; nationalist myth | Ming court factions | Ming; modern PRC |

They address largely different periods and questions, which is why they don't directly contradict each other — but together they demolish every layer of the popular myth: the wall wasn't defensive (Di Cosmo), the nomads weren't simply aggressive raiders (Barfield), and the wall as we know it isn't even ancient (Waldron).

The short answer: Di Cosmo's offensive-expansion logic applies **cleanly to Zhao, Yan, and Qin** — the three northern states facing the steppe. For **Chu, Wei, and Qi**, the walls served a different but equally non-nomadic function: **inter-state boundary-marking and southern defense against other Chinese polities**. This actually *reinforces* Di Cosmo's core point — wall-building was an instrument of state competition and territorial consolidation, not a reaction to nomadic pressure — but the specific target of that competition varies by state.

## The Pattern: Seven States, Three Wall Logics

By the early 4th century BCE, nearly 100 small states had been consolidated by conquest into seven major states. Further encouraging this segregation of territory was the trend of building long defensive walls along frontiers, some of which were several hundred kilometers long. The key is that almost none of these walls faced the steppe — most faced other Chinese states.

---

### Chu — Fangcheng: The Oldest Wall, Facing South

Chu is the clearest case of walls as instruments of inter-state rivalry with zero nomadic logic. As early as the 7th century BCE, the Chu State built Fangcheng, the earliest Great Wall, as a defense against the northern fiefs — specifically Qi and Jin — and other potential threats. Chu was a powerful southern state expanding northward into the Central Plains. Chu was ambitious and aimed to take charge of all of central China. In order to defend against attacks from the northern fiefs, especially Qi, Chu began to build Great-Wall-like defenses. Later, Qi's attack on Chu was blocked by the Chu Great Wall, and Jin was also kept out when he brought troops to attack Chu.

Fangcheng ran along Chu's *northern* border — meaning it faced other Chinese states (Qi, Jin/Wei), not the steppe. There were no nomads in that direction. This is Di Cosmo's point generalized: walls mark the edge of consolidated territory and project power *against the rival next door*, not against distant pastoralists.

---

### Qi — The Long Wall: Facing Rival Chinese States

The Long Wall of Qi played a significant role in Qi's military strategy in the fifth and fourth centuries BCE, bolstering its defensive capabilities. In the long term, however, the wall might have inadvertently hindered Qi's southward expansion, placing it in a disadvantageous position versus its rivals.

Qi's wall ran along its *southern* border in Shandong — facing Chu and the Three Jins (Wei, Han, Zhao). Coalition armies inflicted heavy defeats on Qi in the east in 441 and 430 BCE, causing its rulers to erect the Long Wall along Qi's southern border — probably the first-ever construction of a Long Wall in China's history. The trigger was military defeat by other Chinese states, not nomad raids. The wall was a response to inter-state warfare, functioning as a fixed defensive line that freed Qi forces for offensive operations elsewhere — consistent with Di Cosmo's framework of walls as tools of strategic positioning, just aimed at Chinese rivals rather than nomads.

---

### Wei — Inner Walls Against Qin and Zhao

Wei's situation is structurally the most interesting. Wei built walls on its *western* border facing Qin, and on its *northern* border facing Zhao — both Chinese states. Qin state suffered repeated invasions by Wei State at the beginning of the Warring States Period, and Wei's walls were consolidating the gains of those campaigns, then later trying to hold Qin's expansion back. Again: walls as instruments of inter-Chinese state competition. No steppe involvement whatsoever.

---

### Zhao — The Northern Wall: Closest to Di Cosmo's Model

Zhao is the state where Di Cosmo's offensive-expansion argument applies most directly. Zhao under King Wuling (r. 325–299 BCE) adopted cavalry warfare (*hufu qishe* — "barbarian dress and mounted archery"), conquered the Linhu and Loufan peoples on the steppe, and then built a wall to hold those gains. King Wuling continued the territorial expansion and built a great wall at the foot of the Yinshan Mountains. All these efforts made Zhao the third-largest power in the late Warring States Period, next only to Qin and Qi.

The wall at the Yinshan followed the conquest, not preceded it — the sequence is: cavalry reform → military expansion into steppe territory → wall to consolidate. This is Di Cosmo's model in textbook form. The wall wasn't defensive against nomads; it marked land Zhao had just taken *from* them.

---

### Yan — Northern Wall Against Both Nomads and Donghu

Yan's wall is the ambiguous case. Yan was the weakest of the seven powers, squeezed between Qi to the south and the Donghu (eastern nomads, predecessors or neighbors of the Xiongnu) to the north. Its northern wall followed General Qin Kai's campaigns circa 300 BCE, which pushed the Donghu back hundreds of kilometers and then walled the new boundary. Structure: campaign first, wall second — same sequence as Zhao. But here the adversary *was* genuinely nomadic (Donghu), so this is the one wall that partially fits the older defensive narrative. Even so, it consolidates offensive gains rather than simply cowering behind a line.

---

### Qin — The Convergence Case

Qin built walls on multiple frontiers. Its *inner* walls (facing Wei and Han) were straightforwardly about inter-state competition. Its *northern* walls in the Ordos followed campaigns against the Rong and Di peoples — same offensive-consolidation logic as Zhao and Yan. This is what Di Cosmo focused on: the Qin northern walls were built deep into formerly pastoral territory, not on any pre-existing ecological or ethnic boundary.

---

## The Synthetic Picture

The Warring States wall-building phenomenon was essentially **a new military-administrative technology** that all major states adopted as part of the arms race of state-building. The logic — build walls to hold newly conquered territory, secure supply lines, and free mobile forces for further operations — was the same whether the target was another Chinese state (Chu, Qi, Wei) or a pastoral people (Zhao, Yan, Qin north). The nomadic vs. Chinese distinction is *not* the relevant variable. What matters is: **was the state expanding, and did it need to anchor its gains?** If yes, build a wall.

This is actually *stronger* evidence for Di Cosmo than a narrower reading would suggest. If wall-building was a general technology of Chinese inter-state competition and territorial consolidation, then applying it to the northern frontier required no special "nomadic threat" logic at all — states simply used the same tool on the northern frontier they were already using against each other.

## The Core Reorientation

In Chinese history the Great Wall has been regarded primarily as a political boundary, a military frontier, and an economic divide between agriculturalists and pastoralists. A climatic and environmental approach to the northern regions of China where the Great Wall was erected shows a high degree of variability primarily dependent on the East Asian Monsoons, which are responsible for the amount of yearly precipitation, thus causing droughts or above average rainfalls that changed the local ecology.

The move: add a third axis to the existing political-military framework. Di Cosmo isn't *replacing* his 2002 offensive-expansion argument — he's asking what *drove* the expansion decisions in the first place. The answer he finds is climatic variability.

---

## Why the Ordos Specifically

The talk focuses on the Ordos region, located in the upper part of the loop of the Yellow River, where the earliest walls were built in the Warring States period (5th–3rd century BCE), and remained a sensitive political, economic and climatic zone until the early modern period.

The Ordos is the critical test case because it's where Di Cosmo's 2002 argument was most specific — the Warring States walls Zhao and Qin built ran through the Ordos, and Di Cosmo argued they were offensive consolidations of conquest. The new question is: *why that territory at that moment?* The climate answer: the Ordos sits on the knife-edge of the East Asian Monsoon's northern limit — a zone of extreme precipitation variability where small climatic shifts flip the land between farmable and pastoral. The walls track that ecological boundary.

---

## The Monsoon-Frontier Correlation

During the period of agriculturalist empires (the Han and Ming dynasties), the Great Wall was a frontier between farming and animal husbandry, and was located to the south of the Daqing Mountains. In contrast, during the period of pastoralist empires (the Zhao Kingdom of the Warring States, Northern Wei, and Jurchen Jin periods), the Great Wall was the frontier between animal husbandry and nomadism.

The wall doesn't sit at a fixed ecological line — it *moves* with it. When monsoon precipitation pushes north, agriculture follows, settlers move in, and walls consolidate the new farmable zone. When precipitation retreats south, pastoralism reasserts dominance, and the political frontier shifts again. The wall is less a permanent military installation than a moving marker of where intensive agriculture can be sustained.

Climate studies show how this region was subject to drastic environmental changes that may have affected political decisions involving defensive structures and military occupation, or economic decisions such as opening up land to settlers. The relationship between Chinese dynasties and nomadic peoples may also have been subject to climatic variability. An integrated historical and climatic study of the Ordos region will show interdependencies and correspondences between natural and human dynamics.

---

## The Broader Climate-Empire Framework

The Ordos argument fits inside a larger research program Di Cosmo has developed across multiple journal articles. Three decades of climatological research in Mongolia and neighboring regions have transformed our knowledge about the environmental history of Inner Asian empires. High-resolution climatic reconstructions, when placed in historical contexts, provide clues about the nomads' responses to climatic variability, and thus illuminate critical nexuses between economic production, social structures, and political change.

The method: paleoclimate proxies (tree rings, ice cores, lake sediments) now yield year-by-year resolution, letting historians correlate specific political events — the founding of empires, the timing of raids, sudden confederacy collapses — with specific climatic episodes. Key findings from the journal work:

**Uyghur Empire (744–840 CE):** Di Cosmo and colleagues published on "environmental stress and steppe nomads: rethinking the history of the Uyghur Empire with paleoclimate data" — showing the empire's sudden collapse coincided with drought-induced pastoral stress, not purely military defeat.

**Eastern Türk Khaganate (630 CE):** Di Cosmo published on the "interplay of environmental and socio-political factors in the downfall of the Eastern Türk Empire in 630 CE" — a multi-causal model where climate stress weakened the pastoral base, making the polity vulnerable to Tang military pressure.

**Mongol withdrawal from Hungary (1242 CE):** Di Cosmo and Büntgen argued that the Mongols' unexpected retreat from Central Europe was partly driven by anomalous wet conditions that turned Hungarian plains into unsuitable terrain for steppe cavalry — the same level of precipitation that strengthened the Mongol army and helped conquer so much also caused the withdrawal in a different way in a different place.

---

## What This Does to the 2002 Argument

The 2002 offensive-expansion thesis said: walls were tools of Chinese state-building, not responses to nomadic aggression. The climate work adds: the *timing* and *location* of that expansion were constrained by monsoon variability. Chinese states expanded northward into the Ordos *when climate conditions made that territory farmable* — and pulled back or walled defensively when aridity reasserted pastoral dominance. Political agency operated within ecological possibility.

This is a maturation rather than a reversal. Di Cosmo hasn't said "climate caused everything" — the interaction between climate variability, environmental change and pastoral economy reveals political and economic mechanisms behind the extraordinary success of nomad empires, as well as their fragility and vulnerability — illustrating possible uses of climatic and environmental data in historical research, and related methodological questions. Climate is a structural condition; political actors still make choices within it.

---

## The Suppressed Variable (Now Unsuppressed)

This is exactly the blind spot that kept appearing in earlier turns. The 2002 Di Cosmo and the Barfield/Waldron debate were all conducted in the register of political-military history. The climate turn asks: what made the steppe-agriculture boundary shift? What made nomadic confederacies suddenly fragile or suddenly powerful? The answer isn't just internal politics or Chinese military pressure — it's precipitation.

## Background: What the Uyghur Empire Was

The Uyghur Khaganate (744–840 CE) was the dominant steppe power between the Second Türk and Mongol empires. The Uyghurs agreed to intervene on behalf of the Tang Dynasty during the An Lushan Rebellion and in exchange established a political-economic relationship with the Tang that greatly favored the Uyghurs, enormously enriching them at Tang's expense. They operated the classic Barfieldian extortion model: silk-for-horses, unequal marriages, tribute disguised as trade. At their peak in 821, Uyghur armies were campaigning west into Sogdia, containing Tibet, and exploiting Tang dependency. Then, in 840, everything collapsed in a single season.

---

## What Di Cosmo's Paper Actually Found — and Its Counterintuitive Twist

The paper's headline finding is *not* "drought caused collapse." It's the opposite.

Newly available paleoclimate data suggests that the assumption of a direct causal link between drought and the stability of nomadic societies is not always justified. The fact that a severe drought lasting nearly seven decades did not cause the Uyghur Empire to collapse, to wage war, or to disintegrate gives rise to speculations about which of its characteristics enabled it to withstand unfavorable climatic conditions.

The tree-ring reconstruction reveals a severe drought running from roughly 783–850 CE — sixty-eight years. That's almost the entire second half of the empire's existence. The study identifies a sustained drought lasting sixty-eight years that significantly impacted the Uyghur economy. Despite this, the Uyghur Empire maintained stability, possibly through diversification into agriculture and trade.

So drought weakened the pastoral base for nearly seven decades — and the empire *didn't* collapse from it. This directly refutes simple climate determinism. The Uyghurs absorbed the environmental stress through structural adaptation: diversifying into settled agriculture around Ordubaliq, intensifying the silk-extortion trade with Tang, and maintaining military cohesion through their Manichaean religious ideology. Climate degraded their capacity; it didn't end them.

---

## What Actually Killed Them: The Cascade

The collapse when it came was rapid, total, and triggered by internal politics interacting with climate-weakened resilience. The sequence:

**Step 1 — Succession crisis.** The Uyghur Empire reached its peak during the era of Sorye Qaghan in the late 820s and early 830s, but his successor Changshin Qaghan died after an internal power struggle in 839, and it began to decline. In a situation of great confusion due to internal strife among the upper class, a plague spread and livestock were slaughtered, and the capabilities of the Uyghur Empire were greatly reduced.

**Step 2 — Elite defection.** In 840, one of nine Uyghur ministers, Külüg Bagha, rival of Qasar, fled to the Yenisei Kyrgyz and invited them to invade from the north. The Kyrgyz didn't seize an opportunity — they were *handed* one by a losing faction in an Uyghur court struggle.

**Step 3 — Kyrgyz strike.** Külüg Bagha and his Kyrgyz allies struck, annihilating the Uyghur capital of Ordubaliq and with it the Khaganate as a unified political entity in a single strike. In the course of the conflagration both Kürebir and Qasar Qaghan died, and the Tang princess Taihe was captured.

**Step 4 — Plague and famine seal it.** The downfall is recorded in the New Tang History: "Just that year, there was a famine and pestilence, and also heavy snowfalls." The climate-weakened pastoral economy had no surplus to absorb the shock of sudden political disintegration.

**Step 5 — No successor.** The Yenisei Kyrgyz, rather than replacing the Uyghurs as lords of Mongolia, continued to live in their traditional homeland. All available evidence suggests the Kyrgyz did not maintain a significant political or military presence on the Mongolian Plateau after their victories in the 840s. The steppe vacuum persisted for decades — something Barfield's model predicts perfectly: no strong Chinese dynasty extractable surplus, therefore no incentive to build a new steppe empire.

---

## The Integrated Verdict: Climate as Structural Vulnerability, Politics as Trigger

Di Cosmo's real argument is a two-level model:

**Level 1 (structural):** The 68-year drought degraded the pastoral economic base, reduced livestock numbers, shrunk the army's horse supply, and forced economic diversification that pulled Uyghur elites away from purely nomadic social norms. This didn't cause collapse but *narrowed the margins* — the empire became brittle. A polity with healthy pastoral reserves could have absorbed the 839 succession crisis. The Uyghurs couldn't.

**Level 2 (contingent):** Internal factional conflict, elite defection to the Kyrgyz, and the Kyrgyz raid were the proximate causes. The environmental stress did not trigger immediate collapse or migration, indicating resilience strategies — but those strategies had limits, and when court politics generated a crisis simultaneously with plague and catastrophic frost, the combination was fatal.

The implication for the broader framework: drought alone doesn't topple steppe empires — nomadic polities had deep adaptive repertoires. But prolonged environmental stress narrows the political margin for error. What would have been a survivable court crisis in a well-pastured year became an extinction event after seven decades of drought. Climate set the conditions; politics pulled the trigger.

## Where It Fits

The extortion mechanism worked exactly as Barfield predicted. The Uyghurs used their close proximity and military leverage over the wealthy Tang state to obtain silks by various methods and dispatched them via Sogdian merchants westwards along the Silk Roads. The An Lushan Rebellion was the trigger: the Uyghurs agreed to intervene on behalf of the Tang Dynasty in exchange for establishing a political-economic relationship with the Tang that greatly favored the Uyghurs, enormously enriching them at Tang's expense. Silk-for-horses, princess exchanges, favorable "guest" status for Uyghurs in Chang'an — all classic shadow empire mechanics.

Thomas Barfield goes so far as to argue that the rise of steppe nomadic empires was dependent upon a unified China to exploit, as only a unified China could mobilize sufficient wealth for the nomads to extract that would allow them to snowball into an empire. The Uyghur founding maps perfectly onto this: they rose because Tang China was rich, centralized, and suddenly desperate.

The collapse also follows Barfield's shadow logic. The Yenisei Kyrgyz assault on Ordubaliq was more akin to a piratical raid than a conquest. Being unable to siphon a comparable amount of wealth from China as the previous Turkic empires had done, the Kyrgyz would have found it very difficult to unify the steppe tribes around themselves. When the Uyghurs fell, the Kyrgyz couldn't replace them — exactly because Barfield's model predicts you need a functioning Chinese extraction target to sustain a steppe empire, and in the 840s Tang China was itself in terminal decline.

---

## Where It Breaks — Three Structural Deviations

**1. Sedentarization: the self-undermining move**

The Uyghurs built Ordubaliq — a real city, not a camp. Ordubaliq was a great town, rich in agriculture, surrounded by cultivated villages lying close together, with twelve iron gates of huge size, populous and thickly crowded with markets and various trades. The ruins cover an area of 33 km².

This is where Barfield's model generates its own internal critique. Barfield noted: "the more goods a nomadic society acquired the less mobility it had; hence, at some point, one was more vulnerable trying to protect a rich treasure house by moving it than by fortifying it." By building a fixed city, the Uyghurs created a protected storage space for trade goods from China — they could hold a stable court, receive traders, and cement their role in Silk Road exchange. However, the vulnerability that came with having a fixed city was to be the downfall of the Uyghurs.

Barfield's model assumes the shadow empire *stays nomadic* — mobility is what makes it hard to conquer and cheap to govern. The Uyghurs took the wealth extraction logic to its conclusion, accumulated so much that they had to settle to protect it, and in doing so forfeited the strategic advantage that made nomadic empires viable. The model contains its own destruction mechanism when pushed far enough.

**2. The Sogdian-Manichaean third party**

Standard Barfield is dyadic: steppe empire extracts from Chinese empire. The Uyghur case inserts a powerful third actor. By converting to Manichaeism, the Uyghurs made themselves partners with the Manichaean Sogdians, and through these Sogdians the Uyghurs achieved unparalleled amounts of wealth. The Sogdians negotiated highly favorable terms of trade on behalf of the Uyghurs and redirected their caravan routes through the Khaganate.

Large numbers of Sogdian refugees came to Ordu-Baliq to escape the Islamic conquest of their homeland. They converted the Uyghur nobility from Buddhism to Manichaeism. The Sogdians ran the civil administration of the empire and were helpful in outflanking Chinese diplomatic policies that had destabilized the Göktürk Khaganate.

The Uyghurs weren't just extracting from Tang — they were intermediating the entire Silk Road. This transactional triangulation (steppe military power + Sogdian commercial network + Tang silk supply) generated wealth far exceeding anything in Barfield's Xiongnu-era baseline. His model doesn't have a slot for the Sogdians.

**3. The religion variable**

The Uyghur Khaganate evolved into a rather complex pastoral nomadic state with many sedentary features: urbanism, agriculture, multiple written languages and alphabets, a form of currency, a fairly complex system of government, and the sophisticated religion of Manichaeism.

Manichaeism gave the Uyghur elite a literate, cosmopolitan identity entirely alien to earlier steppe polities. It connected them to an international religious network, legitimized sedentary court culture, and created an ideological framework independent of pure military prowess. Barfield's model treats steppe empires as predatory-extractive; the Uyghurs were building something closer to a hybrid civilization. That's a category strain his framework wasn't designed to handle.

---

## Di Cosmo's Climate Work and Barfield

The 68-year drought finding cuts against Barfield from a different angle. Barfield's model is essentially *political-economic*: empires rise and fall based on the availability of Chinese surplus to extract. Di Cosmo's paleoclimate data shows that the Uyghur pastoral base was being degraded environmentally for decades before the political collapse — meaning the shadow empire's viability wasn't just a function of Chinese state strength but also of steppe ecological conditions Barfield's framework treats as constant. The assumption of a direct causal link between drought and the stability of nomadic societies is not always justified — the Uyghurs maintained stability through diversification into agriculture and trade despite severe prolonged drought. But that diversification *was itself the sedentarization* that made them brittle. Climate stress drove adaptive responses that eroded the nomadic social structure Barfield assumes as given.
