## Abstract — English

This conversation surveys the major frameworks, scholars, and controversies of Chinese and Qing dynasty historiography from classical periodization to the contemporary New Qing History debate. Beginning with the dynastic cycle, Mandate of Heaven cosmology, the *zhengshi* official history tradition, Marxist five-stage periodization, and the PRC's three-epoch model, the discussion establishes that Chinese historical time-division has always been politically constituted. The conversation then examines New Qing History as the field's central contemporary controversy, conducting extended analyses of five major scholarly works: Pamela Kyle Crossley's theory of imperial simultaneity in *A Translucent Mirror*; Mark Elliott's institutional analysis of Banner system ethnic boundary maintenance in *The Manchu Way*; Evelyn Rawski's field-reorienting 1996 AHA presidential address *"Reenvisioning the Qing"*; James Millward's frontier economic and administrative history in *Beyond the Pass*; and Ge Zhaoguang's philosophical defense of Chinese civilizational continuity in *Here in "China" I Dwell*. Four analytical tensions structure the conversation throughout: court versus frontier as analytical site; ideology versus institutional reality as historical evidence; scholarly versus political motivation on both sides of the debate; and the epistemological question of whose historical experience constitutes the primary subject of Qing history. The conversation ultimately frames the NQH controversy not as a resolvable empirical dispute but as a paradigm conflict rooted in incompatible assumptions about what history is for, whose sources are primary, and how civilizational identity relates to imperial governance.

## Summary

This conversation constituted an extended graduate-seminar-level survey of Chinese historiography, beginning with the broadest possible frame — how Chinese historians periodize their own history — and progressively narrowing toward the specific scholarly controversy that has most destabilized the field over the past three decades. The opening exchange established the classical dynastic cycle framework rooted in Mandate of Heaven cosmology, the *zhengshi* official history tradition and its *zhengtong* succession debates, the imposition of Marxist five-stage social formation theory after 1949, and the contemporary three-epoch model (Ancient/Modern/Contemporary divided at 1840 and 1949) that encodes a Communist redemption narrative into the basic structure of historical time. The conversation established from the outset that Chinese historical periodization has never been politically innocent — every scheme for dividing time carries embedded arguments about legitimacy, civilizational identity, and the relationship between past and present.

From this broad foundation the conversation moved into New Qing History as the specific site where questions of periodization, ethnic identity, imperial administration, and civilizational continuity converge most explosively. The NQH overview established the school's core claim — that the Qing dynasty was a multiethnic Inner Asian conquest empire rather than simply a Chinese dynasty that absorbed its Manchu rulers — and identified the methodological innovation driving it: the use of Manchu, Mongolian, and Tibetan sources that Han-centric historiography had ignored. The political stakes were made explicit: NQH's multiethnic empire framework potentially destabilizes Chinese territorial claims over Tibet, Xinjiang, and Inner Mongolia by disconnecting these territories from a narrative of continuous Chinese civilizational possession.

The conversation then conducted extended deep dives into four major Western NQH scholars. **Pamela Kyle Crossley's** *A Translucent Mirror* (1999) was examined chapter by chapter, establishing her central argument about "simultaneity" — the Qianlong Emperor's theorization of a universal sovereign identity that was simultaneously and co-equally Manchu, Confucian, Buddhist, and Inner Asian rather than performing these identities strategically. **Mark Elliott's** *The Manchu Way* (2001) was examined in comparable depth, with particular attention to how his institutional focus on the Banner system — its spatial segregation, legal boundary maintenance, language preservation campaigns, and eventual decay — both complements and subtly contradicts Crossley's ideological framework. The comparison between Crossley and Elliott established one of the conversation's most productive analytical tensions: the gap between imperial ideology (what emperors claimed about themselves) and institutional reality (what structures actually produced and failed to maintain Manchu distinctiveness).

**Evelyn Rawski's** 1996 AHA presidential address *"Reenvisioning the Qing"* was examined as the field-defining provocation that crystallized NQH into a recognizable school. The analysis reconstructed Ping-ti Ho's sinicization thesis as the target, traced Rawski's empirical arguments about Mongol marriage alliances, the Lifanyuan, and Tibetan Buddhist patronage as evidence of genuine multiethnic imperial strategy, and identified her methodological demand — that historians learn non-Chinese languages and use non-Chinese archives — as the address's most consequential long-term contribution. **James Millward's** *Beyond the Pass* (1998) shifted perspective from court to frontier, examining how Qing governance of Xinjiang operated through hybrid administrative structures — the beg system, military colonies, Andijani merchant networks — entirely distinct from Chinese administrative models, while also introducing the economic history of frontier subsidies and the violence of Qing expansion that other NQH scholars underemphasized.

The conversation's most significant intellectual counterweight to NQH was the deep dive into **Ge Zhaoguang's** *Here in "China" I Dwell* (2011) — the most sophisticated Chinese scholarly response to the Western revisionist school. Ge's qualified defense of Chinese civilizational continuity was examined carefully: his distinction between primordial essentialism (which he rejects) and historically grounded civilizational coherence (which he defends); his argument that NQH's court-focused archival methodology mistakes the imperial apex for the empire; his case for the Chinese written language and scholar-gentry class as mechanisms of civilizational continuity that survived dynastic change; and his reversal of the anachronism charge — arguing that NQH imposes postmodern ethnic identity theory onto pre-modern actors just as much as sinicization theory imposes nation-state categories. Ge's framework established that the most serious Chinese response to NQH is not nationalist dismissal but philosophical engagement with genuinely difficult questions about what historical continuity means.

Throughout the conversation several major analytical tensions recurred without resolution, constituting the field's genuine intellectual fault lines. The first is the tension between **court and frontier** as the primary site of historical analysis — Crossley and Rawski focus on what the imperial center claimed, Elliott and Millward focus on what institutions actually produced, Ge focuses on what subjects actually experienced. The second is the tension between **ideology and institution** — between what the Qing said it was doing and what its administrative structures were actually doing, a gap that Elliott's evidence makes impossible to ignore. The third is the tension between **scholarly and political** motivations on both sides — NQH scholars have been accused of providing historical ammunition for separatism, Chinese critics have been accused of defending territorial interests dressed as historiography, and neither accusation is entirely unfounded. The fourth, deepest tension is epistemological: a disagreement about **whose experience counts** as the primary subject of Qing history — the emperor's, the bannermen's, the frontier Muslim population's, or the 300 million Han Chinese subjects whose lives were structured by Chinese civilizational frameworks that had nothing to do with the Inner Asian dimensions of imperial sovereignty.

## The Dynastic Cycle: Classical Framework

The foundational periodization scheme in Chinese historiography is the dynastic cycle (*chaodai* 朝代), rooted in the concept of the Mandate of Heaven (*tianming* 天命). Each dynasty rises through virtue, rules legitimately, then loses legitimacy through corruption or incompetence — triggering collapse and replacement. The *Shiji* (Records of the Grand Historian) by Sima Qian established the template: history organized as sequential dynastic biographies, each dynasty receiving its own *benji* (basic annals). This wasn't merely chronological convenience — it encoded a moral cosmology where political legitimacy was the primary unit of historical time.

The standard dynastic sequence taught in Chinese schools today runs: Xia → Shang → Zhou → Qin → Han → (Three Kingdoms / Six Dynasties) → Sui → Tang → Song → Yuan → Ming → Qing → Republic → PRC. Each transition carries implicit moral judgment. Dynasties founded by Han Chinese carry different historiographical weight than conquest dynasties (Yuan, Qing), a tension that became explosive in modern nationalist scholarship.

---

## The Zhengshi Tradition and Official History

Chinese historians institutionalized periodization through the *zhengshi* (正史) — the Twenty-Four Official Histories commissioned by successive dynasties to record their predecessors. Each new dynasty wrote the history of the dynasty it replaced, a practice that simultaneously legitimized the new rulers and closed the historiographical account of the old. This created a system where periodization was inherently political: the *Yuan Shi*, *Ming Shi*, and *Qing Shi Gao* were state projects, not scholarly ones.

Within this tradition, periodization served succession logic. The debate over *zhengtong* (正統, orthodox succession) was among the most contentious in classical historiography. Did the Jin dynasty in the north hold legitimate succession after 1127, or only the Southern Song? Ouyang Xiu, Zhu Xi, and others fought these battles in ink — because answering the zhengtong question determined which rulers belonged in which annals, and therefore how time itself was carved.

---

## The Twentieth-Century Break: Marxist Periodization

Republican-era scholars like Liang Qichao first challenged dynastic periodization as incompatible with modern historical science. But the decisive rupture came after 1949, when Chinese historians were required to adopt Marxist five-stage social formation theory: Primitive Communism → Slave Society → Feudal Society → Capitalist Society → Socialism. This imposed a universal developmental schema onto Chinese history, forcing periodization debates around specific turning points: Was the Shang dynasty slave society or early feudal? Did feudalism begin with the Western Zhou, the Warring States, or the Qin unification?

These weren't academic disputes.郭沫若 (Guo Moruo) argued feudalism began with the Western Zhou; Fan Wenlan placed the transition at the Qin-Han period. The debate over the "sprouts of capitalism" (*zibenzhuyi mengya*) in late Ming/early Qing occupied decades of scholarship, driven by ideological need to find a Chinese-internal developmental logic rather than one imposed by Western contact. The PRC state had a stake in the answer — a China that could have developed capitalism independently validated the Marxist teleology without conceding that modernity required Western intervention.

---

## Post-Mao Revision and the Three-Epoch Model

After 1978, rigid Marxist periodization softened without being abandoned. The dominant contemporary framework used in Chinese textbooks and official scholarship divides history into three broad epochs: **Ancient** (*gudai* 古代, prehistory to 1840), **Modern** (*jindai* 近代, 1840–1949), and **Contemporary** (*xiandai* 现代, 1949–present). The choice of 1840 — the First Opium War — as the break point is explicitly ideological: it marks the beginning of China's "century of humiliation" and frames modern Chinese history as a story of national degradation followed by Communist restoration.

This scheme embeds a redemption narrative. *Jindai* is the wound; *xiandai* is the healing. The CCP's founding of the PRC in 1949 is positioned as the hinge of all modern Chinese history, giving institutional legitimacy to the current state through historiographical structure. Chinese historians working within this framework are not simply describing time — they are performing a political argument about why the present government is the culmination of historical necessity.

---

## Contemporary Debates and Alternative Frameworks

Since the 1990s, Chinese historians have engaged more openly with non-Marxist periodization. The "New Qing History" controversy — largely sparked by Western scholars but debated fiercely inside China — challenged the Han-centric assimilation narrative by arguing the Qing should be understood as a Manchu conquest empire with its own internal logic, not simply another dynasty absorbed into Chinese civilization. Chinese historians largely rejected this, viewing it as a challenge to territorial and ethnic continuity.

Within China, some scholars have proposed "long duration" (*changqi* 长期) frameworks influenced by Braudel, or civilizational periodization centered on cultural continuity rather than political succession. Others distinguish *wenming shi* (civilization history) from *guojia shi* (state history), allowing periodization that survives dynastic breaks. The tension remains unresolved: official historiography still anchors to the 1840/1949 schema, while academic historians increasingly borrow transnational and comparative frameworks that cut across it.

## What Is New Qing History?

New Qing History (NQH) emerged in American academia during the 1990s–2000s as a revisionist school arguing that the Qing dynasty (1644–1912) cannot be understood primarily as a Chinese dynasty that assimilated its Manchu rulers. Instead, NQH scholars insisted the Qing was a **Manchu empire** — a conquest regime that deliberately maintained distinct Manchu identity, language, and ruling ideology across its multiethnic territories, deploying different legitimating frameworks for different subject peoples simultaneously.

The movement was enabled by a methodological shift: NQH historians learned Manchu, Mongolian, and Tibetan, accessing archival sources that Han-centric historiography had ignored or couldn't read. What they found contradicted the assimilation narrative. Manchu-language court documents, banner records, and imperial correspondence revealed a court that consciously preserved ethnic distinctiveness rather than melting into Confucian Han civilization.

---

## Key Western Theorists

**Pamela Kyle Crossley** (*A Translucent Mirror*, 1999) argued that Qing emperors cultivated a theory of "simultaneous rulership" — presenting themselves as Confucian Son of Heaven to Han subjects, Bodhisattva-king to Tibetan and Mongol subjects, and Khan to Inner Asian peoples, all at once. Manchu identity was not dissolved by sinicization but was theorized as a universal sovereign category encompassing all others.

**Mark Elliott** (*The Manchu Way*, 2001) focused on the Banner system as an institution designed to maintain Manchu ethnic and military distinctiveness across generations. He argued "Manchu-ness" was a constructed but deliberately sustained identity, not an ethnic essence eroding under Han cultural pressure.

**Evelyn Rawski**, in her provocative 1996 AHA presidential address *"Reenvisioning the Qing,"* directly challenged the dominant framework set by Ping-ti Ho's 1967 argument that Qing success derived from sinicization. Rawski argued the opposite: Qing longevity came from the rulers' **refusal** to fully sinicize, maintaining Inner Asian legitimacy structures that held the empire together.

**James Millward** (*Beyond the Pass*, 1998) extended the argument to Xinjiang, showing Qing administration there operated through Central Asian and Islamic frameworks largely independent of Han administrative models, undermining the idea of a uniform "Chinese" imperial project.

---

## The Sinicization Debate: The Original Fault Line

The controversy has a pre-history. **Ping-ti Ho** (何炳棣), the eminent Chinese-American historian, argued in 1967 that Qing success was fundamentally a story of sinicization — Manchu rulers adopted Han institutions, Confucian ideology, and Chinese administrative structures so thoroughly that they became, in practice, a Chinese dynasty. This was the mainstream view in both Western and Chinese scholarship for decades.

Rawski's 1996 rebuttal shattered that consensus in Western academia. The debate between Ho and Rawski — two giants, one Chinese-American defending the assimilation narrative, one American attacking it — set the personal and intellectual stakes before the Chinese scholarly establishment had fully engaged. When Chinese historians entered the debate aggressively in the 2000s, they were in part defending Ho's position, which aligned with their own national historiographical commitments.

---

## Chinese Scholarly Pushback

Chinese historians responded to NQH with sustained institutional and intellectual opposition, most prominently articulated by **Feng Erkang** (冯尔康), **Liu Fengyun** (刘凤云), and especially **Zhong Han** (钟焓), whose detailed critiques accused NQH of misreading sources, overinterpreting Manchu-language documents, and projecting modern ethnic nationalist categories backward onto pre-modern imperial formations.

**Ge Zhaoguang** (葛兆光), one of China's most prominent intellectual historians, offered a more sophisticated critique in *Here in "China" I Dwell* (*宅兹中国*, 2011). He argued that NQH, while methodologically interesting, dangerously dismantles the concept of a continuous Chinese civilizational identity — not because that identity is primordial, but because it has genuine historical coherence that NQH's framework erases in favor of a fragmented, multiethnic imperial mosaic.

The Chinese state's concern was less historiographical than territorial. If the Qing was a Manchu empire rather than a Chinese dynasty, then Tibet, Xinjiang, Mongolia, and Manchuria were not historically "Chinese" — they were conquests of a separate Manchu imperial project. The NQH framework, whether intentionally or not, provided historiographical ammunition for separatist arguments. This made it politically explosive in Beijing, not merely academically contentious.

---

## The Ethnic Identity Problem

At the core of NQH is a question Chinese historiography has always managed carefully: **what is the relationship between "Chineseness" and the Qing state?** Traditional Chinese historiography used the concept of *Hua-Yi* (華夷) distinction — civilized/barbarian — but resolved it by arguing that barbarian conquerors became Chinese through cultural transformation. NQH destabilized this by insisting cultural transformation was incomplete, partial, and strategically performed rather than genuine.

NQH critics argued that Western scholars were importing **modern ethnic nationalist categories** — the idea that ethnic identity must be exclusive and boundary-maintaining — into a pre-modern imperial context where identity was more fluid and layered. Crossley and Elliott countered that it was the sinicization thesis that imposed a modern category (the assimilating nation-state) backward onto Qing history.

Both sides had a point. The Qing court did strategically perform different identities for different audiences. It also genuinely absorbed enormous amounts of Han administrative and cultural practice. The controversy's intractability stems partly from both frameworks containing truth, and partly from neither side being purely disinterested.

---

## Methodological Stakes

Beyond the political controversy, NQH raised fundamental questions about **whose sources** define Chinese history. Classical Chinese-language sources — the *veritable records* (*shilu* 實錄), official histories, and literary works — had long formed the evidentiary base. NQH's use of Manchu archives revealed a parallel documentary universe that told a different story, or at minimum complicated the Han-language record.

Chinese critics responded partly by improving their own engagement with Manchu sources — a methodological advance the controversy directly produced. But they also raised valid questions about whether NQH scholars, lacking deep immersion in Chinese literary and institutional culture, over-weighted the novelty of Manchu sources and under-weighted continuities visible only in the Chinese-language record. **Bettine Birge** and others noted that NQH sometimes reads like a corrective overcorrection — replacing one monocausal story (sinicization) with another (deliberate ethnic preservation).

---

## Where the Debate Stands Now

The controversy has not resolved — it has partially institutionalized. In Western academia, NQH's core insights (Manchu distinctiveness, multiethnic legitimation strategies, the importance of non-Chinese sources) are largely absorbed into mainstream Qing studies. Few serious historians now argue for simple sinicization. In Chinese academia, the assimilation framework remains dominant in textbooks and official historiography, while a minority of scholars engage more critically with NQH methods.

The political dimension ensures the debate cannot be purely academic. Chinese state historiography requires a continuous, territorially coherent "China" extending back through the Qing to justify present borders and sovereignty claims. NQH's imperial mosaic model, even if historically compelling, is institutionally incompatible with that requirement. The result is a genuine historiographical impasse where the two sides are not only arguing about evidence but about what history is **for**.

## Biography: Pamela Kyle Crossley

Pamela Kyle Crossley is Professor of History at Dartmouth College, where she has taught since the 1980s. She received her PhD from Yale University, trained in both Chinese and Manchu languages — the latter being rare enough among Western historians to define her scholarly niche. Her early work focused on Manchu identity and the Banner system before expanding into broader questions of empire, nationalism, and historical theory.

Her major works form a coherent research program: *Orphan Warriors* (1990) examined Manchu bannermen in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as a case study in ethnic identity under pressure; *A Translucent Mirror* (1999) built the theoretical architecture for understanding Qing imperial selfhood; *The Manchus* (1997) provided a synthetic history for general readers; and *What Is Global History?* (2008) signaled her move toward historiographical theory at a global scale. She is considered one of the three or four scholars most responsible for founding New Qing History as a recognizable school, alongside Mark Elliott and Evelyn Rawski.

Crossley is known for dense, theoretically ambitious prose that draws on philosophy of language, anthropology, and postcolonial theory alongside conventional historical evidence. *A Translucent Mirror* is her most challenging and most cited work — influential precisely because it operates at the level of ideology and self-conception rather than event or institution.

---

## The Central Argument

Before the chapter outline, the book's governing thesis requires clear statement. Crossley argues that Qing emperors — particularly the Qianlong Emperor (r. 1735–1796) — developed a theory of imperial identity she calls **"simultaneity."** Rather than the emperor being one thing (Confucian Son of Heaven) who strategically performed other roles, the emperor was **simultaneously and co-equally** all sovereign identities at once: Manchu khan, Confucian monarch, Tibetan Buddhist patron, Mongol overlord.

This was not cynical performance or cultural borrowing. It was a **cosmological claim** — that the Qing emperor embodied a universal sovereign principle that manifested differently across different cultural registers without being reducible to any one of them. The "translucent mirror" of the title refers to an imperial self that reflects all subject peoples back to themselves while remaining irreducibly itself — neither transparent (absorbed into subject cultures) nor opaque (purely alien).

This argument directly challenged sinicization theory: the Qing emperor was not becoming Chinese. He was becoming something categorically new, for which "Chinese" was only one facet.

## Chapter One: The Problem of the Emperor's New Clothes

The opening chapter establishes the historiographical problem Crossley is intervening in. She surveys how both Western and Chinese historians have conceptualized Qing rulership — predominantly through the lens of sinicization, cultural borrowing, or strategic mimicry. She argues these frameworks all share a common flaw: they treat Han Chinese civilization as the **normative center** against which Qing emperors are measured as more or less successful imitators.

Crossley introduces the philosophical stakes. Drawing on theories of representation and selfhood, she asks: what does it mean for a ruler to *be* something versus to *perform* something? The sinicization thesis implicitly treats Manchu identity as the "real" substrate that gets covered over by Chinese cultural performance. Crossley challenges the premise — arguing that for the Qing emperors, identity was not substrate-plus-performance but a theorized **multiplicity** held together by sovereign will and cosmological claim.

She also introduces the primary source base that distinguishes her approach: Manchu-language imperial texts, edicts, and self-representations that have been largely ignored by historians working only in Chinese. These sources, she argues, reveal a self-understanding systematically different from what the Chinese-language record projects.

---

## Chapter Two: Origins and the Construction of Manchu Identity

This chapter addresses the pre-conquest period and the early construction of what "Manchu" meant as a category. Crossley examines how Nurhaci (1559–1626) and Hong Taiji (1592–1643) actively **created** Manchu identity as a political instrument — unifying diverse Jurchen-speaking peoples under a new ethnonym and new institutional structures (the Banner system) that produced a bounded, militarized community where none had existed in that form before.

This is a crucial move for Crossley's larger argument. If Manchu identity was constructed rather than primordial, then its deliberate preservation under the Qing was not ethnic conservatism but ongoing **political work** — the emperors were not simply inheriting an identity, they were continuously manufacturing and theorizing it. Hong Taiji's renaming of his people as "Manchu" in 1635 and his dynasty as "Qing" are read not as administrative housekeeping but as acts of identity engineering with long-term ideological consequences.

Crossley also examines Hong Taiji's early experiments with multiethnic rulership — maintaining separate administrative structures for Manchu, Mongol, and Han bannermen — as the institutional precursor to the simultaneity doctrine that would be fully theorized under Qianlong.

---

## Chapter Three: The Conquest and the Problem of Legitimacy

The 1644 conquest of China proper created an acute legitimacy problem that this chapter analyzes in depth. The Qing rulers needed to be accepted as legitimate by an enormous Han Chinese population with sophisticated political theories of dynastic legitimacy — the Mandate of Heaven, Confucian moral kingship, the *zhengtong* (orthodox succession) framework. Simply ruling by military force was insufficient for stable long-term governance.

Crossley traces how the early Qing — particularly the Shunzhi (r. 1644–1661) and Kangxi (r. 1661–1722) emperors — navigated this by **selectively adopting** Han legitimating frameworks without abandoning Manchu ones. The Kangxi Emperor's famous southern tours, his patronage of Chinese classical scholarship, his Confucian self-presentation — Crossley reads these not as evidence of sinicization but as the first systematic experiments in simultaneous sovereign self-presentation.

She analyzes the tension this created: performing Confucian kingship for Han subjects while maintaining the Banner system, Manchu-language administration, and Inner Asian alliance structures that served non-Han constituencies. The early Qing managed this tension pragmatically; it would fall to Qianlong to theorize it into a coherent ideology.

---

## Chapter Four: The Qianlong Emperor and the Theory of Simultaneity

The intellectual core of the book. Crossley provides close readings of Qianlong's own writings — edicts, poems, philosophical essays, and notably his *Discourse on Lamas* and various pronouncements on the nature of imperial identity — to reconstruct what she calls his **theory of universal sovereignty**.

Qianlong articulated the claim that the emperor was not a Han ruler, not a Manchu ruler, not a Buddhist patron-king — but a sovereign principle that encompassed and transcended all these simultaneously. His multilingual inscriptions at imperial sites (Chinese, Manchu, Mongol, Tibetan, often Uyghur) were not translations of a single message but **parallel instantiations** of a sovereign identity that existed in all these registers at once. Removing any one language would not produce a remainder — each was complete.

Crossley reads Qianlong's famous condemnation of Han Chinese who called themselves "Chinese" (*Zhongyuan ren*) as if this were synonymous with "subject of the Qing" — he insisted this was a **category error**. The Qing was not China; it contained China as one domain among several. This is Crossley's most politically charged textual evidence, and Chinese critics have disputed her readings extensively.

She also analyzes the Chengde Mountain Resort complex as physical embodiment of simultaneous sovereignty — its architecture deliberately incorporating Han, Tibetan, and Mongol forms not eclectically but as a theorized spatial statement of imperial multiplicity.

---

## Chapter Five: Representing the Ancestors — The *Veritable Records* and Imperial Genealogy

This chapter examines how the Qing managed **historical self-representation** through the official *Veritable Records* (*Shilu*) and genealogical texts. Crossley argues that the Qing court produced systematic, layered historical narratives about Manchu origins that were designed to sustain the simultaneity claim across time.

The construction of Manchu genealogies connecting the ruling house to earlier Jurchen dynasties (particularly the Jin dynasty, 1115–1234), while simultaneously claiming descent from figures with significance in Mongol and Buddhist cosmological frameworks, was deliberate ideological work. The Qing emperors needed ancestors that validated each of their simultaneous sovereign identities — and they produced them through historiographical manufacture as much as genealogical fact.

Crossley's method here is particularly sophisticated: she compares Manchu and Chinese versions of the same imperial genealogical texts, showing systematic divergences that reveal different identity claims being made for different audiences in the same official documents. The Chinese version emphasizes Confucian virtues and dynastic continuity; the Manchu version emphasizes martial origins and Inner Asian connections. Neither is the "real" text — both are simultaneous sovereign self-representations in Crossley's framework.

---

## Chapter Six: The Nineteenth Century — Dissolution of Simultaneity

The final analytical chapter traces how the simultaneity framework collapsed under nineteenth-century pressures. The Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864), Western imperial encroachment, and the slow erosion of Banner military capacity all destabilized the institutional basis of Qing multiethnic empire. But Crossley's focus is ideological: the rise of Han Chinese nationalism in the late nineteenth century forced a binary that simultaneity could not survive.

Han nationalist intellectuals — and eventually revolutionaries like those in the Tongmenghui — posed the question of Qing legitimacy in ethnic terms: was this a Chinese dynasty or a foreign occupation? The simultaneity answer ("neither and both, in a way that transcends the question") was philosophically sophisticated but politically useless against nationalist mobilization. The Qing court's attempts to reframe itself as a constitutional multiethnic state in 1905–1911 were too late and too thin.

Crossley also examines how the Manchu bannermen themselves — the institutional carriers of Manchu identity — experienced identity crisis as the nineteenth century progressed: economically marginalized, militarily obsolete, culturally pressured toward Han assimilation on the ground even as the court theorized Manchu distinctiveness from above.

---

## Conclusion: The Mirror and Its Implications

Crossley's conclusion draws out the theoretical implications for how historians should understand pre-modern empire, identity, and sovereignty. She argues that the Qing case reveals the **inadequacy of nation-state categories** — ethnicity, assimilation, cultural borrowing — for understanding imperial formations that operated with fundamentally different ontologies of political selfhood.

She ends with a pointed remark on historiographical politics: both the sinicization narrative (which makes the Qing a Chinese dynasty) and simple conquest narratives (which make it a foreign occupation) are retrospective impositions of nation-state logic. The Qing emperors understood themselves neither way. Recovering their actual self-understanding requires reading their own sources in their own languages — which is simultaneously a methodological argument and a rebuke to historians who have not done so.

## Critical Reception

*A Translucent Mirror* was praised in Western academia for theoretical ambition and archival depth, criticized for prose density and for what some saw as over-reading thin textual evidence into grand ideological claims. **Mark Elliott** and **Evelyn Rawski** broadly endorsed its framework. **Ping-ti Ho** rejected its premises. Chinese historians, particularly **Zhong Han**, produced detailed source-by-source rebuttals arguing Crossley misread or decontextualized Manchu documents and that her "simultaneity" theory imposes postmodern identity theory onto a court that was doing something far more pragmatic.

The deepest criticism — from both sinologists and theorists — is that Crossley's framework sometimes makes the Qing emperors sound like postmodern theorists of identity avant la lettre, projecting twentieth-century intellectual categories onto eighteenth-century actors. Her defenders respond that the alternative — assuming pre-modern rulers couldn't theorize their own identity — is its own form of anachronism.

## Mark Elliott: Brief Biography

Mark Elliott is Professor of Chinese and Inner Asian History at Harvard University, where he also serves as Vice Provost for International Affairs. He completed his PhD at UC Berkeley under the supervision of Frederic Wakeman Jr., one of the twentieth century's foremost historians of late imperial China. Elliott reads and works in Manchu, Chinese, and several other languages — the multilingual competence that defines the New Qing History school.

His scholarly trajectory has moved from the granular institutional history of *The Manchu Way* (2001) toward broader questions of ethnicity, empire, and borders in Chinese history. Other significant works include *Emperor Qianlong: Son of Heaven, Man of the World* (2009), a biographical study that complements and partially revises the more ideological Qianlong of Crossley's *Translucent Mirror*, and numerous articles on Qing ethnic policy and Inner Asian frontier administration. He has also been a prominent voice in Western academic responses to Chinese critiques of New Qing History, engaging Chinese scholars more directly and diplomatically than some of his colleagues.

Elliott is generally considered more empirically grounded and institutionally focused than Crossley — less given to grand theoretical claims, more attentive to how identity actually functioned on the ground through specific administrative mechanisms.

---

## The Central Argument of *The Manchu Way*

Elliott's thesis is more contained than Crossley's but equally revisionist. He argues that the Banner system — the military-administrative institution organizing all Manchu, Mongol, and Han bannermen — functioned as the primary **ethnic boundary mechanism** preserving Manchu distinctiveness across the nearly three centuries of Qing rule. Manchu identity did not survive despite contact with Han Chinese civilization; it survived **because** the Banner system created physical, legal, social, and economic separation that made assimilation structurally difficult even when culturally tempting.

The key concept Elliott introduces is **"ethnic sovereignty"** — the idea that the Qing dynasty's legitimacy rested not merely on military conquest or Confucian moral kingship but on the preservation of a distinct Manchu ruling caste whose ethnic particularity was itself a source of imperial authority. When the Banner system decayed in the nineteenth century, it took Manchu identity — and ultimately Qing legitimacy — down with it.

Where Crossley's argument is primarily ideological and textual (what did emperors claim about themselves?), Elliott's is primarily institutional and sociological (what structures actually produced and maintained Manchu distinctiveness?). This difference in emphasis, more than any direct disagreement, defines the productive tension between them.

---

## Chapter One: Introduction — Ethnicity and the Question of Manchu Identity

Elliott opens by confronting the central problem directly: what does it mean to call the Qing a "Manchu dynasty" when its emperors spoke Chinese, governed through Confucian institutions, and presided over a population that was 97% Han? His answer requires disaggregating "Manchu" from "Chinese" in ways that neither traditional Chinese historiography nor simple conquest narratives permit.

He introduces ethnicity not as primordial essence but as a **socially constructed and institutionally maintained** boundary — drawing on Fredrik Barth's anthropological framework of ethnic boundary maintenance. Manchu identity persisted not because Manchus were inherently different from Han Chinese in some deep cultural sense, but because specific institutions continuously reproduced the boundary between them. This sets up the Banner system as the book's central object of analysis.

Elliott also confronts the "sinicization" debate directly, positioning himself against Ping-ti Ho and in qualified agreement with Rawski and Crossley, while noting that the sinicization thesis contains a partial truth: enormous amounts of Han cultural practice did penetrate Manchu life. His argument is not that sinicization didn't happen, but that it never fully dissolved the institutional and identity boundaries that the Banner system maintained.

---

## Chapter Two: The Origins of the Banner System Under Nurhaci and Hong Taiji

This chapter provides the institutional history of the Banners from their origins as Nurhaci's military units in the early seventeenth century through Hong Taiji's systematic elaboration of the system before the 1644 conquest. Elliott traces how what began as practical military organization — dividing forces into units distinguished by colored flag banners — became a comprehensive **social classification system** that assigned every Manchu, Mongol, and eventually Han soldier and his family to a permanent, hereditary administrative unit.

The Eight Banner structure (eight Manchu, eight Mongol, eight Han Chinese banners) created a tripartite ethnic hierarchy with Manchus at the apex, Mongols as junior partners, and Han bannermen as auxiliaries. Elliott shows how this structure was not merely military convenience but a deliberate **ethnicization of administration** — building ethnic categories into the fundamental architecture of the state.

Hong Taiji's role receives particular attention: his creation of the Manchu ethnonym in 1635, his establishment of separate deliberative councils for each ethnic constituency, and his insistence on maintaining Manchu language and martial customs as formal institutional requirements. Elliott reads these as the foundations of the ethnic sovereignty claim that would sustain Qing legitimacy for two centuries.

---

## Chapter Three: Bannermen in Beijing — Spatial Segregation and Urban Identity

One of the book's most original contributions. After 1644, the Qing resettled the entire bannerman population of Beijing into the Inner City, expelling Han Chinese residents to the Outer City. Elliott reconstructs this as a massive act of **spatial ethnic engineering** — creating a physical geography of Manchu separateness in the imperial capital that reinforced every other boundary mechanism.

Banner garrisons were established in major cities across China, each following the same segregation principle: bannermen lived in walled garrison compounds separate from the surrounding Han population, governed by Banner rather than civilian law, supported by imperial stipends rather than commercial activity. Elliott uses Beijing household registers and garrison records — largely in Manchu — to reconstruct the daily texture of bannerman life in extraordinary detail.

The spatial argument matters theoretically: Elliott shows that Manchu identity was not just a legal category or cultural preference but was **embedded in the built environment**. Bannermen didn't just think of themselves as Manchu — they literally lived in Manchu space, shopped in Manchu markets, married within Manchu social networks, and were governed by Manchu administrative structures. Physical separation did much of the work that ideology alone could never accomplish.

---

## Chapter Four: Law, Marriage, and the Reproduction of Ethnic Boundaries

This chapter examines the legal and social mechanisms that prevented the dissolution of Manchu identity through intermarriage, cultural absorption, or economic integration with the Han majority. Elliott analyzes Qing law's systematic distinctions between bannermen and civilians (*minren*) — separate legal codes, separate courts, separate punishments for the same offenses, and crucially, restrictions on intermarriage and on bannermen engaging in trade or agriculture.

The marriage regulations receive sustained analysis. Official policy discouraged and in some contexts prohibited Han-Manchu intermarriage, not from racial ideology in a modern sense but from the practical recognition that intermarriage was the most reliable vector of cultural assimilation. Elliott traces how these regulations were applied, evaded, and renegotiated over time — showing both the state's commitment to boundary maintenance and the constant social pressure eroding those boundaries.

He also examines the **stipend system** — bannermen received hereditary imperial salaries in exchange for military availability, explicitly prohibiting them from engaging in the commercial and agricultural economy that integrated Han Chinese society. This created a Manchu population economically dependent on the state and structurally separated from Han economic networks. When stipends became insufficient and the prohibition on civilian occupations broke down in the eighteenth century, Elliott identifies this as the beginning of the Banner system's — and Manchu identity's — institutional crisis.

---

## Chapter Five: Language, Education, and the Manchu Cultural Program

Elliott examines the Qing state's sustained campaign to maintain Manchu language as a living administrative and cultural medium — and its ultimate failure. Imperial edicts repeatedly mandated Manchu-language competency for bannermen, established schools teaching Manchu language and script, and required Manchu-language examinations alongside the Chinese civil service examinations for Banner candidates.

The documentary evidence Elliott marshals here is striking: by the mid-eighteenth century, imperial edicts are already complaining that bannermen cannot speak Manchu, that Manchu-language examinations are being failed at alarming rates, that young bannermen growing up in Beijing garrison compounds are functionally monolingual in Chinese. Qianlong's famous edicts on Manchu language — simultaneously mandating its preservation and lamenting its decay — appear here as evidence of **institutional failure** rather than successful cultural policy.

This is one of the points where Elliott's account creates tension with Crossley's. If Qianlong was simultaneously theorizing a grand ideology of Manchu imperial distinctiveness, his own bannermen were already losing the linguistic capacity that was supposed to be one of that distinctiveness's primary markers. Elliott's institutional evidence suggests the simultaneity ideology was being constructed precisely as its material basis was eroding — raising the question of whether Crossley's ideology was describing reality or compensating for its absence.

---

## Chapter Six: Martial Values and the Decline of Banner Military Capacity

The Manchu self-conception as a warrior people — skilled in riding and archery, hardened by the disciplines of the hunt, defined by martial virtue — was central to Qing imperial ideology and to Manchu ethnic identity. This chapter traces the comprehensive failure of the Banner military system to maintain that identity in practice across the eighteenth century.

Banner forces performed catastrophically against the Taiping Rebellion and other nineteenth-century crises, having been militarily obsolete for decades. Elliott traces this not simply to moral decay (the standard Qing official explanation) but to **structural contradictions** in the Banner system itself: hereditary military status divorced from actual military performance, stipends paid regardless of martial competency, garrison life in cities that produced administrative functionaries rather than warriors.

The imperial response — ever more urgent edicts demanding a return to Manchu martial values, competitions in riding and archery, prohibitions on bannermen learning Chinese literary arts — Elliott reads as evidence of the same compensatory dynamic visible in the language edicts. The state was legislating an identity that the institutional structure was simultaneously destroying. This creates one of the book's most powerful arguments: **ethnic sovereignty requires institutional maintenance**, and when institutions decay, ideology alone cannot sustain identity.

---

## Chapter Seven: The Nineteenth-Century Crisis and the Unraveling of Ethnic Sovereignty

The Banner system's institutional collapse in the nineteenth century is the subject of this chapter. Military defeat, fiscal crisis, and the Taiping devastation forced the Qing to rely on Han Chinese regional armies — the Xiang Army of Zeng Guofan, the Huai Army of Li Hongzhang — that operated entirely outside the Banner framework. Manchu ethnic sovereignty, premised on Manchu military indispensability, became increasingly fictional.

Elliott traces the social consequences for bannermen themselves: stipends cut, garrison walls crumbling, prohibitions on civilian occupations abandoned out of fiscal necessity, intermarriage restrictions loosening. The institutional architecture that had maintained Manchu distinctiveness for two centuries was being dismantled piece by piece, not by cultural assimilation but by fiscal and military emergency.

He also examines the late Qing reform period and the abortive attempts to convert Banner forces into modern armies, retrain bannermen as civilians, and ultimately abolish the Banner system's legal distinctiveness altogether in 1912. The Republican revolution completed what the nineteenth century had begun: the formal elimination of the institutional basis of Manchu ethnic sovereignty.

---

## Chapter Eight: Conclusion — What Manchu Identity Meant and What Its Loss Signifies

Elliott's conclusion draws together the institutional and theoretical threads. He argues that the Qing case demonstrates both the **constructedness** and the **reality** of ethnic identity — Manchu identity was built through institutions, but once built, it was genuinely experienced, defended, and mourned. The bannermen who found themselves identity-less after 1912 were not discovering that their ethnicity had been fake; they were experiencing the collapse of the structures that had made it real.

He ends by returning to the historiographical stakes: if Manchu identity was institutionally produced and maintained, then the sinicization thesis is wrong not because the Manchus didn't absorb Han culture (they did) but because cultural absorption and ethnic boundary maintenance are **not mutually exclusive**. Bannermen could speak Chinese, practice Buddhism, write poetry in classical Chinese forms, and still remain institutionally, legally, and socially Manchu. This is Elliott's most direct rebuttal to Ping-ti Ho.

---

## Where Elliott and Crossley Diverge

**Level of analysis.** Crossley operates at the level of imperial ideology and sovereign self-conception — her evidence is primarily texts produced by or for emperors. Elliott operates at the level of social institution and everyday practice — his evidence is household registers, garrison records, legal codes, stipend accounts. These are genuinely different objects of study, and their findings are not always compatible.

**The nature of Manchu identity.** For Crossley, Manchu identity under the Qing was theorized into something categorically new — the emperor's simultaneity transcended and transformed what "Manchu" originally meant. For Elliott, Manchu identity remained more continuous and sociologically grounded — it was the identity of a military caste maintained by specific institutions, not a philosophical construct invented by imperial theorists.

**The role of the Qianlong Emperor.** Both scholars focus heavily on Qianlong, but to different ends. Crossley's Qianlong is an ideological architect constructing a sophisticated theory of universal sovereignty. Elliott's Qianlong is simultaneously a theorist **and** a desperate administrator issuing futile edicts trying to stop his bannermen from forgetting their language and martial skills. The gap between these two Qianlongs is significant — one is building an empire of meaning, the other is watching an institution decay.

**Agency and structure.** Crossley foregrounds imperial agency — emperors theorize, construct, and claim. Elliott foregrounds structural constraint — institutions produce, maintain, and fail. This is partly a difference in historical methodology (intellectual history vs. social history) and partly a genuine disagreement about what drives historical change.

**Political implications.** Crossley's simultaneity framework most directly challenges the sinicization thesis and raises questions about what "China" even means as a historical category. Elliott's institutional framework is somewhat less destabilizing to Chinese territorial claims — it explains Manchu distinctiveness without necessarily implying that Qing territories were non-Chinese in any meaningful sense. Chinese critics have been harder on Crossley than on Elliott for this reason.

## Brief Biography: Evelyn Rawski

Evelyn Sakakida Rawski is University Professor Emerita of History at the University of Pittsburgh, one of the most decorated positions in American academia — the "University Professor" title reserved for scholars of exceptional cross-disciplinary distinction. She received her PhD from Harvard University, trained in Chinese history under John King Fairbank, the towering figure who dominated American sinology for decades. That lineage matters: Rawski was trained inside the very tradition she would later challenge.

Her early work focused on economic and social history of late imperial China — *Agricultural Change and the Peasant Economy of South China* (1972) and *Education and Popular Literacy in Ch'ing China* (1979) established her as a rigorous empirical historian of Chinese society. Neither book suggested the revisionist turn that would define her later career. The pivot came through her engagement with Manchu sources and Inner Asian history, which gradually reoriented her away from the Han-centric frameworks of her Fairbank training.

Her major synthetic work *The Last Emperors: A Social History of Qing Imperial Institutions* (1998), published two years after the AHA address, elaborated the arguments of *Reenvisioning* into a full scholarly monograph — examining Qing court ritual, marriage alliances, religious patronage, and administrative structures through explicitly multiethnic lenses. She has also co-edited major volumes on Chinese history and material culture, and her work on literacy and popular culture remains influential independently of the New Qing History controversy.

Rawski served as President of the Association for Asian Studies in addition to the AHA presidency — a rare double distinction reflecting her standing across the field. Her 1996 address was not a youthful provocation but a considered intervention by a scholar at the peak of institutional authority, which amplified both its impact and the ferocity of the response.

---

## Context: Why a Presidential Address Matters

The American Historical Association presidential address is not a typical academic paper. Delivered to the entire assembled membership at the annual conference, it is understood as a **field-defining statement** — an opportunity for a senior scholar to reorient collective attention, challenge settled assumptions, and set an agenda. Rawski knew exactly what she was doing. She chose as her explicit target the most eminent living Chinese-American historian, Ping-ti Ho, and the most foundational claim in Qing studies. The address was designed to be controversial. It succeeded.

The 1996 address was subsequently published in the *Journal of Asian Studies* (Vol. 55, No. 4, 1996) under the title *"Reenvisioning the Qing: The Significance of the Qing Period in Chinese History."* Ping-ti Ho published a direct rebuttal in the same journal. The exchange became one of the most widely cited polemics in late imperial Chinese historiography — required reading in graduate seminars across the field.

---

## The Target: Ping-ti Ho's Sinicization Thesis

To understand Rawski's address, Ho's 1967 article *"The Significance of the Ch'ing Period in Chinese History"* (the title Rawski deliberately echoed) must be reconstructed carefully. Ho argued that the Qing dynasty's extraordinary success — its longevity, territorial expansion, and administrative effectiveness — was fundamentally a product of **sinicization**: the Manchu rulers' wholesale adoption of Han Chinese institutions, ideology, and cultural practices.

Ho's argument had several interlocking components. First, the Manchus were relatively small in number and administratively inexperienced before the conquest — their success required absorbing Chinese expertise. Second, the Confucian examination system, bureaucratic structures, and ideological frameworks the Qing inherited and maintained were the actual machinery of governance; Manchu ethnic distinctiveness was peripheral to how the empire actually ran. Third, and most provocatively, Ho argued that **the Manchus were essentially absorbed into Chinese civilization** — that by the high Qing, to speak of a distinct Manchu ruling identity was to describe an increasingly thin fiction overlying a fundamentally Chinese imperial system.

Ho wrote from a position of considerable authority — he was among the most technically accomplished historians of his generation, with deep archival competence in classical Chinese sources. His argument also carried an implicit nationalist dignity: it credited Chinese civilization with the adaptive power to absorb and transform even its conquerors, a formulation that resonated with both Chinese scholarly pride and Cold War-era American area studies assumptions about the gravitational power of "high civilization."

---

## Rawski's Opening Move: Reframing the Question

Rawski begins the address by identifying the foundational assumption embedded in Ho's framing that she intends to displace. Ho's question — why did the Qing succeed? — presupposes that the answer must be found in the Qing's relationship to Chinese civilization. The answer is either sinicization (Ho) or resistance to sinicization (various critics), but in both cases **Chinese civilization is the reference point**.

Rawski argues this is the wrong question generating the wrong axis of analysis. The Qing was not primarily a Chinese dynasty that happened to have Manchu rulers. It was a **Central Asian conquest empire** that happened to include China as its largest and most populous domain. Reframing the Qing as a species of Inner Asian empire — alongside the Mongol Yuan, the Timurid states, the Mughal Empire — opens entirely different analytical frameworks and comparative possibilities.

This reframing is not merely rhetorical. It changes what counts as evidence, which sources are central, and what needs to be explained. If the Qing is a Chinese dynasty, Manchu language documents are marginal curiosities. If it is an Inner Asian empire, those documents are primary evidence of how the empire actually understood and governed itself.

---

## The Empirical Core: What Manchu Sources Reveal

The methodological heart of Rawski's argument is the claim that historians working exclusively in Chinese sources have systematically misread the Qing. She catalogs the archival resources that had been underutilized or entirely ignored: Manchu-language palace memorials, Inner Asian diplomatic correspondence, Mongol and Tibetan administrative records, banner household registers.

She argues these sources reveal several things invisible in the Chinese record. The Qing court maintained **parallel administrative structures** for different subject populations — a Chinese-facing bureaucracy organized around Confucian norms, and an Inner Asian-facing structure organized around steppe diplomatic conventions, Buddhist patronage networks, and banner military organization. These were not translations of each other. They operated on different principles for different constituencies.

The Lifanyuan (理藩院), the Court of Colonial Affairs managing relations with Mongols, Tibetans, and other Inner Asian peoples, is a central piece of evidence. It operated primarily in Manchu and Mongolian, followed Inner Asian rather than Chinese administrative conventions, and had no real analog in previous Chinese dynasties. Its existence is difficult to explain within a sinicization framework — it is not a Chinese institution that absorbed non-Chinese peoples but a non-Chinese institution that managed non-Chinese constituencies on their own terms.

---

## The Alliance System: Marriage Politics as Imperial Strategy

One of Rawski's most substantive empirical arguments concerns Qing marriage alliances with Mongol nobility. The Qing systematically married imperial daughters into Mongol ruling families and took Mongol women as imperial consorts — maintaining a web of affinal ties across Inner Asia that bound Mongol elites to the dynasty through kinship obligation.

This practice had deep roots in Inner Asian steppe diplomacy, where marriage alliance (*quda* relationships) was a primary tool of political incorporation. It was emphatically **not** a Chinese practice — Chinese dynastic tradition regarded marriage alliance with "barbarians" as humiliation, a desperate measure of weak dynasties. The Qing not only practiced it systematically but institutionalized it, establishing formal procedures for selecting Mongol brides and managing the ongoing kinship network.

Rawski argues this is decisive evidence against the sinicization thesis. A dynasty that had genuinely adopted Chinese political culture as its operative framework would have abandoned or minimized steppe marriage diplomacy — it contradicted Chinese norms too directly. The Qing's enthusiastic maintenance of the practice reveals a court that understood itself as operating within Inner Asian as well as Chinese political traditions, and that valued the Inner Asian framework enough to maintain it against Chinese cultural pressure.

---

## Tibetan Buddhism as Sovereign Technology

Rawski gives substantial attention to the Qing emperors' relationship with Tibetan Buddhism — a relationship she argues has been systematically underestimated by historians focused on Confucian legitimation. The emperors were not merely patrons of Tibetan Buddhism in a casual or decorative sense. They cultivated deep relationships with the Dalai Lama institution, built elaborate Tibetan Buddhist temple complexes (including the famous reproductions of Tibetan sacred architecture at Chengde), sponsored Tibetan canonical translation projects, and in the case of the Qianlong Emperor, were recognized by Tibetan religious authorities as emanations of Manjushri, the bodhisattva of wisdom.

This Tibetan Buddhist identity performed specific political functions. It bound Tibetan and Mongol elites — for whom Tibetan Buddhism was the primary framework of cosmic and political legitimacy — to the Qing emperor as a figure of genuine religious authority, not merely military dominance. Rawski argues this was a **sovereign technology** as important as the Confucian examination system: it incorporated Inner Asian populations into the imperial order through religious obligation rather than administrative coercion.

The scale of Qing investment in Tibetan Buddhist infrastructure — temples, translations, patronage networks, the management of reincarnation searches for major lamas — was enormous and is difficult to explain as mere performance for external audiences. Rawski argues it reflects genuine imperial commitment to Tibetan Buddhist frameworks as constituent elements of Qing sovereign identity, not decorative additions to a fundamentally Confucian edifice.

---

## The Manchu Language Question

Rawski addresses the Manchu language with particular care, because the evidence here cuts in multiple directions. She acknowledges what Elliott's later work would document in detail: Manchu language competence among bannermen declined dramatically across the eighteenth century. By the nineteenth century most bannermen were functionally monolingual in Chinese.

But she distinguishes between language use among the general bannerman population and language use at the **court and administrative apex**. Manchu remained a functional language of imperial communication, court ritual, and diplomatic correspondence with Inner Asian peoples well into the nineteenth century — longer than the general population decline suggests. Imperial edicts were issued in Manchu as well as Chinese; court rituals included Manchu-language components; key administrative documents were drafted in Manchu first.

More importantly, Rawski argues that the Qing court's **anxiety** about Manchu language loss — visible in Qianlong's repeated edicts lamenting bannerman linguistic incompetence — is itself evidence of the language's ideological significance. Courts don't issue panicked decrees about the preservation of practices they regard as merely decorative. The urgency of the language preservation campaign reveals how central Manchu linguistic identity was to the imperial self-conception, even as its material basis eroded.

---

## Rejecting the Assimilation Narrative: The Political Subtext

Rawski is explicit about the political stakes of the sinicization debate in a way that her critics sometimes used against her. She argues that the assimilation narrative serves **Chinese nationalist historiography** — it incorporates the Qing into a continuous Chinese civilizational story, validates Chinese cultural superiority (even conquerors become Chinese), and provides historical legitimation for the territorial claims of both the Republic of China and the People's Republic over territories (Tibet, Xinjiang, Mongolia, Manchuria) that the Qing conquered.

She is careful to distinguish between the nationalist use of the sinicization thesis and its scholarly validity — arguing that even if the thesis serves nationalist purposes, it must be evaluated on historical evidence. But by naming the political function, she implicitly asks her audience to examine whether the thesis's scholarly durability owes something to its political convenience. This was the move Chinese critics found most objectionable — and most difficult to answer.

Rawski also situates the debate within American area studies history, noting that Fairbank-tradition sinology's China-centric framework made the sinicization thesis seem natural and its alternatives marginal. The intellectual infrastructure of the field — which journals existed, which languages graduate students were trained in, which archives were accessible — systematically privileged Chinese-language sources and Han-centric frameworks. NQH's archival turn was not just a scholarly preference but a **structural correction** to institutional biases built into the field's formation.

---

## The Comparative Framework: Qing Among Conquest Dynasties

One of Rawski's most productive analytical moves is placing the Qing within a comparative framework of conquest dynasties and multiethnic empires. She invokes the Mongol Yuan, the Jurchen Jin, the Khitan Liao, and by extension the Mughal and Ottoman empires as comparative cases — asking what strategies conquest elites use to maintain ruling identity over subject populations, and how those strategies succeed or fail.

Within this comparative frame, the Qing's institutional maintenance of Manchu distinctiveness looks less like an anomaly requiring explanation and more like a **standard feature** of successful conquest dynasty governance. What requires explanation is not why the Qing maintained Manchu identity but why historians assumed they wouldn't — why the default expectation was assimilation rather than the boundary maintenance that characterizes most successful conquest regimes.

This comparative move also helps explain the Qing's extraordinary longevity and territorial reach. Previous conquest dynasties that sinicized rapidly — the Toba Wei is the classic case — either lost their conquest elite identity and eventually collapsed or were absorbed into successor states. The Qing's institutional resistance to full sinicization, Rawski argues, was a **survival strategy** that previous dynasties had failed to sustain.

---

## What "Reenvisioning" Actually Demands

Rawski concludes the address by specifying what a properly reenvisioned Qing history would require methodologically and institutionally. Graduate students must learn Manchu, Mongolian, and Tibetan alongside Chinese. Archives in Beijing, Taipei, Ulaanbaatar, Lhasa, and St. Petersburg (where Qing-era Manchu documents ended up) must be systematically exploited. Comparative frameworks from Inner Asian, Tibetan, and Islamic studies must be brought into conversation with Chinese history.

She also argues for what amounts to a **decentering of Han Chinese experience** in Qing historiography — not erasing it, but treating it as one domain among several rather than the normative center around which everything else orbits. The Qing empire included vast non-Han territories administered through non-Chinese frameworks; a history of the Qing that treats these as peripheral to the "real" Chinese story has already decided its conclusion before examining the evidence.

The address ends with a challenge to the field: the tools exist, the archives are increasingly accessible, and the theoretical frameworks from comparative empire studies are available. What remains is the scholarly will to use them — which requires overcoming both institutional inertia and the political pressures, from both Chinese nationalism and American area studies tradition, that make the sinicization thesis comfortable.

---

## Ping-ti Ho's Rebuttal and the Subsequent Exchange

Ho's published response (*"In Defense of Sinicization,"* Journal of Asian Studies, 1998) was sharp and personal. He accused Rawski of misreading sources, overstating Manchu distinctiveness, and — most pointedly — of serving a political agenda hostile to Chinese civilizational integrity. He defended his original empirical claims about the depth of Qing adoption of Chinese institutions while conceding some secondary points about Inner Asian policy.

The exchange revealed the debate's irreducible tensions. Ho's rebuttal was strongest on the administrative evidence — the Chinese bureaucratic structures, examination system, and Confucian ideology that did genuinely govern most of the empire most of the time. Rawski's framework was strongest on the Inner Asian frontier, the court's self-presentation, and the non-Chinese institutional structures. Neither side fully engaged the other's strongest evidence, which is characteristic of paradigm conflicts rather than ordinary scholarly disagreements.

The subsequent field — Elliott, Crossley, Millward, Perdue — largely validated Rawski's methodological program while producing more nuanced empirical accounts that acknowledged greater complexity than the address's polemical framing allowed. The address succeeded not as a final statement but as a **permission structure** — it made it professionally viable to pursue the NQH research program that her juniors then carried out in institutional detail.

## Brief Biography: Ge Zhaoguang

Ge Zhaoguang (葛兆光, b. 1950) is arguably the most intellectually serious historian working in mainland China today on questions of Chinese identity, intellectual history, and historiographical method. He is Distinguished Professor and Director of the Institute of Chinese Historical Geography at Fudan University in Shanghai — one of China's most prestigious academic positions. His career spans the full arc of post-Mao Chinese intellectual life: educated during the Cultural Revolution when formal schooling was disrupted, he rebuilt his scholarly formation through independent reading before entering academic life in the reform era.

His foundational scholarly work is the three-volume *Zhongguo Sixiang Shi* (中国思想史, *A History of Chinese Thought*, 1998–2001), a massive intellectual history of China from antiquity through the nineteenth century that established him as the leading historian of Chinese ideas of his generation. Unlike most Chinese intellectual historians of his era, Ge engaged seriously with Western historiographical theory — Foucault, Braudel, Hayden White — without simply importing their frameworks wholesale. This gave his work a methodological sophistication unusual in mainland Chinese scholarship.

*Zhai Zhi Zhongguo* (宅兹中国, *Here in "China" I Dwell*, 2011) emerged directly from Ge's engagement with New Qing History and related challenges to Chinese historical identity. A second volume, *He Wei Zhongguo* (何为中国, *What Is China?*, 2014), extended and sharpened the argument. Together they constitute the most sophisticated Chinese scholarly response to NQH — not a nationalist dismissal but a genuine philosophical engagement with the hardest questions NQH raises. Ge has also written *Xiang Qi Dongya* (想象异域, *Imagining the Other*) examining how China has historically constructed images of neighboring civilizations.

Ge occupies an unusual position: too independent and methodologically sophisticated to be simply a state-sponsored counter-voice to NQH, yet genuinely committed to defending the historical coherence of Chinese civilizational identity against what he sees as its dissolution by Western academic frameworks. Chinese nationalists sometimes claim him; he sometimes resists that claim.

---

## The Book's Origins and Occasion

*Here in "China" I Dwell* was not conceived as a polemic against New Qing History specifically, though it became the most cited Chinese response to it. Ge describes its origins in a set of lectures and essays written between 2003 and 2010 as he grappled with what he saw as a convergent set of challenges to the coherence of Chinese history as a field: NQH's multiethnic empire framework, postcolonial theory's challenge to civilizational categories, the "Asia as method" movement in East Asian studies, and domestic Chinese debates about Han ethnicity and minority history.

The title comes from a bronze inscription on a Zhou dynasty vessel — *"宅兹中国"* — meaning approximately "dwelling here in the central realm" or "making one's home in China." Ge chose it deliberately: it is among the earliest uses of the term *Zhongguo* (中国, "China" or "Middle Kingdom") in the archaeological record, dating to roughly 1040 BCE. Beginning with this inscription, Ge signals that his subject is the **historical depth and material reality** of "China" as a concept — not a modern nationalist fiction but a term with three thousand years of continuous if evolving usage.

The quotation marks around "China" in the title are equally deliberate: Ge acknowledges that *Zhongguo* is a constructed, contested, historically variable category. He is not arguing for a naive primordialism. He is arguing that the construction has enough historical depth, internal coherence, and material grounding to constitute a legitimate object of historical analysis — against positions (NQH, postcolonial theory) that he reads as dissolving it entirely.

---

## Part One: The Problem of "China" as Historical Category

### Why the Question Is Not Simple Nationalism

Ge opens by distinguishing his project from Chinese nationalist historiography, a distinction he maintains with some care throughout. He acknowledges that "China" as a historical category has been abused — by imperial-era sinocentrism, by Republican-era nationalist teleology, by PRC historiography's instrumental use of historical continuity to justify territorial claims. He is not defending those uses.

What he is defending is the claim that dismissing "China" as a historical category — treating it as a retrospective nationalist fiction with no pre-modern reality — throws out genuine historical content along with the nationalist distortions. The challenge is to recover what is historically real about Chinese civilizational continuity without reproducing the ideological uses to which that continuity has been put. This is a more sophisticated position than most Western readers of Chinese responses to NQH have credited.

### The Constructedness Problem

Ge engages seriously with the constructivist challenge: if "Manchu," "Han," "Mongol" are all constructed categories, why should "Chinese" be any different? He accepts the constructivist premise — all ethnic and civilizational identities are constructed — but argues that construction does not mean **arbitrary** or **equivalent**. Some constructions have deeper material and historical roots than others; some have been continuously inhabited and reproduced over longer periods; some generate more genuine collective experience and cultural production.

"China" as a civilizational category, Ge argues, has been continuously constructed and reconstructed over three millennia in ways that generated real cultural coherence — shared written language, shared literary and philosophical canons, shared ritual practices, shared cosmological frameworks — that constitute historical reality regardless of their constructed origin. This does not make "China" natural or inevitable, but it makes it historically substantial in ways that cannot be dissolved by pointing to its constructedness.

---

## Part Two: Critiquing New Qing History's Framework

### The Mosaic Problem

Ge's most fundamental critique of NQH — particularly Crossley's simultaneity thesis and Rawski's multiethnic empire framework — is what he calls the **mosaic problem**. NQH produces a picture of the Qing as a mosaic of distinct ethnic constituencies held together by a sovereign who performed different identities for each. This is historically illuminating as far as it goes, Ge argues, but it dissolves the question of what, if anything, held the mosaic together as something more than military domination.

Crossley's simultaneity answer — the emperor embodied a universal sovereign principle transcending all particular identities — Ge finds philosophically interesting but historically thin. It explains the emperor's self-conception but not the coherence experienced by subjects. What made the Qing empire something other than a collection of separately governed peoples under one military overlord? Ge's answer — which NQH cannot provide within its own framework — is the gravitational pull of Chinese civilizational frameworks that gave the empire its administrative language, its philosophical vocabulary, its literary culture, and its dominant social institutions.

### The Anachronism Charge Reversed

NQH critics frequently accuse Chinese historians of projecting modern nation-state categories backward onto the Qing. Ge argues the accusation applies equally to NQH itself, though differently. NQH's emphasis on ethnic boundary maintenance, sovereign identity performance, and multiethnic constituency management uses **modern concepts of ethnic identity and multicultural statecraft** that are no less anachronistic than the nation-state frameworks NQH criticizes.

Crossley's Qianlong, theorizing simultaneous sovereign identities, sounds remarkably like a postmodern theorist of subject positions. Elliott's bannermen, maintaining ethnic boundaries through institutional mechanisms, sound remarkably like Fredrik Barth's mid-twentieth century ethnographic subjects. Ge is not arguing these frameworks are wrong — he acknowledges their illuminating power — but that they are no more "native" to the eighteenth century than the sinicization narrative. Both are retrospective impositions; the question is which distorts less.

### The Source Selection Problem

Ge raises a methodological challenge to NQH's archival claims that is more pointed than simple defense of Chinese-language sources. He argues that NQH's privileging of Manchu-language court documents systematically overstates the Inner Asian dimensions of Qing governance because **court documents are not the empire**. The Manchu-language edicts, the multilingual inscriptions at Chengde, the Tibetan Buddhist patronage networks — these are the paper trail of a court that managed multiethnic constituencies at the imperial apex.

But the empire was governed, day to day, through Chinese-language bureaucratic structures administered by officials trained in the Confucian examination system, implementing legal codes derived from Ming precedent, managing a population that was overwhelmingly Han Chinese in culture and practice. The court's Inner Asian performances were real but they were **thin** compared to the massive Chinese administrative infrastructure that actually ran the empire. NQH, Ge argues, has mistaken the court's ideological self-presentation for the empire's operational reality.

---

## Part Three: The Civilizational Continuity Argument

### What Continuity Does and Does Not Mean

The heart of Ge's positive argument is a carefully qualified claim for Chinese civilizational continuity. He is explicit that he does not mean: ethnic continuity (the Han Chinese population was never ethnically stable or bounded), territorial continuity (China's borders have shifted enormously across history), political continuity (dynasties rose and fell, the state form changed radically), or cultural stasis (Chinese civilization transformed profoundly across three millennia).

What he does mean is continuity of a **civilizational core** — a constellation of practices, texts, institutions, and frameworks that remained recognizable across dynastic change, ethnic mixing, and territorial fluctuation. This core includes: the Chinese written language and its classical literary tradition; the Confucian social and philosophical framework; the examination-based bureaucratic meritocracy; the cosmological framework centering heaven-earth-human relationships; and the ritual practices that encoded these frameworks in daily and ceremonial life.

This core was not unchanging — Ge emphasizes its continuous evolution — but it was continuous in the sense that each generation of educated Chinese could read texts produced by previous generations, participate in inherited ritual frameworks, and recognize themselves as inhabitants of the same civilizational tradition. This kind of continuity, Ge argues, has no equivalent in the histories of most other world civilizations and constitutes a genuine historical phenomenon demanding explanation rather than deconstruction.

### The Scholar-Gentry as Civilizational Carrier

A key mechanism in Ge's continuity argument is the role of the **scholar-gentry class** (*shidafu* 士大夫) as the primary carrier of civilizational continuity across dynastic change. When dynasties fell and new rulers — including conquest rulers like the Mongol Yuan and Manchu Qing — took power, the scholar-gentry persisted as the administrative and cultural class that actually ran local society, educated the next generation, maintained the literary tradition, and staffed the bureaucracy.

This is where Ge partially rehabilitates the sinicization thesis without adopting Ho's version of it. The Qing didn't need to fully sinicize the emperor for Chinese civilization to remain operative — it needed to employ, accommodate, and ultimately depend on the scholar-gentry class whose entire formation was Chinese civilizational. The gravitational pull was institutional and social, not primarily ideological. Even a Qianlong who genuinely maintained Manchu identity at the court apex was governing through administrative structures whose operators were formed by and committed to Chinese civilizational frameworks.

### The Written Language as Civilizational Anchor

Ge gives particular weight to the **Chinese writing system** as the most powerful mechanism of civilizational continuity — an argument with no real equivalent in NQH scholarship. The Chinese script, non-alphabetic and morphosyllabic, created a written standard that transcended spoken dialect diversity and persisted across millennia with sufficient continuity that educated readers in each generation could access texts from previous generations.

This produced a civilizational memory mechanism with unusual depth and reach. A Song dynasty scholar could read Zhou dynasty bronze inscriptions (like the one in Ge's title) and Han dynasty philosophical texts. A Qing dynasty official could read Tang dynasty poetry and Warring States philosophy. This textual continuity generated a **shared imaginative world** — a civilizational interiority — that survived political disruption, ethnic mixing, and territorial change in ways that no purely political or ethnic framework could explain.

NQH's focus on Manchu-language documents, Ge argues, cannot account for this dimension of Qing civilization because the Manchu writing system was newly created in the seventeenth century and had no comparable depth of textual tradition behind it. Whatever the Qing court claimed about simultaneous sovereign identity, the civilizational weight of the written Chinese tradition was simply incommensurable with the weight of the Manchu tradition.

---

## Part Four: The Territorial and Political Stakes

### Why the Historical Argument Has Contemporary Consequences

Ge is more candid than most Chinese historians about the connection between historiographical argument and territorial politics — candid enough that his position is more nuanced than simple state apologetics. He acknowledges that Chinese historians' resistance to NQH is not purely scholarly: it is shaped by awareness that NQH's multiethnic empire framework, whatever its authors' intentions, provides historical arguments that could support Tibetan, Uyghur, and Mongolian claims to distinct political identities outside the Chinese state.

But he argues this awareness does not automatically invalidate the Chinese scholarly position. The question of whether Tibet was "historically Chinese" or "historically part of a Manchu empire that also included China" is a genuine historical question — and its answer has genuine political consequences. Scholars on both sides are aware of this. Pretending that NQH operates in a politics-free zone of pure scholarship while Chinese responses are contaminated by nationalism is, Ge argues, a double standard.

His own position on the territorial question is careful: he argues that the historical record supports a conception of Chinese civilizational space that has genuine pre-modern reality and that the Qing incorporated non-Han territories into this space through processes more complex than simple conquest — including genuine cultural exchange, administrative integration, and the operation of Chinese civilizational gravity on frontier populations over centuries.

### The "Imagined Community" Problem

Ge engages with Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* framework — the argument that nations are modern constructs of print capitalism and administrative standardization — and argues it applies poorly to the Chinese case. Anderson's framework was developed primarily from European and colonial Asian evidence; his claim that national identity is a modern phenomenon assumes the absence of pre-modern civilizational identity of the kind Ge is arguing China possesses.

China had a form of **civilizational community** before modern nationalism — held together by written language, examination culture, and Confucian social frameworks — that is not identical to modern national identity but is not simply its absence either. Applying Anderson's framework to Chinese history without modification produces the conclusion that "China" didn't exist before the twentieth century, which Ge finds historically untenable given three millennia of self-conscious *Zhongguo* identity in the textual record.

---

## Part Five: Methodology — *Jing*, *Shi*, *Zi*, *Ji* and the Sources of History

### Reading Across the Classical Canon

One of *Here in "China" I Dwell*'s most distinctive methodological contributions is Ge's argument for reading Chinese civilizational history through the full breadth of the traditional literary canon — the four categories of *jing* (classics), *shi* (histories), *zi* (philosophical masters), and *ji* (literary collections) — rather than privileging official historiography or court documents.

This is a methodological rebuke to both traditional Chinese historiography (which privileges official histories) and NQH (which privileges archival documents, especially in non-Chinese languages). Ge argues that civilizational continuity is most visible not in official records but in the **accumulated texture of literary and philosophical production** — poetry, essays, philosophical commentaries, local gazetteers, popular religious texts — that constitutes the living tradition Chinese literati inhabited across dynasties.

A historian who reads only official histories misses the civilizational world that gave those histories their meaning. A historian who reads only Manchu archival documents misses the Chinese civilizational world that surrounded, shaped, and ultimately outlasted the Manchu court's Inner Asian performances.

### The Ordinary People Problem

Ge makes an argument that NQH's court-focused methodology systematically ignores: the experience of **ordinary Chinese people** across the Qing period. Whatever the court was claiming about simultaneous sovereignty and Manchu ethnic distinctiveness, the roughly 300 million Han Chinese subjects of the Qing were living in a world structured by Chinese civilizational frameworks — Confucian family ethics, local religious practice, examination culture aspirations, literary tradition — that had nothing to do with the Inner Asian dimensions of Qing imperial identity.

These people were not experiencing the Qing as a multiethnic Inner Asian empire. They were experiencing it as the latest iteration of a Chinese dynastic order — legitimate or not depending on circumstances, but recognizably Chinese in its administrative forms, its cultural demands, and its relationship to the civilizational tradition they inhabited. Their experience, Ge argues, is historical evidence too — and NQH has no framework for incorporating it.

---

## Reception and Significance

*Here in "China" I Dwell* was received in China as the most intellectually serious response to NQH from within Chinese academia — praised for engaging the Western scholarship on its own terms rather than dismissing it from nationalist reflex. It became widely assigned in Chinese graduate programs in history and generated substantial critical discussion.

Western reception was more mixed. NQH scholars acknowledged Ge's methodological sophistication and the genuine force of his civilizational continuity argument while maintaining that his framework remained too centered on the literate Han Chinese experience to account for what Manchu-language sources reveal. **Mark Elliott** engaged Ge's work more directly and charitably than most Western NQH scholars, acknowledging that the debate had produced more heat than light on both sides and that genuine synthesis remained elusive.

The deepest unresolved tension the book exposes is between two legitimate historical objects: the **Qing court and its Inner Asian sovereign performances** (NQH's object) and **Chinese civilizational continuity as experienced by its inhabitants** (Ge's object). Both are real. Neither framework adequately contains the other. The controversy endures because it is not ultimately resolvable by more evidence — it is a disagreement about what history is for and whose experience counts as its primary subject matter.

## Brief Biography: James Millward

James Millward is Professor of Intersocietal History at Georgetown University, where he has taught since the late 1990s. He received his PhD from Stanford University, trained in Chinese, Manchu, and Central Asian history with particular emphasis on the Qing frontier. His linguistic range is exceptional even within New Qing History — he works in Chinese, Manchu, Persian, Chagatai Turkic, and Russian, giving him access to the full polyglot documentary universe of Central Asian imperial history.

*Beyond the Pass* (1998) was his first book, revised from his Stanford dissertation, and established him immediately as a major voice in both NQH and Central Asian studies. His subsequent work has moved progressively toward the modern period and the politics of the present: *Eurasian Crossroads: A History of Xinjiang* (2007) provided a comprehensive history from antiquity to the contemporary era for general and specialist readers alike; *Strangers on the Western Frontier* examined Qing policy toward Muslim and other non-Han peoples; and various articles and edited volumes have addressed Uyghur history, cultural policy in contemporary Xinjiang, and the politics of historical memory in Chinese Central Asian policy.

Millward has become increasingly prominent as a public intellectual on Xinjiang — appearing before the US Congress, writing in major newspapers, and providing historical context for contemporary debates about Uyghur rights and Chinese state policy in the region. This public role has made him a particular target of Chinese government criticism, which has accused him of providing historical cover for separatism. The line between his scholarly work on Qing Xinjiang and his public commentary on contemporary Xinjiang is one he navigates carefully but does not pretend does not exist.

Within NQH, Millward's contribution is distinct from Crossley's ideological focus and Elliott's institutional focus. His primary object is **the frontier itself** — the physical, economic, demographic, and administrative space where Qing imperial ambition met Central Asian reality — and he is more attentive than his NQH colleagues to the perspective of non-Manchu, non-Han peoples on the receiving end of Qing expansion.

---

## The Central Argument

*Beyond the Pass* argues that the Qing conquest and administration of Xinjiang (which the Qing called *Xinjiang* — "New Frontier" — after 1884, and before that administered as *Huijiang* or "Muslim Frontier") cannot be understood through Chinese administrative history alone. The Qing governed Xinjiang through a distinctive hybrid system that drew simultaneously on Chinese bureaucratic practice, Manchu banner administration, Mongol steppe governance conventions, and Central Asian Islamic political traditions — producing something genuinely novel that fits none of these frameworks neatly.

The book's territorial focus — the Tarim Basin, Dzungaria, and the passes connecting them to China proper — gives it a geographical argument as well as a historical one. The "pass" of the title is Jiayuguan, the western terminus of the Great Wall, traditionally marking the boundary between "China" and the outer world. Millward argues that the Qing's incorporation of territory "beyond the pass" fundamentally changed what "China" could mean as a spatial and civilizational concept — the empire was not China expanding outward but something larger than China absorbing new domains, including China, within itself.

This directly supports the NQH framework: if the Qing was simply China expanding, then Xinjiang's incorporation is Chinese history. If the Qing was a multiethnic Inner Asian empire, then Xinjiang's incorporation is Central Asian history that intersected with Chinese history — a very different story with very different contemporary implications.

---

## Chapter One: Introduction — Economy, Ethnicity, and Empire on the Qing Frontier

### Reframing the Frontier

Millward opens by identifying the analytical frameworks he is challenging. Traditional Chinese historiography treats Xinjiang as a peripheral territory that China periodically controlled or lost, with the Qing conquest representing a restoration of Chinese suzerainty over a region culturally and historically connected to the Chinese core. Western histories of Central Asia, conversely, have often treated Xinjiang as part of a Inner Asian world only tangentially connected to Chinese history.

Millward argues both frameworks are wrong for the same reason: they treat the frontier as a line rather than a zone, a boundary rather than a space with its own history, economy, and social dynamics. *Beyond the Pass* proposes to treat Xinjiang as a **frontier zone** — a space where multiple imperial systems, trade networks, ethnic communities, and cultural traditions intersected, competed, and hybridized — rather than as either Chinese periphery or Central Asian interior.

### The Economic Argument

Unusually for a work in the NQH tradition, Millward foregrounds economic history. He argues that the Qing's decision to conquer and maintain control of Xinjiang cannot be explained purely through strategic or ideological factors — the costs were enormous, the territory was distant, and the population was small and economically underdeveloped by Chinese standards. The economic logic of Qing frontier policy, including the massive grain and silver subsidies flowing westward from China proper, is central to understanding what the empire was actually doing in Central Asia.

He introduces the concept of the **fiscal-military frontier** — the Qing maintained Xinjiang through a continuous transfer of resources from the Chinese interior that amounted to a permanent imperial subsidy. This subsidy structured everything: the size of the garrison, the terms of trade with local merchants, the relationship between the Qing administration and local Uyghur (*Turki*) society. Understanding Xinjiang means understanding this economic architecture, which existing historiography had largely ignored.

---

## Chapter Two: The Land Beyond the Pass — Geography, People, and Pre-Conquest History

### The Physical Environment as Historical Actor

Millward devotes substantial attention to Xinjiang's physical geography — the Tarim Basin's desert interior ringed by mountain ranges, the oasis cities strung along the Silk Road routes, the Dzungarian steppe to the north — arguing that geographic structure shaped the possibilities of both indigenous society and imperial administration in ways historians typically underestimate.

The oasis geography of the Tarim Basin produced a distinctive social formation: relatively small, densely populated agricultural communities separated by vast desert, each with its own local identity and leadership structure but connected by trade networks and Islamic religious institutions. This geography made Xinjiang simultaneously easy to dominate (capture the oases, control the population) and difficult to administer (the distances between oases made integrated governance expensive and communication slow).

### The Peoples of the Frontier

This chapter provides the most sustained ethnographic attention to the **non-Qing peoples** of the region that NQH scholarship as a whole tends to underweight. Millward introduces the main population groups: the Turki-speaking oasis Muslims (ancestors of contemporary Uyghurs, though that term was not in use until the twentieth century), the Dzungar Mongols who had dominated the region before the Qing conquest, the Kazakhs and Kirghiz of the surrounding steppes, and smaller communities of Tajiks, Tibetans, and others in the mountain zones.

He is careful to historicize these categories — the "Uyghur" identity is a modern reconstruction applied retrospectively to what was in the Qing period a diverse population of oasis dwellers who identified primarily by oasis city, Islamic community, and sometimes lineage rather than by any overarching ethnic category. This careful attention to the historical construction of ethnic categories in Xinjiang becomes increasingly important as the book progresses toward the contemporary period in his later work.

### The Dzungar Empire and Its Destruction

The immediate pre-conquest history of Xinjiang is dominated by the Dzungar Mongol empire, which controlled most of Inner Asia from the mid-seventeenth century until the Qing conquest of the 1750s. Millward provides a careful reconstruction of Dzungar political organization, their relationships with the oasis Muslim populations, and the geopolitical dynamics that brought them into eventually fatal conflict with the expanding Qing empire.

The Qing conquest of Dzungaria in 1755–1757 involved what Millward carefully documents as a **near-genocidal destruction** of the Dzungar people — Qianlong's campaigns killed or dispersed the vast majority of the Dzungar population through a combination of military violence, deliberate massacre, famine, and epidemic disease. This history is significant both for understanding the demographic vacuum that subsequent Qing colonization policy was designed to fill, and for establishing that the Qing's relationship to Central Asian peoples included extreme violence alongside the more diplomatically sophisticated approaches that NQH tends to emphasize.

---

## Chapter Three: Conquest and Its Aftermath — The Qing Administrative System in Xinjiang

### The Military Colony System

The chapter examines how the Qing organized its newly conquered territory. Rather than extending the standard Chinese provincial administrative system westward, the Qing established a hybrid structure centered on **military colonies** (*tuntian* 屯田) and banner garrisons that represented a deliberate choice to govern Xinjiang through military rather than civilian administrative frameworks.

Millward traces the logic of this choice: the Qing recognized that Xinjiang was not Chinese territory being reintegrated but new territory being incorporated, and that Chinese civilian administrative institutions — the county (*xian*) system, the examination-based magistracy, the Confucian ritual frameworks — were neither appropriate nor sufficient for governing a Muslim, Turkic-speaking population with entirely different social institutions. The military colony system was an admission that different administrative tools were needed for different kinds of subjects.

### The Beg System and Indigenous Governance

The most important institutional innovation in Qing Xinjiang was the **beg system** — the incorporation of indigenous Uyghur local elites (*begs*) into the Qing administrative hierarchy as ranked officials responsible for governing their own communities under Qing oversight. Millward's analysis of this system is one of the book's most original contributions.

The begs were existing local leaders — landowners, merchants, Islamic scholars — whom the Qing appointed to formal administrative positions with Qing ranks and salaries while leaving their actual governance functions largely intact. They collected taxes, managed irrigation systems, adjudicated local disputes according to Islamic law, and served as intermediaries between the Qing garrison administration and the oasis Muslim population. The Qing neither replaced indigenous governance institutions nor integrated them into the Chinese administrative system — it **layered Qing authority over existing structures** while leaving those structures largely functional.

This is Millward's most direct contribution to the NQH argument: the beg system is evidence that the Qing governed Xinjiang through frameworks entirely distinct from Chinese administrative models, adapting to local institutional realities rather than extending Chinese civilization outward. It cannot be explained within a sinicization framework.

### The Garrison Economy and Its Contradictions

Millward examines the economic contradictions built into the Qing garrison system from its establishment. The military colonies were designed to make Xinjiang administratively self-sufficient — soldiers farming their own food, reducing the need for expensive supply lines from China proper. In practice this goal was never achieved. The garrison population was never large enough, the agricultural conditions too harsh, and the administrative costs too high for self-sufficiency.

The result was **permanent fiscal dependency** on subsidies from China proper — primarily silver from the Jiangnan region flowing westward to pay garrison salaries, and grain from Gansu flowing westward to supplement military food supplies. Millward quantifies these flows with unusual precision for an early Qing frontier study, making the economic architecture of empire visible in ways that purely political or cultural histories obscure.

---

## Chapter Four: Trade Networks and the Frontier Economy

### The Ili Valley as Commercial Hub

The Qing established its regional administrative headquarters at Ili (Ghulja) in the Dzungarian region, making it the commercial and administrative center of the new frontier. Millward reconstructs the Ili valley's economy in detail — a hub connecting Chinese commercial networks from the east with Central Asian caravan trade from the west, with the Qing administration both facilitating and taxing the flows between them.

He introduces a cast of commercial actors largely invisible in both Chinese and NQH historiography: **Andijani merchants** from the Kokand Khanate (in present-day Uzbekistan) who dominated long-distance trade between Xinjiang and Central Asia; Chinese Muslim merchants (*Hui*) who served as commercial intermediaries between Chinese and Turki-speaking communities; and Qing-licensed merchant houses operating under official supervision. The frontier economy was a multilingual, multiethnic commercial space that the Qing administered without fully controlling.

### The Kokand Connection

The relationship between the Qing and the Kokand Khanate — the Central Asian state that controlled the mountain passes west of Xinjiang and whose Andijani merchants dominated Xinjiang's external trade — receives sustained analysis. Millward shows this relationship was neither simple subordination (Kokand was too powerful and distant to be easily coerced) nor simple equality (the Qing regarded Kokand as a tributary state).

The result was a **negotiated commercial relationship** that gave Kokand merchants extraordinary privileges within Xinjiang — extraterritorial legal status, exemption from certain taxes, their own commercial judges — in exchange for political deference and the commercial tax revenue their trade generated. Millward draws an explicit comparison to the treaty port system that would be imposed on China by Western powers in the nineteenth century — a comparison that cuts against the standard narrative of Qing China as passive victim of Western commercial imperialism, revealing the Qing as itself a practitioner of asymmetric commercial diplomacy on its own frontiers.

### Chinese Merchant Networks

Alongside the Andijani and Hui merchant networks, Millward traces the gradual penetration of Han Chinese merchants from Gansu and Shaanxi into the Xinjiang market. These merchants — often operating as suppliers to the garrison — established commercial networks that over time created economic ties between Xinjiang and China proper independent of official administrative structures.

This commercial integration is one of the mechanisms through which Xinjiang was gradually connected to the Chinese economic world even as it remained administratively distinct. Millward is careful not to read this as evidence of sinicization — commercial integration does not imply cultural assimilation or administrative standardization — but acknowledges that over the long run, economic ties created dependencies and connections that shaped the terms of Xinjiang's incorporation into the Chinese state.

---

## Chapter Five: Social Structure and Ethnic Relations in Qing Xinjiang

### The Ethnic Hierarchy of the Frontier

Millward reconstructs the social structure of Qing Xinjiang as an explicitly hierarchical ethnic system — not the ethnic sovereignty of Crossley's imperial ideology but a practical social stratification visible in law, economic opportunity, and daily interaction. At the apex were Manchu and Mongol banner officers of the garrison. Below them were Han Chinese soldiers and merchants with greater legal protections than indigenous inhabitants. At the base were the Turki-speaking Muslim oasis farmers, governed through the beg system, subject to different legal codes and economic obligations.

This hierarchy was not merely theoretical — it structured daily life in the garrison towns, where residential segregation, commercial regulation, and legal jurisdiction all reinforced ethnic stratification. Millward uses garrison records and legal cases to make the hierarchy visible in concrete social practice rather than administrative theory.

### The Question of Qing Tolerance

NQH scholarship has sometimes implied that the Qing's multiethnic administrative flexibility amounted to a kind of **imperial tolerance** — a sophisticated recognition of diversity that managed different peoples on their own cultural terms. Millward complicates this characterization significantly. He documents instances of Qing administrative coercion, cultural imposition, and explicit discrimination against Xinjiang Muslims that sit uneasily with the tolerance narrative.

The Qing prohibited Muslim-Han intermarriage, restricted Muslim religious practice in certain contexts, and responded to Muslim rebellion with disproportionate violence — most notably in the suppression of the Jahangir Khoja uprising of 1820–1828, which Millward examines in detail. He argues these instances reveal that Qing administrative pluralism was instrumental rather than principled — the empire governed different peoples differently because it was pragmatically efficient, not because it was philosophically committed to cultural autonomy.

### Muslim Society and the Limits of Qing Penetration

A crucial argument in this chapter is that Qing administrative authority over Xinjiang's Muslim population was, in many respects, **shallow**. The beg system gave the Qing tax collection and basic order maintenance, but the deep social institutions of oasis Muslim life — Islamic law (*sharia*) as administered by religious scholars (*ulama*), Sufi brotherhood (*tariqa*) networks connecting oasis communities to each other and to Central Asian Islamic centers, agricultural water management systems governed by customary law — operated largely outside Qing administrative reach.

This shallowness of Qing penetration is significant for both historical and contemporary arguments. It means the Qing "possession" of Xinjiang involved far less cultural transformation of the indigenous population than the standard incorporation narrative implies — the oasis Muslims remained connected to Central Asian Islamic civilization in ways that Qing administration neither interrupted nor intended to interrupt.

---

## Chapter Six: The Qianlong Emperor and the Ideology of Frontier Expansion

### Qianlong's Self-Presentation as Universal Conqueror

This chapter engages most directly with the NQH ideological arguments of Crossley and Rawski, examining how Qianlong represented the Xinjiang conquest in his own writings, in official commemorative literature, and in visual and material culture. Millward finds substantial evidence supporting NQH claims about multiethnic sovereign self-presentation — Qianlong's commemorations of the Xinjiang conquest deployed multiple legitimating frameworks simultaneously: Confucian virtue (bringing civilization to barbarians), Buddhist karma (a bodhisattva-king expanding his realm), and Inner Asian martial prowess (a great khan defeating enemies in the steppe tradition).

The famous series of copper-plate engravings commemorating the Xinjiang campaigns, produced by Jesuit artists at the Beijing court and printed in Europe, receive careful analysis. Millward shows these images were designed for **multiple audiences** — their visual language drew on both Chinese court painting conventions and European military engraving traditions — suggesting Qianlong's ideological self-presentation extended even to imagined Western audiences.

### Where Ideology Met Reality

Millward's most important qualification of the NQH ideological argument is his attention to the **gap between imperial self-presentation and frontier reality**. Qianlong's ideological performances of universal sovereignty were produced at the Beijing court, for audiences who could appreciate their symbolic registers. In Xinjiang itself, governance was conducted by pragmatic military administrators dealing with fiscal shortfalls, garrison indiscipline, beg corruption, and periodic Muslim unrest — a world in which the subtleties of simultaneous sovereign identity were largely irrelevant.

This gap between court ideology and frontier practice is not fatal to Crossley's argument — she was always primarily making claims about the court, not the frontier — but it limits the historical reach of the ideological framework. The Qing empire contained both Qianlong's ideological performances and the mundane administrative realities of Xinjiang governance; a complete history needs both.

---

## Chapter Seven: Economic Integration and the Transformation of the Frontier

### The Colonization Program

Following the Dzungar genocide, the Qing faced a Dzungarian steppe largely depopulated. The response was a systematic colonization program — relocating Han Chinese, Hui Muslim, and Manchu bannermen populations into the vacant territory, establishing agricultural colonies to supply the garrison and reduce dependence on expensive supply lines from China proper.

Millward traces this colonization in demographic detail, showing how different ethnic groups were settled in different zones under different administrative arrangements — Han and Hui colonists in agricultural areas under modified civilian administration, bannermen in garrison colonies under military administration, Kazakh and Kirghiz herders in steppe areas under light oversight. The result was a **deliberately engineered ethnic mosaic** quite different from the relatively homogeneous oasis Muslim society of the Tarim Basin.

### Long-Term Economic Transformation

The chapter's most significant argument concerns the **long-term economic consequences** of Qing colonization for Xinjiang's relationship to China proper. The colonization program, whatever its immediate administrative motivations, gradually created economic connections — supply chains, commercial relationships, administrative dependencies — that tied Xinjiang to the Chinese economic world in ways that would prove difficult to reverse.

Millward is careful to distinguish this economic integration from cultural assimilation or political incorporation in a modern nationalist sense. Economic ties did not make Xinjiang "Chinese" in any meaningful cultural sense — the Muslim oasis population remained culturally, linguistically, and religiously connected to Central Asia. But they created material facts that subsequent Chinese claims over the territory would invoke as historical evidence of integration.

---

## Chapter Eight: Crisis and Transformation — The Nineteenth-Century Breakdown

### The Fiscal Crisis of Frontier Empire

The nineteenth century brought cascading crises to the Qing frontier system in Xinjiang. The silver drain caused by opium imports and indemnity payments following the Opium Wars reduced the flow of subsidy to frontier garrisons; the Taiping Rebellion diverted military resources from the frontier; and the Russian Empire's expanding presence in Central Asia created new geopolitical pressures that the weakened Qing could barely manage.

Millward traces how fiscal crisis translated directly into administrative breakdown in Xinjiang — garrisons underpaid, beg system supervision reduced, commercial regulation weakened. The conditions for the massive Muslim rebellions of the 1860s were created by fiscal failure at the imperial center, not simply by local grievances, though local grievances were genuine and substantial.

### The Yakub Beg Interlude

The rebellion of Yakub Beg, who established an independent Muslim state controlling most of Xinjiang from 1865 to 1877, receives careful treatment as a test case for the depth of Qing administrative and cultural penetration. Yakub Beg's state, which drew support from both the local Muslim population and from the Kokand Khanate, demonstrated that **Qing control of Xinjiang had been institutional rather than deeply rooted** — when the institutions broke down, indigenous and external alternatives emerged quickly.

Millward examines Yakub Beg's own legitimating strategies — he sought Ottoman recognition, cultivated British interest, and deployed Islamic rather than Inner Asian or Chinese political frameworks — showing that Xinjiang's political imagination remained connected to Central Asian Islamic civilization in ways that two centuries of Qing administration had not transformed.

### Zuo Zongtang's Reconquest and Provincialization

The Qing reconquest of Xinjiang under Zuo Zongtang (1876–1878) and the subsequent establishment of Xinjiang as a formal province in 1884 mark a crucial transition that Millward reads as evidence of **changing imperial strategy** rather than continuity. The provincialization decision — extending Chinese civilian administrative structures into Xinjiang for the first time — represented an acknowledgment that the hybrid military-beg system had failed. The Qing was now attempting to govern Xinjiang through the same institutions it used in China proper.

This transition, Millward argues, is the moment when Xinjiang's incorporation into "China" in a meaningful administrative sense actually began — not with the 1759 conquest, which established Qing sovereignty without Chinese administrative integration, but with the 1884 provincialization, which began that integration. The implication for contemporary territorial claims is significant but Millward leaves it largely implicit in this book, developing it more explicitly in later work.

## Chapter Nine: Conclusion — What Xinjiang Tells Us About the Qing

### The Frontier as Analytical Mirror

Millward's conclusion argues that studying the frontier illuminates the empire's center in ways that court-focused or China-proper-focused histories cannot. The Qing's management of Xinjiang reveals the empire's actual administrative repertoire — the range of governance strategies it deployed, the limits of its ideological coherence, the gap between imperial self-presentation and frontier reality — more clearly than studying the Chinese heartland, where Chinese institutional traditions were so dominant that Manchu distinctiveness became hard to isolate.

The frontier, precisely because it was genuinely new territory where Chinese administrative traditions had no purchase, forced the Qing to improvise — and those improvisations reveal the multiethnic, multi-institutional character of the empire that NQH identifies but that is harder to see in the Chinese core.

### The NQH Argument Extended and Qualified

Millward endorses the core NQH claim — the Qing was not simply a Chinese dynasty, and its governance of Xinjiang cannot be understood through Chinese administrative history alone — while qualifying it in ways his NQH colleagues have not always welcomed. The Qing's multiethnic administrative flexibility was pragmatic rather than principled; its ideological claims to universal sovereignty were produced at the court and did not necessarily structure governance practice on the frontier; and the tolerance implied by administrative pluralism coexisted with significant coercion and violence.

He also raises what would become the central political question of his subsequent career: if the Qing governed Xinjiang through non-Chinese frameworks, maintained its Muslim population's cultural connections to Central Asia, and only began genuine administrative integration with China in 1884, what historical arguments support the PRC's claim to Xinjiang as an inalienable part of China? He does not answer this question in *Beyond the Pass* — that comes later — but he has made asking it unavoidable.

## Where Millward Diverges from Crossley, Elliott, and Rawski

**Scale and subject.** Crossley focuses on the emperor, Elliott on the bannermen, Rawski on court institutions. Millward focuses on the frontier population — including the non-Manchu, non-Han peoples who were the objects of Qing imperial ambition. This shift of perspective from the imperial center to the imperial periphery is *Beyond the Pass*'s most distinctive methodological contribution.

**Violence and coercion.** Millward is more willing than his NQH colleagues to foreground the violence of Qing expansion — the Dzungar near-genocide, the brutal suppression of Muslim rebellions, the coercive dimensions of frontier administration. NQH's emphasis on sophisticated multiethnic governance can produce an inadvertently celebratory picture of Qing imperial flexibility; Millward's frontier perspective corrects this.

**Economic history.** The fiscal-military analysis of frontier administration — the quantification of subsidy flows, the reconstruction of commercial networks, the attention to how economic structures shaped administrative possibilities — is more developed in *Beyond the Pass* than in any other NQH work. Millward is doing economic history as well as cultural and institutional history.

**Contemporary implication.** Millward is the NQH scholar whose work has the most direct and explicit contemporary political implications — implications he has increasingly pursued in public as well as scholarly forums. This has made him both the most politically exposed member of the NQH school and, in Chinese government discourse, its most criticized representative.
