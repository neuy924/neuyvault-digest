## Abstract in English

This discussion compares Ann Laura Stoler, Patrick Wolfe, and James C. Scott as complementary theorists for analysing imperial borderlands, especially Xinjiang, Tibet, Taiwan, and Manchuria. Stoler foregrounds the intimate and epistemic machinery of empire: archives, racial classification, domesticity, family, sexuality, affect, colonial common sense, ruination, and imperial duress. Wolfe provides the structural grammar of settler colonialism: land seizure, elimination, demographic transformation, and the replacement or neutralisation of Indigenous sovereignty. Scott adds a third corrective by shifting attention to local agency under domination: hidden transcripts, infrapolitics, tactical compliance, evasion, state legibility, and the strategic use of imperial categories.

Applied to the four cases, the combined framework shows that these regions cannot be reduced to one model of colonialism. Xinjiang is best read as a frontier of territorial consolidation, securitised development, Islamic governance, and minority classification. Tibet requires analysis of sovereignty, monastic authority, sacred geography, religious memory, and the conversion of political authority into administrable culture. Taiwan is a layered colonial formation involving Indigenous dispossession, Japanese imperial subject-making, KMT authoritarian nationalism, and democratic reappropriation of Indigenous and Taiwanese identity. Manchuria is a multi-imperial railway-industrial frontier shaped by Qing legacies, Han migration, Russian and Japanese expansion, Manchukuo’s puppet sovereignty, and later nationalist memory.

The strongest conclusion is that Stoler and Wolfe explain what imperial power seeks to produce, while Scott explains how local actors survive within and against that production. Local actors did not merely resist or submit; they bargained, collaborated, reinterpreted, appropriated, evaded, and sometimes weaponised the very categories created to govern them.

最重要的結論是：斯托勒與沃爾夫說明帝國權力企圖生產什麼，而斯科特說明地方行動者如何在這些生產機制之中、之下與之外生存。地方行動者並非只是在反抗或服從；他們也協商、合作、重新詮釋、挪用、逃避，並且有時將統治者用來治理他們的分類轉化為反向主張的工具。

## Preamble

Ann Laura Stoler and Patrick Wolfe are often placed near each other because both helped move colonial studies away from a simple “Europe dominates non-Europe” model. But they are not doing the same thing.

**Stoler** asks: *How does empire make categories of race, intimacy, domesticity, knowledge, anxiety, and affect governable?*  
**Wolfe** asks: *What structural logic distinguishes settler colonialism from other colonial forms, especially when land seizure requires the replacement or elimination of Indigenous sovereignty?*

Put brutally: **Stoler studies the nervous system of empire; Wolfe studies the land logic of settler colonialism.**

## 1\. Ann Laura Stoler: brief bio, publications, major concepts

## 1.1 Bio

**Ann Laura Stoler** is an American anthropologist and historian of colonialism, currently associated with The New School, where she is listed as Willy Brandt Distinguished University Professor of Anthropology and Historical Studies. Her work has been central to colonial and postcolonial studies, especially around race, intimacy, affect, archives, and imperial afterlives.

Her early empirical terrain was Dutch colonial Southeast Asia, especially Sumatra and the Netherlands Indies, but her later work became broader: colonial archives, imperial formations, ruins, ruination, carceral worlds, affective governance, and the persistence of empire in liberal states.

## 1.2 Key publications

| Publication | Year | Importance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Capitalism and Confrontation in Sumatra’s Plantation Belt, 1870–1979* | 1985 | Early historical-anthropological work on capitalism, labour, plantation society, and colonial rule. |
| *Race and the Education of Desire* | 1995 | Reworks Foucault by arguing that European bourgeois sexuality and racial thinking cannot be understood apart from empire. |
| *Carnal Knowledge and Imperial Power* | 2002; later editions | Signature work on intimacy, sexuality, domesticity, mixed-race children, and race-making in colonial governance. |
| *Along the Archival Grain* | 2009 | Methodological intervention: read colonial archives not only for facts but for anxieties, categories, habits, and epistemic disturbance. |
| *Imperial Debris: On Ruins and Ruination* | 2013, edited volume | Shifts attention from ruins as objects to **ruination** as an ongoing process through which empire occupies the present. |
| *Duress: Imperial Durabilities in Our Times* | 2016 | Develops “duress” as a way of thinking colonial persistence, not merely colonial aftermath. |
| *Interior Frontiers* | 2022 | Extends her concern with inequality, affect, border-making, and imperial residues into contemporary political worlds. |

## 1.3 Major concepts

### A. Imperial intimacy

Stoler’s most influential move is to show that empire is not only made through armies, maps, treaties, law codes, or economic extraction. It is also made through **households, sexuality, childcare, servants, concubinage, mixed-race offspring, domestic hygiene, education, and affective discipline**.

In *Carnal Knowledge and Imperial Power*, she argues that colonial states were deeply concerned with who could sleep with whom, who counted as European, how children of mixed unions were classified, and how domestic life maintained racial boundaries. The colonial household was not private space outside politics; it was a site where racial rule was reproduced.

**Example: Dutch East Indies / Netherlands Indies**  
European men’s relations with Indigenous or mixed-race women were not simply “private.” They raised administrative questions: Was the child European? Could the child inherit? Was the household morally respectable? Did concubinage weaken European prestige? Stoler reads these questions as central to colonial rule, not peripheral scandal.

### B. Race as unstable colonial labour, not fixed doctrine

Stoler does not treat race as a finished ideology imposed from Europe onto colonies. Race is something colonial regimes had to **keep making**, policing, revising, and defending. Colonial categories were unstable because lived society constantly exceeded them: mixed families, multilingual children, servants, converts, educated elites, and mobile labourers all disturbed neat racial classification.

So race is not merely “belief.” It is an administrative, affective, sexual, domestic, and archival practice.

### C. Colonial common sense

In *Along the Archival Grain*, Stoler argues that colonial archives should not be read only “against the grain,” as if the archive simply hides subaltern truth beneath official lies. They should also be read **along the grain**: for the categories, worries, repetitions, silences, and anxieties that made colonial governance feel sensible to administrators.

This is a powerful methodological point. The archive is not only a repository of documents. It is a machine for producing what officials believed was real, normal, dangerous, deviant, or governable.

**Example: colonial memoranda on “native morality”**  
A Stolerian reading would not only ask whether the report is factually true. It would ask why administrators were obsessed with morality at all, what anxieties the category condensed, and how morality became a proxy for race, class, sexuality, labour discipline, and political loyalty.

### D. Imperial debris, ruination, duress

Stoler’s later work attacks the idea that empire is simply “over.” *Imperial Debris* argues for studying **ruination** rather than merely ruins: not just the leftover fort, plantation, prison, toxic site, or racial category, but the ongoing processes through which imperial arrangements continue to damage bodies, landscapes, institutions, and possibilities.

**Duress** names a condition in which colonial histories remain active without appearing as simple continuity. Colonialism persists as pressure, sediment, atmosphere, law, infrastructure, affect, and administrative habit.

**Example: postcolonial citizenship regimes**  
A Stolerian analysis of French republican universalism would not simply say “France had colonies.” It would ask how colonial categories of civilization, race, hygiene, family, danger, and assimilation continue to structure debates about migrants, Muslims, banlieues, schooling, veiling, policing, and national belonging.

## 2\. Patrick Wolfe: brief bio, publications, major concepts

## 2.1 Bio

**Patrick Wolfe** was an Australian historian and theorist whose work was foundational for settler colonial studies. His scholarship focused on race, colonialism, anthropology, Aboriginal history, and comparative settler societies including Australia, the United States, Canada, South Africa, Brazil, Israel, and India.

Wolfe is especially associated with the claim that **settler colonialism is a structure, not an event**, and with the concept of the **logic of elimination**.

## 2.2 Key publications

| Publication | Year | Importance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Settler Colonialism and the Transformation of Anthropology* | 1999 | Major account of anthropology’s entanglement with settler colonialism, especially in Australia. |
| “Settler Colonialism and the Elimination of the Native” | 2006 | His most cited and influential essay; formulates the logic of elimination. |
| *Traces of History: Elementary Structures of Race* | 2016 | Comparative account of racialization across land, labour, sovereignty, and colonial formations. |

## 2.3 Major concepts

### A. Settler colonialism is a structure, not an event

This is Wolfe’s most famous claim. It means settler colonialism is not reducible to the moment of invasion, conquest, or frontier violence. It continues through law, property, mapping, schooling, child removal, citizenship, policing, resource extraction, heritage regimes, and national mythology.

The settler colony does not merely exploit Indigenous people. It seeks to **replace Indigenous sovereignty with settler sovereignty**.

**Example: Australia**  
The British arrival in 1788 is not the whole story. The continuing structure includes terra nullius, land law, pastoral expansion, missions, reserves, child removal, assimilation policy, mining rights, heritage management, and the politics of recognition.

### B. Logic of elimination

Wolfe’s best-known phrase is the **logic of elimination**. In his 2006 article, he argues that settler colonialism is inherently eliminatory but not always genocidal; elimination can take many forms, including physical killing, forced removal, assimilation, biological absorption, legal erasure, cultural destruction, and statistical disappearance.

The key mechanism is this: **settlers want land, and Indigenous societies are obstacles because they possess prior claims to that land**.

This is why Wolfe’s sentence “land is life” became conceptually important. The contest is not only over resources but over the conditions of collective existence.

### C. Negative and positive dimensions of settler colonialism

Wolfe’s model has two sides:

| Dimension | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Negative | Destroy, remove, absorb, or dissolve Indigenous society | massacres, removals, boarding schools, bans on language, termination policy |
| Positive | Build a new settler society on the seized land | farms, towns, cadastral maps, constitutions, national myths, property law |

This is why settler colonialism is not only violence. It is also **world-building**. It destroys one political geography and manufactures another.

### D. Race as a technology of colonial organization

In *Traces of History*, Wolfe argues that racialization is historically specific. Different racial regimes arise from different colonial needs: land seizure, labour exploitation, enslavement, exclusion, or incorporation.

This is a major point: Wolfe does not think “race” works the same way everywhere.

**Example: Indigenous peoples vs enslaved Africans in the United States**  
A Wolfean reading would say that Indigenous peoples were racialized in ways tied to elimination from land, while enslaved Africans were racialized in ways tied to extraction of labour. Both are racial regimes, but their structural functions differ.

## 3\. Deep conceptual comparison

## 3.1 The core difference: intimacy versus land

The cleanest distinction is this:

| Question | Stoler | Wolfe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is colonial power trying to manage? | The boundaries of race, sexuality, family, affect, knowledge, and imperial belonging | The replacement of Indigenous sovereignty by settler sovereignty |
| Where does power operate? | Household, archive, school, bureaucracy, affect, body, memory | Land, law, property, sovereignty, demographic replacement, racial regime |
| What is the hidden engine? | Anxiety over unstable colonial categories | Structural need to eliminate Indigenous claims to land |

Stoler is strongest when colonial power depends on **classification, intimacy, and affective governance**. Wolfe is strongest when colonial power depends on **territorial replacement**.

## 3.2 Different theories of race

Stoler and Wolfe both reject race as a simple biological belief. But they explain race differently.

### Stoler: race as anxious boundary-work

For Stoler, race emerges through repeated acts of boundary maintenance. Colonial states are nervous because their categories do not hold. Mixed children, servants, concubines, educated colonized elites, poor Europeans, and mobile subjects blur the line between ruler and ruled.

So race is produced through anxious administration.

**Example: Netherlands Indies**  
The colonial regime worries about European children raised by Indigenous nannies, European men living with local women, mixed-race children claiming status, and domestic arrangements that blur the distinction between “European” and “native.” Race must be taught, performed, and policed.

### Wolfe: race as structural technology

For Wolfe, race is a tool used to organize colonial relations according to material objectives. If the colonial objective is land, race helps eliminate Indigenous people as political claimants. If the objective is labour, race helps preserve an exploitable labour force.

**Example: United States**  
Indigenous people are pushed toward disappearance, removal, or assimilation because they obstruct settler land claims. Enslaved Africans, by contrast, are reproduced as a labour force. Hence different racial logics coexist in the same imperial field.

**Comparison:**  
Stoler gives you the **microphysics of racial anxiety**. Wolfe gives you the **structural function of racialization**.

## 3.3 Different theories of time

| Temporal model | Stoler | Wolfe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Colonial past | Persists as residue, affect, debris, duress | Persists as ongoing structure |
| Main temporal critique | Empire is not over; it remains in habits, ruins, categories, bodies | Settler invasion is not over; it is reproduced through institutions |
| Key danger | Mistaking colonialism for a closed historical period | Mistaking settler colonialism for a past event |

Stoler’s time is **sedimentary**: empire leaves residues, pressures, and traces that shape the present. Wolfe’s time is **structural**: settler colonialism continues because the settler polity continues to depend on Indigenous dispossession.

**Example: Canada**  
A Stolerian analysis might focus on residential school archives, child welfare files, bureaucratic affect, apology rituals, trauma inheritance, and the administrative production of Indigenous family life as a problem.  
A Wolfean analysis would focus on how residential schools served the elimination of Indigenous societies by targeting reproduction, kinship, language, and land-based sovereignty.

Both are valid; they illuminate different layers.

## 3.4 Different views of the archive

Stoler makes the archive a central theoretical object. Wolfe uses archives historically, but the archive is not his main conceptual machine.

| Issue | Stoler | Wolfe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Archive | A site where colonial common sense is produced | Evidence for structural relations of settler colonialism |
| Reading method | Read along the grain: anxiety, repetition, categories, affect | Read for land seizure, elimination, replacement, racialization |
| Best question | What did colonial officials fear, assume, classify, and normalize? | What institutional pattern did these documents help sustain? |

**Example: land survey records**  
A Stolerian reading asks: What categories of improvement, idleness, domesticity, productivity, and civilization made land seizure appear rational?  
A Wolfean reading asks: How did cadastral mapping convert Indigenous territory into settler property?

## 3.5 Different political ontology

Stoler’s empire is unstable, nervous, internally contradictory, and affectively charged. Wolfe’s settler colonialism is structurally coherent even when its techniques vary.

This does not mean Wolfe is simplistic. It means he privileges a deeper logic: **replacement**. Stoler privileges the instability and labour required to make imperial categories endure.

| Theoretical temperament | Stoler | Wolfe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emphasis | Ambivalence, anxiety, intimacy, contradiction, residues | Structure, land, sovereignty, elimination, replacement |
| Colonial power appears as | Nervous, recursive, affective, epistemic | Durable, territorial, eliminatory, institutional |
| Best metaphor | Colonial rule as an anxious household/archive | Settler colonialism as a land-replacement machine |

## 4\. Worked examples

## 4.1 Australia

### Wolfean reading

Australia is a paradigmatic settler colony. The central issue is not merely British rule over Aboriginal peoples but the replacement of Indigenous sovereignty with settler sovereignty. Terra nullius, pastoral leases, reserves, child removal, assimilation, and contemporary land politics are not separate episodes; they are part of an ongoing structure of elimination.

Wolfe is especially useful here because the question is land.

### Stolerian reading

Stoler would ask different questions. How did settler Australians learn to feel entitled to land? How did domestic arrangements, child welfare bureaucracies, sexuality, hygiene, schooling, and archival categories produce Aboriginal families as objects of intervention? How do colonial affects persist in contemporary liberal language: care, protection, improvement, welfare?

### Comparison

| Australia | Wolfe sees | Stoler sees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core structure | Indigenous dispossession and settler replacement | Affective-bureaucratic production of Aboriginal life as governable |
| Key institution | Property law, frontier violence, child removal, sovereignty | Welfare files, domestic policy, archives, moral categories |
| Strongest insight | Settler society requires elimination of Indigenous claims | Elimination works through intimate and bureaucratic categories |

## 4.2 Netherlands Indies / Indonesia

### Stolerian reading

This is Stoler’s strongest terrain. Dutch colonial rule depended on racial categories that were unstable in practice. European men formed households with Indigenous women; mixed-race children challenged legal and social boundaries; domestic life became a site of racial governance. Colonial power operated through sexuality, family, schooling, servants, and moral regulation.

### Wolfean reading

Wolfe is less directly suited here because the Netherlands Indies was primarily an extractive and administrative colony, not a classic settler colony in the Australian or North American sense. Land mattered, but the Dutch did not seek wholesale demographic replacement by settlers in the same way.

### Comparison

| Netherlands Indies | Stoler sees | Wolfe sees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core issue | Racial intimacy and imperial boundary-making | Not a paradigmatic settler-colonial structure |
| Best evidence | Household rules, concubinage debates, education, mixed children | Plantation land, labour, colonial capitalism |
| Stronger theorist | Stoler | Wolfe only partially applicable |

## 4.3 United States

### Wolfean reading

The United States is a settler-colonial formation built through Indigenous elimination and African enslavement. Wolfe’s comparative theory helps distinguish two racial logics: Indigenous peoples are targeted as obstacles to land, while enslaved Africans are racialized as exploitable labour.

### Stolerian reading

Stoler would push into the intimate machinery: anti-miscegenation law, slave households, domestic service, sexual coercion, boarding schools, sentimental nationalism, family separation, archives of welfare and criminality.

### Comparison

| United States | Wolfe | Stoler |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indigenous history | Elimination of Native sovereignty for settler land | Intimate/bureaucratic governance of Native family, schooling, affect |
| Slavery | Racialization tied to labour extraction | Sexuality, reproduction, domestic violence, archival silences |
| Present | Settler structure persists | Imperial residues and racial affects persist |

## 4.4 Palestine / Israel

This is where the comparison becomes politically charged.

### Wolfean reading

A Wolfean analysis would ask whether Zionist settlement and Israeli state formation operate through settler-colonial dynamics: land acquisition, demographic engineering, removal, fragmentation of Indigenous Palestinian territory, and replacement of prior sovereignty. Wolfe’s framework is often used in scholarship precisely because it foregrounds land, demography, and elimination.

### Stolerian reading

A Stolerian analysis would examine permits, family unification rules, archives, humanitarian language, security affect, bureaucratic categories, home demolition paperwork, spatial anxiety, and the everyday production of differential rights.

### Comparison

| Palestine / Israel | Wolfean strength | Stolerian strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land and sovereignty | Very strong | Secondary |
| Bureaucracy and affect | Useful but less central | Very strong |
| Demography | Central | Also relevant, especially through intimacy and family law |
| Risk | May flatten internal histories and ideological heterogeneity | May understate the structural land-replacement dynamic |

## 4.5 Okinawa / Ryukyu

Given your previous Okinawa thread, this is a useful border case.

### Wolfean reading

Okinawa is not a straightforward settler colony in the same sense as Australia or the United States. Japan incorporated the Ryukyu Kingdom through annexation, state assimilation, militarization, and cultural subordination. There was no simple mass settler replacement of the entire Indigenous population, though there were demographic, military, and administrative transformations.

A Wolfean frame helps only if focused on **land, military bases, sovereignty, and the displacement of local autonomy**. It is less adequate if forced into a classic settler-colonial template.

### Stolerian reading

Stoler is arguably more flexible here. Her framework can analyze language policy, schooling, bodily discipline, affective assimilation, imperial anxiety, archival recoding, and the production of Okinawans as simultaneously Japanese and not-quite-Japanese.

### Comparison

| Okinawa / Ryukyu | Wolfe | Stoler |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annexation | Useful if tied to sovereignty and land | Strong for assimilation, classification, imperial hierarchy |
| Military bases | Stronger: territorial dispossession and sacrifice zones | Strong: ruination, duress, imperial debris |
| Identity | Less central unless racialized through elimination/replacement | Very strong: ambivalent belonging, affect, hierarchy |
| Best combined reading | Japanese imperial incorporation plus military-colonial land regime | Imperial intimacy, bureaucratic classification, durable debris |

## 5\. The deepest theoretical contrast

## 5.1 Stoler: colonialism as unstable rule that must continually secure itself

Stoler’s colonialism is never fully confident. It produces files, categories, domestic rules, racial distinctions, and moral panics because it is anxious. It fears contamination, degeneration, mixedness, sexual disorder, mimicry, and administrative uncertainty.

Her power is in showing that colonial rule is not only domination from above. It is a fragile project of **making distinctions feel natural**.

## 5.2 Wolfe: settler colonialism as a coherent structure of replacement

Wolfe’s settler colonialism is not primarily anxious; it is strategic and structural. It wants land. Because Indigenous people stand in relation to land as prior sovereigns, settler colonialism seeks to eliminate them as peoples, jurisdictions, and historical claimants.

His power is in showing that benevolent-sounding policies—protection, assimilation, recognition, development—can still serve eliminatory ends.

## 5.3 Their disagreement is partly about where explanation should begin

| Starting point | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Start with Stoler | You see the colonial state’s categories, affects, anxieties, and everyday practices. |
| Start with Wolfe | You see the structural need for land, sovereignty, replacement, and racialized elimination. |

Neither is “better” in general. But they are not interchangeable.

For **settler colonies**, Wolfe should usually be the base layer.  
For **imperial governance, mixed colonial societies, archives, race-sexuality regimes, and postcolonial residues**, Stoler should usually be the base layer.  
For **complex cases such as Okinawa, Algeria, Taiwan, South Africa, Palestine/Israel, or Xinjiang**, the best analysis usually requires both.

## 6\. Integrated model: how to use both together

A strong combined framework would proceed in layers:

| Layer | Question | Best theorist |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1\. Territorial structure | Is the colonial project based on land seizure, replacement, or sovereignty transfer? | Wolfe |
| 2\. Racial technology | How are groups classified differently according to land, labour, sexuality, or security? | Wolfe + Stoler |
| 3\. Intimate governance | How are family, sexuality, children, household, and reproduction governed? | Stoler |
| 4\. Archive and knowledge | How does the state produce “common sense” about the colonized? | Stoler |
| 5\. Afterlife | How does colonialism persist in law, land, affect, infrastructure, and institutions? | Both |
| 6\. Political implication | Is the remedy recognition, decolonization, land return, reparations, archival repair, institutional transformation, or sovereignty? | Wolfe + Stoler |

## 7\. Red Team Review

| Potential weakness | Why it matters | How to test it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Over-Wolfean analysis can reduce every colonial situation to land and elimination | Some empires were extractive, administrative, commercial, or assimilationist without full settler replacement | Ask whether settlers sought demographic sovereignty, not merely rule or extraction |
| Over-Stolerian analysis can make colonialism feel like atmosphere rather than structure | “Duress” and “debris” can become too broad if not tied to institutions | Identify specific legal, material, bureaucratic, or spatial mechanisms |
| “Settler colonialism” can become politically overextended | It loses precision if applied to every annexation or minority-borderland case | Distinguish settlement, annexation, militarization, assimilation, and replacement |
| “Imperial intimacy” can become microhistorical without enough political economy | Household and affect matter, but land, labour, capital, and sovereignty still structure the field | Pair Stoler with land/labour analysis |
| Both can underplay anti-colonial agency if read carelessly | Colonized people are not only governed, classified, or eliminated; they resist, adapt, and theorize | Include Indigenous, colonized, and subaltern intellectual traditions |

---

## 8\. Bottom line

**Patrick Wolfe gives you the structural grammar of settler colonialism: land, elimination, replacement, sovereignty.**  
**Ann Laura Stoler gives you the intimate and epistemic grammar of empire: race, sexuality, family, affect, archive, ruination.**

Use **Wolfe** when the key mechanism is **settler replacement of Indigenous sovereignty**.  
Use **Stoler** when the key mechanism is **imperial classification, intimacy, anxiety, and durable colonial residue**.  
Use **both** when the case involves land seizure plus intimate/bureaucratic governance: Australia, Canada, the United States, Palestine/Israel, French Algeria, South Africa, Okinawa under militarized incorporation, and many frontier borderlands.

## Preamble

The useful distinction is not **Stoler or Wolfe**. It is **which layer of colonial power each theorist exposes**.

**Ann Laura Stoler** is best for analysing how empire makes rule feel normal through family, sexuality, education, archives, affect, racial classification, domesticity, fear, “common sense,” and durable residues. **Patrick Wolfe** is best for analysing settler colonialism as a land-centred structure: the replacement or neutralisation of Indigenous sovereignty through elimination, settlement, law, demography, and property.

Applied to **Xinjiang, Tibet, Taiwan, and Manchuria**, Wolfe clarifies the territorial and demographic logic; Stoler clarifies the intimate, bureaucratic, epistemic, and affective machinery by which those projects become governable.

| **Manchuria** | Qing homeland/frontier; Han migration; Russian/Japanese imperial rivalry; Manchukuo settler-developmental state | How did Japanese and other state projects seek land, resources, and replacement through settlement and puppet sovereignty? | How did Manchukuo manufacture legitimacy, modernity, race, labour, and imperial affect? | **Wolfe for settlement; Stoler for Manchukuo ideology** |

## I. Stoler’s Concepts in Depth

## 1\. Stoler’s central move: empire is not outside domestic life

Stoler attacks the habit of treating empire as something that happens only through armies, governors, treaties, plantations, and extractive economies. Her major insight is that empire also works through **bedrooms, nurseries, schools, servants’ quarters, marriage law, concubinage, adoption, hygiene, food, affect, and respectable manners**.

In *Carnal Knowledge and Imperial Power*, Stoler shows that colonial governance was deeply concerned with sexuality, mixed descent, European domesticity, and the moral boundaries of colonial society. Race was not simply believed; it was **reproduced through intimate regulation**. Colonial authorities cared about who could marry whom, who could inherit European status, how children were raised, whether European men living with Indigenous women undermined prestige, and whether domestic life blurred the line between coloniser and colonised. Her work is especially associated with Dutch colonial Southeast Asia, but the conceptual reach is broader.

### Core implication

Empire does not merely govern territory. It governs **reproduction**, literally and socially.

A Stolerian question is therefore not only:

> Who owns the land?

It is also:

> Who may form a family, transmit status, speak the legitimate language, raise children, inherit property, enter school, appear respectable, and count as fully civilised?

---

## 2\. Race as anxious boundary-work

Stoler’s concept of race is more fragile and processual than many race theories. She does not treat race as a fixed doctrine imposed from above. Race is a **repeated administrative labour**: always being clarified, repaired, policed, and re-naturalised because colonial reality constantly unsettles it.

Mixed families, multilingual children, servants, converts, local elites, poor Europeans, military auxiliaries, and mobile traders all disturb neat racial categories. The colonial state therefore produces files, laws, etiquette, anxieties, and moral panics.

### Stoler’s race is not only phenotype

It is also:

| Domain | Stolerian meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Sexuality** | Who may desire whom without threatening imperial hierarchy |
| **Domesticity** | Whether the household reproduces racial order |
| **Education** | Whether children are trained into proper imperial categories |
| **Language** | Whether speech marks civilisation, mimicry, or danger |
| **Hygiene** | Whether bodies are coded as disciplined or degenerate |
| **Affect** | Whether colonised people are feared, pitied, desired, infantilised, or distrusted |
| **Archive** | Whether categories become administratively real |

This is why Stoler is useful for borderlands. Frontier zones are precisely where race, civilisation, loyalty, ethnicity, language, religion, and kinship rarely align neatly.

---

## 3\. “Imperial intimacies”

“Imperial intimacy” is not only sex. It refers to the fact that colonial power penetrates the domains conventionally imagined as private: family, sentiment, child-rearing, morality, domestic service, bodily comportment, and affective discipline.

In a Stolerian frame, the household is not a retreat from empire. It is a **colonial institution**.

### Example logic

In a frontier colony, the state may ask:

- Are local children being schooled in the imperial language?
- Are women’s reproductive lives aligned with state goals?
- Are marriages legible to the state?
- Are religious teachers competing with state schooling?
- Are families transmitting loyalties that exceed the state?
- Are domestic spaces producing “separatism,” “backwardness,” “fanaticism,” or “insufficient civilisation”?

These questions are not secondary. They are the intimate infrastructure of empire.

---

## 4\. “Along the archival grain”

Stoler’s *Along the Archival Grain* is a methodological intervention. Many postcolonial scholars were trained to read “against the grain”: to recover suppressed subaltern voices from biased colonial records. Stoler does not reject that. But she says we should also read **along the grain**: to understand how colonial officials thought, worried, classified, repeated themselves, contradicted themselves, and manufactured common sense.

The archive is not just a storehouse of facts. It is a site where colonial reality is produced. Stoler’s institutional profile describes her work on colonial archives and “common sense” as central to her scholarship.

### What to look for in a Stolerian archive

| Archival feature | What it reveals |
| --- | --- |
| Repetition | Administrative obsession |
| Classification | How the state makes people legible |
| Moral vocabulary | Anxiety disguised as ethics |
| Ambiguous categories | Places where rule is unstable |
| Silences | What the archive cannot admit |
| Excessive detail | Where officials feel threatened |
| Contradiction | Where imperial common sense fails |

### Example

A report on “frontier stability” is not merely evidence of unrest. It is also evidence of how the state imagines proper order, improper loyalty, suspicious religion, dangerous kinship, and acceptable speech.

---

## 5\. Colonial common sense

“Colonial common sense” is the set of assumptions that make domination appear natural, benevolent, technical, or necessary.

It is not necessarily crude racism. It may appear as:

- development
- hygiene
- education
- security
- women’s liberation
- anti-feudal reform
- modernisation
- counterterrorism
- ecological protection
- poverty alleviation
- cultural integration
- infrastructure planning

Stoler’s usefulness lies in asking how these vocabularies become plausible, affective, and bureaucratically durable.

### Analytical warning

A Stolerian reading does **not** say that all development or education is fake. It asks how “development” and “education” may become vehicles for rule, classification, dispossession, assimilation, and the remaking of subjectivity.

---

## 6\. Ruins, ruination, imperial debris

Stoler’s later work shifts from colonial rule as a past event to empire as an ongoing condition. *Imperial Debris* redirects attention from ruins as inert remains to **ruination** as an active process through which imperial power occupies the present.

This is crucial. A ruined monastery, abandoned railway, toxic mine, military base, border checkpoint, resettlement village, or family archive is not merely “left over.” It may continue to organise life.

### Ruination means

| Not merely | But rather |
| --- | --- |
| Old colonial buildings | Active material arrangements |
| Memory of violence | Ongoing psychic and social pressure |
| Historical injustice | Present infrastructure |
| Aftermath | Continuation under changed vocabulary |
| Ruins | Ruination as process |

---

## 7\. Duress

In *Duress*, Stoler asks how imperial formations endure as pressure, exposure, habit, and constraint. One description of the book frames it as examining the temporal and affective space in which colonial inequities endure.

**Duress** is stronger than “legacy.” A legacy can sound passive. Duress means people live under conditions shaped by past imperial violence that remains active in law, land, body, institution, and atmosphere.

### Stolerian duress in borderlands

Duress appears when a population is formally incorporated but remains structurally suspected.

It may take the form of:

- permanent loyalty testing
- language hierarchy
- securitised schooling
- regulated religious life
- infrastructural extraction
- border surveillance
- controlled mobility
- state-sponsored demographic engineering
- environmental degradation
- archives that classify a people as a problem

---

## II. Wolfe’s Concepts as Counterpoint

Patrick Wolfe’s central argument is that settler colonialism is not an episode of conquest but an enduring structure. His 2006 article formulates the now-standard claim that settler colonisers “come to stay” and that invasion is a structure rather than an event.

His second key concept is the **logic of elimination**. Settler colonialism seeks land; Indigenous peoples are obstacles because their existence sustains prior claims to land and sovereignty. Elimination does not always mean killing. It can mean removal, assimilation, legal erasure, child transfer, cultural destruction, statistical absorption, or conversion of Indigenous peoples into minorities within a settler state. Wolfe explicitly distinguishes elimination from genocide, while arguing that genocide is one possible expression of the eliminatory logic.

## Wolfe’s model in one table

| Concept | Meaning | Diagnostic question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure, not event | Invasion persists through institutions | Does the system continue to require dispossession? |
| Logic of elimination | Native sovereignty must be removed, absorbed, or neutralised | What forms of disappearance are being produced? |
| Land is central | Settlers want territory, not merely tribute | Is land/resource control the core objective? |
| Positive/negative dimensions | Destroy Indigenous society; build settler society | What is being dismantled, and what is being erected? |
| Race as technology | Race works differently depending on colonial objective | Is racialisation tied to land, labour, security, or assimilation? |

## III. Applying Stoler and Wolfe to Xinjiang

## 1\. Historical baseline

Xinjiang became “Xinjiang” — literally “new frontier” or “new territory” — through Qing expansion. Britannica notes that Qing authorities asserted control over the region, sent loyal Muslims from Gansu to settle in northern Xinjiang oases, and made Xinjiang a province in 1884.

This matters because Xinjiang is not simply a modern PRC problem. It is a long imperial frontier converted into a province, then into a socialist autonomous region, then into a heavily securitised borderland.

## 2\. Wolfean reading of Xinjiang

A Wolfean analysis asks whether Xinjiang shows a settler-colonial structure: land seizure, demographic transformation, elimination of Indigenous sovereignty, and replacement by state-backed settlement.

### Strong Wolfean elements

| Element | Xinjiang case |
| --- | --- |
| Land and resources | Strategic borderland, oil, gas, minerals, cotton, agriculture, infrastructure corridors |
| Settlement | Long history of state-sponsored military/agricultural settlement and Han in-migration |
| Native sovereignty neutralised | Uyghur, Kazakh, Mongol, Kyrgyz, and other local claims subordinated to state sovereignty |
| Elimination beyond killing | Cultural assimilation, language shift, religious regulation, reclassification as minorities within a unitary state |
| Positive settler construction | New towns, farms, production units, transport corridors, industrial zones, security infrastructure |

A strict Wolfean reading would say: the core question is not whether the state is “repressive” in a generic sense. The question is whether territorial consolidation requires the transformation of Indigenous peoples into administratively managed minorities whose land, mobility, religion, and reproduction are subject to state control.

### Wolfean caution

Xinjiang is not identical to Australia or North America. There is no simple overseas settler colony. It is an **imperial-borderland settler formation** inside a continental state. Wolfe can still be useful, but only if adapted to Inner Asian empire and socialist/post-socialist state-building.

## 3\. Stolerian reading of Xinjiang

Stoler is extremely useful for Xinjiang because the region is governed not only through land but through **classification, intimacy, affect, archive, and suspicion**.

### Stolerian objects in Xinjiang

| Domain | Stolerian question |
| --- | --- |
| Family | How does the state read household life as a site of loyalty or danger? |
| Religion | How does Islamic practice become classified as culture, extremism, backwardness, or threat? |
| Language | How does bilingual education reorganise hierarchy and futurity? |
| Labour | How are work, discipline, and poverty alleviation moralised? |
| Dress and bodily conduct | How are piety, modernity, gender, and suspicion made visible? |
| Archives/data | How do files, registries, biometrics, and risk categories produce governable subjects? |
| Affect | How do fear, gratitude, suspicion, shame, and aspiration become political tools? |

### Stoler’s strongest Xinjiang insight

The state does not merely control Uyghurs as a population. It attempts to transform the **conditions under which Uyghur social reproduction happens**: language, piety, marriage norms, kinship, education, labour discipline, mobility, memory, and acceptable aspiration.

That is Stoler’s world: empire inside the household.

## 4\. Combined reading of Xinjiang

| Layer | Wolfe | Stoler |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Territory | Frontier converted into state space | Frontier made legible through files, maps, statistics, security categories |
| Population | Indigenous sovereignty reduced to minority status | Minority identity regulated through intimacy, pedagogy, affect |
| Settlement | Demographic and infrastructural consolidation | Settler/state common sense framed as development and stability |
| Elimination | Cultural/political neutralisation of native claims | Domestic, religious, and linguistic remaking of subjectivity |
| Present | Settler structure persists | Duress persists through surveillance, suspicion, and “stability” discourse |

**Judgment:** Xinjiang is one of the strongest cases for combining Wolfe and Stoler. Wolfe explains why land, settlement, and sovereignty matter. Stoler explains why the family, school, archive, religious life, and affect are not peripheral but central.

## IV. Applying Stoler and Wolfe to Tibet

## 1\. Historical baseline

Tibet has a distinct imperial history and was a major Central Asian power in the 7th–9th centuries. Britannica notes that Tibet functioned as a de facto independent government after the fall of the Qing until 1951, and that its incorporation into the PRC began in 1950 and remains highly controversial.

This baseline matters because the Tibet question is not merely ethnic minority policy. It concerns sovereignty, religion, territory, and competing historical claims.

## 2\. Wolfean reading of Tibet

A Wolfean reading asks whether Chinese rule in Tibet functions through settler-colonial elimination: not necessarily mass killing, but the reduction of Tibetan sovereignty, religious authority, and land-based life into a governable minority status.

Existing scholarship explicitly applies settler colonial theory to Tibet, including analyses of Chinese settler colonialism and government control.

### Wolfean elements in Tibet

| Wolfean dimension | Tibet case |
| --- | --- |
| Land | Plateau as strategic military, ecological, mineral, and hydrological space |
| Sovereignty | Tibetan political-religious authority displaced by PRC sovereignty |
| Elimination | Not simply physical removal; also institutional transformation of monasteries, language, education, and political memory |
| Positive construction | Infrastructure, towns, roads, railways, administrative integration, tourism, resource extraction |
| Native persistence | Tibetan religion, exile institutions, memory, language, and resistance continue to frustrate full incorporation |

### Specific Wolfean point

Tibet is not only an ethnic issue. In Wolfean terms, the Tibetan problem is that Tibetans are not merely a “minority”; they are a people with a historical territorial-religious sovereignty. Settler-colonial logic attempts to convert that sovereignty into culture: acceptable as dance, costume, tourism, spirituality, heritage; unacceptable as jurisdiction, political memory, or territorial claim.

That conversion — **sovereignty into culture** — is a classic eliminatory manoeuvre.

## 3\. Stolerian reading of Tibet

Stoler is powerful in Tibet because Tibetan society is reproduced through institutions that are not reducible to land ownership: monastery, lineage, reincarnation, language, ritual geography, pilgrimage, education, and memory.

### Stolerian objects in Tibet

| Domain | Stolerian question |
| --- | --- |
| Monastic life | How is religious authority made governable? |
| Reincarnation | How does the state seek archival/bureaucratic control over charismatic succession? |
| Language | How does schooling reshape future Tibetan subjectivity? |
| Tourism | How is Tibetan culture aestheticised while political sovereignty is neutralised? |
| Family | How are households linked to education, labour, mobility, and loyalty? |
| Memory | Which histories are permitted as folklore, and which are criminalised? |
| Landscape | How do monasteries, mines, roads, and sacred mountains become sites of ruination? |

### Stoler’s strongest Tibet insight

The state does not only occupy Tibet territorially. It attempts to reorganise the **sensible world** in which Tibetans know Tibet: what counts as religion, superstition, separatism, heritage, development, backwardness, gratitude, and loyalty.

A Stolerian reading would pay close attention to the emotional grammar of governance: the demand that Tibetans appear grateful for liberation, development, infrastructure, and modern schooling.

## 4\. Combined reading of Tibet

| Layer | Wolfe | Stoler |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sovereignty | Tibetan political-religious authority replaced by state sovereignty | Tibetan authority translated into administrable religion/culture |
| Territory | Plateau incorporated into state security/resource space | Sacred landscape turned into heritage, tourism, infrastructure, extraction |
| Elimination | Neutralisation of Tibetan jurisdiction and political futurity | Remaking of memory, education, monastic life, affect |
| Resistance | Indigenous persistence interrupts settler closure | Archive, exile, ritual, language, and embodied memory preserve alternatives |

**Judgment:** Tibet is not a simple replica of Australia-style settler colonialism. But Wolfe’s “elimination” is highly relevant if understood as political-religious neutralisation, not only demographic replacement. Stoler is indispensable because Tibet’s governance is intensely intimate, archival, spiritual, pedagogical, and affective.

## V. Applying Stoler and Wolfe to Taiwan

## 1\. Historical baseline

Taiwan has multiple colonial layers: Indigenous Austronesian societies; Dutch and Spanish presences; Qing expansion and Han settlement; Japanese colonial rule from 1895 to 1945; and post-1945 Republic of China/KMT rule. Britannica notes that prior to the 1600s Taiwan was self-governing without a central ruling authority, then experienced Dutch colonisation, Qing control, Japanese acquisition in 1895 after the First Sino-Japanese War, and later ROC rule after 1945.

This makes Taiwan a layered case, not a single colonial relation.

## 2\. Wolfean reading of Taiwan

Taiwan is the clearest of the four cases for classical settler colonialism if the analytic focus is **Indigenous Taiwan**.

### Wolfean layers

| Period | Wolfean reading |
| --- | --- |
| Qing period | Han settler expansion into Indigenous lands; frontier pacification; conversion of Indigenous territories into taxable/agricultural space |
| Japanese period | Formal colonial state intensifies mapping, policing, resource extraction, Indigenous classification, and mountain control |
| KMT/ROC period | Mainland state-in-exile imposes sovereignty, martial law, Mandarinisation, land/state restructuring |
| Contemporary Taiwan | Democratic recognition of Indigenous peoples coexists with unresolved land, resource, and sovereignty issues |

A Wolfean reading should not reduce Taiwan to “China vs Japan vs KMT.” The deeper settler-colonial question is: **what happened to Indigenous territorial orders?**

### Wolfean Taiwan thesis

Taiwan’s dominant national narratives — Chinese recovery, Japanese modernisation, Taiwanese democracy — often marginalise the Indigenous question. Wolfe forces the issue back to land and sovereignty: who was eliminated as a land-holding political subject so that Han, Japanese, and later ROC state formations could claim Taiwan?

## 3\. Stolerian reading of Taiwan

Stoler helps explain how each regime produced different subject categories.

### Qing Taiwan

Qing governance classified people through frontier categories: “raw” and “cooked” Indigenous groups, Han settlers, taxable households, pacified communities, rebels, migrants. A Stolerian reading would focus on how the frontier archive made Indigenous difference legible and governable.

### Japanese Taiwan

Japanese colonial rule is highly Stolerian. It involved schooling, hygiene, household registration, police governance, language policy, bodily discipline, and the production of imperial subjects. Taiwan was ceded to Japan in 1895 by the Treaty of Shimonoseki and became a Japanese colony until 1945.

A Stolerian question would ask:

- How did Japan define Taiwanese as assimilable but subordinate?
- How did household registration and policing produce colonial legibility?
- How did hygiene, education, and infrastructure become signs of imperial benevolence?
- How did Indigenous peoples become ethnographic, administrative, and security objects?
- How did the empire regulate intimacy, marriage, naming, language, and loyalty?

### KMT/ROC Taiwan

After 1945, the ROC/KMT regime brought a different imperial-state logic: martial law, Mandarinisation, anti-communist mobilisation, mainlander/local distinction, and the remaking of Taiwan as “Free China.” AP’s recent historical timeline notes the KMT retreat to Taiwan in 1949 and martial law until 1987.

A Stolerian reading would treat martial-law Taiwan as an archive of suspicion: household registration, political files, language hierarchy, school curricula, surveillance, anti-communist affect, and loyalty rituals.

## 4\. Combined reading of Taiwan

| Layer | Wolfe | Stoler |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indigenous Taiwan | Settler replacement of Indigenous land orders | Classification of Indigenous peoples as savage, pacified, folkloric, assimilable |
| Japanese colony | Territorial/resource colonialism | Pedagogy, hygiene, household registration, imperial subject-making |
| KMT Taiwan | State-exile sovereignty and martial-law rule | Suspicion, Mandarinisation, archive, affective anti-communism |
| Contemporary issue | Indigenous land sovereignty remains unresolved | Democratic multicultural recognition may aestheticise difference without full restitution |

**Judgment:** Taiwan is best analysed as a **stacked colonial formation**. Wolfe is essential for Indigenous land. Stoler is essential for Japanese and KMT subject-making. Any account that discusses Taiwan only through China/Japan/US geopolitics erases the Indigenous settler-colonial layer.

## VI. Applying Stoler and Wolfe to Manchuria

## 1\. Historical baseline

Manchuria is unusually complex because it was both a homeland and a frontier. The Qing dynasty originated from Manchu power in the northeast; later, Manchuria became a site of Han migration, Russian and Japanese imperial rivalry, railway imperialism, resource extraction, and the Japanese puppet state of Manchukuo. Britannica notes that Japan occupied Manchuria in 1931 and created Manchukuo in 1932, installing the former Qing emperor Puyi while Japan rigidly controlled the state.

## 2\. Wolfean reading of Manchuria

Manchuria is not a simple Indigenous-settler binary. It has multiple layers:

| Layer | Wolfean relevance |
| --- | --- |
| Manchu homeland | Qing state formation emerges from the region, complicating “native” and “imperial” categories |
| Han migration | Large-scale demographic transformation weakens Manchu/Mongol/Tungusic territorial distinctiveness |
| Russian railway imperialism | Infrastructure as territorial penetration |
| Japanese Manchukuo | Strong settler-developmental colonial project |
| PRC Northeast | Industrial incorporation and national integration |

### Manchukuo as settler colonial project

Manchukuo is where Wolfe fits most strongly. Japan’s project involved puppet sovereignty, migration schemes, agrarian settlement, industrial extraction, military planning, and racialised imperial ideology. Japan presented Manchukuo as an independent multiethnic state, but it functioned as a controlled imperial base for expansion into Asia.

A Wolfean reading asks:

- Whose land was transformed into imperial settlement and resource space?
- How did puppet sovereignty erase Chinese, Manchu, Mongol, Korean, and other claims?
- How did Japanese migration convert military occupation into settler permanence?
- How did development rhetoric mask replacement and extraction?

## 3\. Stolerian reading of Manchuria

Manchukuo is extremely Stolerian because it was an ideological theatre of imperial modernity.

### Stolerian objects in Manchukuo

| Domain | Stolerian question |
| --- | --- |
| Puppet sovereignty | How did documents, ceremonies, flags, and titles manufacture legitimacy? |
| Race | How did “multiethnic harmony” conceal Japanese hierarchy? |
| Development | How did railways, cities, farms, and factories become moral evidence of benevolent empire? |
| Labour | How were bodies disciplined into imperial productivity? |
| Family settlement | How did Japanese settler households make occupation appear permanent? |
| Archive | How did Manchukuo produce records of itself as lawful, modern, and inevitable? |
| Affect | How were hope, order, anti-communism, racial harmony, and modernity mobilised? |

### Stoler’s strongest Manchuria insight

Manchukuo was not just conquered territory. It was a **staged imperial reality**. Its bureaucracies, architecture, ceremonies, racial slogans, maps, and development plans tried to make Japanese domination appear as post-imperial liberation and modern state-building.

This is Stoler’s “colonial common sense” in full theatrical form.

## 4\. Combined reading of Manchuria

| Layer | Wolfe | Stoler |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Japanese occupation | Military seizure and settler/resource project | Puppet legality, affective legitimacy, racial harmony discourse |
| Settlement | Japanese agrarian and urban colonisation | Family settlement as intimate imperial permanence |
| Development | Railways and industry as material colonisation | Development as moral alibi |
| Race | Hierarchical empire masked by multiethnic ideology | Racial categories made administratively and emotionally plausible |
| Ruination | War, extraction, displacement | Imperial debris in landscapes, memory, industry, archives |

**Judgment:** Manchuria/Manchukuo is one of the best cases for combining the two. Wolfe explains settlement, land, and puppet sovereignty; Stoler explains how Manchukuo made domination look like development, order, and multiethnic modernity.

## VII. Cross-Case Comparison

## 1\. Which theorist fits each case best?

| Case | Wolfe fit | Stoler fit | Best diagnosis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Xinjiang | High | Very high | Settler-borderland consolidation plus intimate securitised governance |
| Tibet | Medium-high | Very high | Territorial incorporation plus religious/affective/pedagogical remaking |
| Taiwan | Very high for Indigenous Taiwan; medium for geopolitical Taiwan | Very high | Layered settler colonialism and imperial subject-making |
| Manchuria | High for Manchukuo; mixed for longer history | Very high | Settler-developmental puppet empire plus staged colonial modernity |

## 2\. What Wolfe sees that Stoler may understate

Wolfe keeps forcing the analysis back to the brutal question:

> Who gets the land, and whose sovereignty must disappear for the new order to become normal?

Without Wolfe, analysis risks becoming too atmospheric: discourse, affect, archive, anxiety, memory. All matter, but settler colonialism ultimately builds a world on land taken from others.

## 3\. What Stoler sees that Wolfe may flatten

Stoler keeps forcing the analysis into the fine machinery of rule:

> How does domination enter children, households, records, desires, categories, schoolbooks, rituals, shame, gratitude, fear, and aspiration?

Without Stoler, analysis risks becoming too structural: land, settlers, natives, elimination. All matter, but colonialism works through intimate and epistemic labour.

## VIII. Synthetic Framework for These Four Cases

A good analytic sequence would be:

| Step | Question | Main theorist |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Was the region incorporated by conquest, treaty, annexation, migration, puppet state, or revolution? | Historical baseline |
| 2 | Was Indigenous/local sovereignty converted into minority status, culture, folklore, or administrative dependency? | Wolfe |
| 3 | Did state-backed settlement or demographic engineering alter the political meaning of land? | Wolfe |
| 4 | How were families, schools, religion, sexuality, and language governed? | Stoler |
| 5 | What did the archive classify as backward, dangerous, loyal, civilised, grateful, separatist, or modern? | Stoler |
| 6 | How did development, infrastructure, or security become colonial common sense? | Stoler + Wolfe |
| 7 | What remains as ruination or duress? | Stoler |
| 8 | What political remedy would follow: recognition, autonomy, land return, sovereignty, demilitarisation, archival repair, language rights, reparations? | Both |

## X. Bottom Line

**Stoler** gives you the grammar of **imperial intimacy**: how rule enters the family, school, archive, affect, language, religion, sexuality, and everyday common sense.

**Wolfe** gives you the grammar of **settler colonial structure**: how land, sovereignty, elimination, settlement, and replacement organise domination across time.

Applied to the four cases:

- **Xinjiang**: Wolfe explains land, settlement, and frontier consolidation; Stoler explains securitised intimacy, files, family, religion, and affect.
- **Tibet**: Wolfe explains the neutralisation of Tibetan sovereignty; Stoler explains the governance of religion, reincarnation, memory, language, and sacred landscape.
- **Taiwan**: Wolfe is essential for Indigenous dispossession; Stoler is essential for Qing, Japanese, and KMT subject-making.
- **Manchuria**: Wolfe explains Manchukuo as settler-developmental occupation; Stoler explains its staged legality, multiethnic ideology, and imperial modernity.

The strongest formulation is:

> **Wolfe tells us what colonial power must structurally achieve; Stoler tells us how it becomes administratively, emotionally, and intimately livable.**

**Confidence:** High on conceptual comparison; medium-high on case applications. The broad patterns are well supported, but each region requires careful periodisation to avoid forcing all cases into the same settler-colonial template.

## Suppressed Angles / Lenses

## 1\. Qing empire as Inner Asian empire, not merely proto-China

The previous analysis risked compressing Qing expansion into a straight line leading toward modern Chinese territorial nationalism. That is analytically convenient but historically crude.

The Qing was not simply a Han-Chinese nation-state in embryonic form. It was a **Manchu-led imperial formation** ruling through different logics across China proper, Mongolia, Tibet, Xinjiang, Manchuria, and frontier zones. It used **banners, protectorates, tribute, religious patronage, military colonies, multilingual administration, marriage diplomacy, Buddhist kingship, and differentiated legal regimes**.

This matters because applying Wolfe too quickly may misread Qing frontier rule as if it were already a modern settler-colonial nation-state. Some parts were settler-like, especially Han migration and military-agricultural colonies. But other parts were **imperial suzerainty, frontier management, aristocratic brokerage, military occupation, and ritual sovereignty**.

**Suppressed question:**  
Was Qing rule in Xinjiang, Tibet, and Manchuria best understood as settler colonialism, conquest empire, multiethnic imperial sovereignty, or a hybrid?

## 2\. Indigenous and local agency

The prior Stoler/Wolfe framing made local peoples appear mainly as objects of classification, elimination, assimilation, and surveillance. That is a weakness.

A fuller account needs to ask how local actors shaped outcomes:

| Region | Suppressed agency question |
| --- | --- |
| **Xinjiang** | How did Uyghur, Kazakh, Hui, Mongol, and other elites negotiate Qing, Republican, Soviet, and PRC power? |
| **Tibet** | How did monastic elites, aristocrats, local rulers, traders, and exile institutions shape political trajectories? |
| **Taiwan** | How did Indigenous groups, Han settlers, Japanese-trained elites, benshengren, waishengren, and democracy activists act differently? |
| **Manchuria** | How did Manchu, Han, Mongol, Korean, Russian, Japanese, and local warlord actors exploit imperial competition? |

Stoler can recover intimate governance, but she can still leave local actors trapped inside the colonial archive. Wolfe can clarify structural elimination, but he can make Indigenous peoples appear structurally acted upon rather than politically active.

**Suppressed question:**  
Where did local actors resist, bargain, collaborate, reinterpret, or strategically appropriate imperial categories?

## 3\. Political economy and infrastructure

The previous discussion mentioned resources and infrastructure but did not make them central enough.

For these regions, political economy is not background. It is often the engine.

| Region | Political-economic core |
| --- | --- |
| **Xinjiang** | Cotton, oil, gas, minerals, solar, logistics corridors, border trade, production-construction systems |
| **Tibet** | Water, hydropower, minerals, tourism, transport infrastructure, ecological security |
| **Taiwan** | Sugar, camphor, rice, tea, industrialisation, ports, Japanese colonial extraction, postwar developmental state |
| **Manchuria** | Railways, coal, iron, soybeans, heavy industry, Japanese imperial planning, Soviet seizure, PRC industrial base |

A Stolerian account may over-focus on discourse, affect, and archive unless tethered to extraction. A Wolfean account may say “land” but under-specify **what kind of land**: agricultural, mineral, sacred, strategic, infrastructural, ecological, or military.

**Suppressed question:**  
Which material systems made these borderlands valuable enough to govern so intensively?

## 4\. Military and security history

The previous analysis underplayed war.

These are not merely cultural or administrative borderlands. They are militarised zones shaped by conquest, rebellion, garrisons, counterinsurgency, invasion, Cold War alignments, and strategic depth.

| Region | Military lens |
| --- | --- |
| **Xinjiang** | Qing conquest, Muslim rebellions, Soviet influence, PRC security state, border with Central Asia |
| **Tibet** | PLA entry, border war context, India-China frontier, internal security, exile politics |
| **Taiwan** | Japanese military colony, KMT retreat, White Terror, US security umbrella, cross-strait militarisation |
| **Manchuria** | Russo-Japanese War, Kwantung Army, Manchukuo, Soviet invasion, Chinese Civil War, Korean War hinterland |

Wolfe can handle structural occupation, but he is less fine-grained on military institutions. Stoler can analyse security discourse, but she may underweight strategic calculations.

**Suppressed question:**  
How did military necessity, frontier anxiety, and strategic geography shape incorporation?

---

## 5\. Legal pluralism and jurisdictional layering

The prior analysis used sovereignty as a large concept but did not unpack the legal mechanics enough.

Empires often govern through **layered jurisdiction**, not simple domination. Different populations may be subject to different land law, marriage law, religious authority, mobility rules, language policy, taxation systems, and courts.

This is especially important for Qing and Japanese imperial contexts.

| Lens | What to inspect |
| --- | --- |
| Land law | Who can own, inherit, lease, survey, or alienate land? |
| Family law | Whose marriages, adoptions, and descent lines are legally recognised? |
| Religious law | Are monasteries, mosques, temples, or ritual authorities autonomous or state-regulated? |
| Citizenship/subjecthood | Who is a subject, citizen, native, minority, settler, imperial subject, or alien? |
| Mobility law | Who may move, settle, trade, cross borders, or return? |

**Suppressed question:**  
What specific legal transformations converted frontier peoples into administrable populations?

## 6\. Nationalism and state-formation theory

The earlier discussion used Stoler and Wolfe but did not adequately bring in nationalism theorists.

That matters because **Xinjiang, Tibet, Taiwan, and Manchuria are not only colonial cases**. They are also cases of **nation-state formation, minority classification, border consolidation, and imagined community-making**.

Relevant theorists:

| Theorist | What they add |
| --- | --- |
| **Benedict Anderson** | Print capitalism, official nationalism, census-map-museum, imagined communities |
| **Rogers Brubaker** | Nationalising states, national minorities, external homelands, triadic nexus |
| **Ernest Gellner** | Industrial modernity, schooling, high culture, nation-state homogenisation |
| **Eric Hobsbawm** | Invented traditions, mass politics, state ritual |
| **Miroslav Hroch** | National movements through elite, activist, mass phases |

**Suppressed question:**  
When are we seeing colonial domination, and when are we seeing nationalising state formation? When are they fused?

## 7\. Chinese, Japanese, Russian, and Soviet imperial comparison

The prior discussion treated the cases mainly through Qing/PRC and Japanese empire, but did not sufficiently compare **multiple imperial grammars**.

Manchuria especially cannot be understood without Russian and Soviet factors. Xinjiang also requires Soviet/Central Asian comparison. Taiwan requires Japan and the US. Tibet requires British India, India, the PRC, and exile politics.

| Region | Underweighted imperial comparator |
| --- | --- |
| **Xinjiang** | Russian/Soviet Central Asia |
| **Tibet** | British India, India, Nepal, Mongol-Tibetan Buddhist networks |
| **Taiwan** | Japan, US empire/security order, Austronesian Pacific |
| **Manchuria** | Russia, Soviet Union, Japan, Korea |

**Suppressed question:**  
How did competing empires shape local possibilities rather than a single coloniser-colonised dyad?

## 8\. Religion as political infrastructure

Religion appeared in the Tibet and Xinjiang sections, but mostly as something governed. That is too thin.

Religion is not merely identity, belief, or culture. In these regions it can be:

- legal authority
- educational system
- transregional network
- landholding institution
- calendar and ritual order
- memory system
- diplomatic language
- legitimacy structure
- alternative sovereignty

This matters most in Tibet, Xinjiang, and Qing Inner Asia.

**Suppressed question:**  
How did Buddhist, Islamic, shamanic, Confucian, and imperial ritual systems function as political infrastructures?

Stoler gave us sexuality, family, domesticity, and reproduction, but the previous discussion did not push gender far enough.

A deeper gender lens would ask:

**Suppressed question:**  
How did gendered governance produce colonial modernity, not merely reflect it?

## 10\. Environmental and ecological history

Land was treated mainly as sovereignty. That is Wolfean, but incomplete.

These regions are ecological frontiers:

| Region | Ecological lens |
| --- | --- |
| **Xinjiang** | Oasis ecology, desertification, cotton irrigation, water politics, arid logistics |
| **Tibet** | Plateau ecology, rivers, glaciers, mining, hydropower, climate security |
| **Taiwan** | Mountain forests, typhoons, Indigenous hunting territories, camphor forests, Japanese forestry |
| **Manchuria** | Forests, steppe, soy agriculture, coal, iron, industrial pollution |

Environmental history would show that imperial power often works through **ecological conversion**: forest to timber reserve, steppe to pasture, oasis to cotton, sacred mountain to tourist site, river to hydropower asset.

**Suppressed question:**  
How did ecological transformation make imperial sovereignty materially real?

## 11\. Affect from below

The previous discussion used Stoler’s affect mostly from the viewpoint of imperial governance: anxiety, suspicion, gratitude, loyalty, fear.

But affect also exists from below:

- resentment
- humiliation
- nostalgia
- religious longing
- exile grief
- frontier pride
- settler insecurity
- Indigenous mourning
- revolutionary hope
- postcolonial ambivalence
- collaborationist aspiration

**Suppressed question:**  
What emotional worlds did local populations produce, preserve, or weaponise against imperial categories?

## 12\. Historiographic controversy and source politics

The previous discussion presented frameworks, but did not sufficiently mark the historiographic contest.

These topics are politically charged. Terms like **colonialism**, **settler colonialism**, **genocide**, **assimilation**, **liberation**, **frontier**, **minority**, **autonomy**, and **sovereignty** are not neutral descriptors. They are part of the political battlefield.

A more disciplined version would separate:

| Category | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Established historical fact | Japanese rule over Taiwan from 1895–1945 |
| Strong scholarly interpretation | Taiwan involved settler-colonial dispossession of Indigenous peoples |
| Contested interpretation | Whether PRC rule in Tibet/Xinjiang is best classified as settler colonialism |
| Political claim | Whether sovereignty should be restored, devolved, recognised, or reinterpreted |
| State narrative | Liberation, development, unity, anti-separatism |
| Counter-narrative | Occupation, colonisation, cultural destruction, Indigenous sovereignty |

**Suppressed question:**  
Who benefits from each naming convention?

## Most Important Missing Lenses by Region

## Xinjiang

The missing lenses are **Soviet/Central Asian comparison, Islam as institutional order, political economy of cotton/oil/corridors, and security-state technocracy**.

The earlier Stoler/Wolfe reading was strong but too generic. Xinjiang needs a **triangular frame**: Qing frontier + Soviet-style nationality governance + PRC security-development state.

## Tibet

The missing lenses are **Buddhist sovereignty, sacred geography, monastic economy, exile diplomacy, and India-China geopolitics**.

The prior analysis correctly identified religion and sovereignty but did not fully treat Tibetan Buddhism as a political infrastructure rather than only a governed culture.

## Taiwan

The missing lenses are **Indigenous sovereignty, Japanese colonial developmentalism, KMT authoritarian nationalism, US security order, and Taiwanese democratic subject-formation**.

The key risk is compressing Taiwan into “settler colonialism” without separating **Indigenous Taiwan**, **Japanese Taiwan**, **KMT Taiwan**, and **democratic Taiwan**.

## Manchuria

The missing lenses are **Manchu homeland politics, Russian/Soviet imperialism, railway capitalism, Korean migration, warlordism, and industrial modernity**.

Manchuria is the easiest case to distort if treated only as Japanese settler colonialism. It is a multi-imperial hinge zone.
