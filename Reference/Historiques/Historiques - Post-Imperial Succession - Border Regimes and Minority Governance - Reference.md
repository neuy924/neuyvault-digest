# From Imperial Frontier to Classified Nationhood: Post-Qing and Post-Tsarist Border Regimes, Minority Governance, and Majority Formation

Abstract (English)

This discussion develops a comparative framework for analyzing how Chinese and Russian successor regimes managed the territorial, demographic, and conceptual afterlives of empire. It argues that post-Qing and post-Tsarist states confronted a similar structural problem—how to preserve large, heterogeneous imperial spaces after dynastic collapse—but solved it through different institutional logics. Chinese successor regimes, especially from the Republic of China to the People’s Republic of China, largely sought to preserve the territorial shell of the Qing empire within a unitary state form, recognizing minorities while denying them constituent sovereignty. Soviet regimes, by contrast, rebuilt much of the former Romanov space through an ethnofederal architecture that classified, territorialized, and politically institutionalized nationality, even as real power remained centralized. This difference shaped not only border management and frontier incorporation but also the long-term durability of territorial order.

The discussion also examines Chinese minzu and Soviet natsional’nost’ as distinct but related classification regimes. Minzu is treated primarily as a system of official group recognition within a bounded multiethnic state, while natsional’nost’ is analyzed as a regime of personal ethnonational inscription embedded in a territorialized federal order. From this comparison follows a further distinction in majority formation: Han emerged as the integrative majority of a unitary multiethnic state, whereas Russians occupied the more contradictory position of dominant nationality within a formally multinational empire-federation. Reframed through Rogers Brubaker’s concepts of the nationalizing state, national minority, external homeland, and the critique of “groupism,” the discussion argues that Hanification and Russification should not be collapsed into the same process. Hanification is best understood as majority-centered incorporation within a nationalizing state that constrains alternative sovereignty, whereas Russification is better read as an imperial-majority project unfolding across territorially differentiated and often triadically entangled borderlands.

**Preamble**

The cleanest contrast is this: after the Qing fell in 1911, Chinese elites largely argued that the **empire’s territory should survive intact even if the dynasty did not**. After the Romanovs fell in 1917, the Bolsheviks rebuilt much of the old imperial space by a different formula: **formal ethno-territorial federalism under real party centralism**. Both projects were anti-separatist in practice. But they differed sharply in how they named territory, drew internal borders, and incorporated peripheral peoples.

**Topic map**

This comparison works best across five axes:

1. **Imperial inheritance**: what survives the old empire?
2. **Border logic**: fixed outer borders or layered frontier zones?
3. **Internal territorial design**: unitary inheritance or ethnofederal partition?
4. **Minority governance**: assimilation, autonomy, or controlled nation-building?
5. **Long-run consequence**: what kind of breakup pressure did each model create?

## Deep analysis

### 1) Basic post-imperial formula

**Post-Qing China:**
The dominant Republican formula was not “let the borderlands go,” but “the republic inherits the Qing realm.” “Five Races Under One Union” tried to solve the transition from dynastic empire to nation-state by redefining Mongols, Tibetans, Hui/Muslims, Manchus, and Han as co-owners of one republic rather than separate sovereign peoples. In other words, the state changed its legitimacy principle, but sought continuity of imperial space. Republican elites repeatedly treated Mongolia, Tibet, and the Muslim northwest as territories of the new republic, even when actual control was weak. ([JSTOR][1])

**Post-Tsarist/Soviet space:**
The Bolsheviks did something structurally different. They rejected old Russian imperial nationalism rhetorically, recognized “nationalities” as politically real, and then organized much of the former empire into **union republics, autonomous republics, oblasts, and districts**. That is, they preserved empire by **territorializing nationality**. In Central Asia especially, Moscow sponsored national-territorial delimitation rather than simply inheriting tsarist provinces unchanged. ([JSTOR][2])

**Established knowledge:** both successor regimes sought to keep large imperial spaces together.
**Key difference:** China preserved imperial territory through a **single state claiming indivisible succession**; the USSR preserved imperial territory through a **federation of named nations**.

### 2) Territory and external borders

**China after Qing:**
Post-Qing territorial management was marked by **claim continuity, control discontinuity**. The Republic claimed nearly all former Qing lands, but its actual reach across Tibet, Outer Mongolia, Xinjiang, and parts of Inner Asia was uneven and often fragile. In the 1910s–30s, many frontiers were not tightly policed by modern customs and border-defense institutions; the state often had more cartographic ambition than administrative capacity. Outer Mongolia is the clearest case: it declared independence in 1911/12; Russia treated it as autonomous under Chinese suzerainty; China intermittently reasserted claims, but eventually the ROC recognized Mongolian independence only in 1946 under altered geopolitical conditions. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][3])

**Post-Tsarist/Soviet:**
The Soviet regime moved much faster toward **harder, bureaucratically legible borders**, both external and internal. The outer border of the USSR became a state-security frontier. Internally, nationality-based units were delimited, mapped, classified, and nested into a hierarchy. This did not make borders natural or uncontested. It made them **administratively consequential**. Soviet border-making was not merely defensive; it was also productive. It manufactured political territories that later became republics and eventually independent states. ([JSTOR][2])

**Contrast:**
China’s early post-Qing frontier was often a **zone of sovereignty claims**; the Soviet frontier became a **grid of security administration plus ethnopolitical territorialization**.

### 3) Internal border design

**China:**
The Republican and later PRC model did create autonomous regions and minority institutions, but not a genuinely federal system of coequal national homelands. The basic principle was always **autonomy under unified state leadership**, not constituent sovereignty. In effect, China converted a Qing imperial geography into a modern state geography without granting frontier peoples a constitutional route to separate statehood. ([China Embassy in Ireland][4])

**USSR:**
The Soviet Union institutionalized nationality more deeply. Union republics had constitutions, titular peoples, elites, languages, and at least formal indicators of sovereignty; later Soviet constitutions even preserved a formal right of secession, though in practice the system was tightly centralized and coercive. This contradiction mattered enormously in 1989–91: the state had spent decades building national-territorial containers that later served as launchpads for exit. ([encyclopediaofukraine.com][5])

**Core contrast:**
China: **ethnic autonomy without constituent sovereignty**.
USSR: **constituent sovereignty on paper, central domination in practice**.

That is probably the single most important difference.

### 4) Management of peripheral minorities

**China’s post-Qing pattern:**
Chinese successor regimes generally moved toward a vocabulary of **integration**. The rhetoric differed across time—Republican “Five Races,” later PRC “regional ethnic autonomy,” and more recently stronger “national unity” language—but the directional logic was stable: minorities were recognized, categorized, and sometimes institutionally accommodated, yet always inside a non-negotiable territorial whole. The center did not seek to turn Tibetans, Mongols, Uyghurs, or others into separate state-bearing nations. It sought to turn them into recognized components of a larger Chinese polity. ([Springer][6])

**Soviet pattern:**
The early Bolshevik regime did almost the opposite in form. Through **korenizatsiia** it promoted local cadres, local languages, local schools, and titular-national representation in the republics and autonomous units. This was not liberal pluralism. It was a state project to create governable socialist nations that would remain loyal to Moscow. By the 1930s, however, much of this gave way to purges, suspicion of “bourgeois nationalism,” deportations, and stronger Russifying pressures. So the Soviet model was double-edged: it empowered minority national forms and then repeatedly repressed them. ([lib.uchicago.edu][7])

**Comparison in one sentence:**
China mostly preferred **controlled cultural recognition plus territorial indivisibility**; the USSR pursued **controlled nation-building plus imperial centralism**.

### 5) Religion and the periphery

This is a major asymmetry.

**Post-Qing China:**
On many peripheries, religion was inseparable from territorial politics—Tibetan Buddhism in Tibet and Mongolia, Islam in Xinjiang and parts of the northwest. Chinese regimes often saw frontier religion not merely as faith but as a political connector linking populations across borders. That made monasteries, clerical networks, and pilgrimage routes matters of state security as well as culture. ([Open Research Europe][8])

**USSR:**
The Soviet regime was more radically transformative and often more destructive toward religion as such. It did not merely seek to contain transborder religious authority; it sought to subordinate or break religious institutions within an explicitly atheist state. In Muslim Central Asia, Buddhist zones, and elsewhere, this meant aggressive intervention in clerical, educational, and communal life. Minority management was therefore fused not only with nationality policy but with anti-religious state building. ([lib.uchicago.edu][7])

**Implication:**
Both states securitized peripheral religion. The Soviet state did so within a broader anti-religious revolutionary project; China more often framed it within territorial integration and sovereignty defense.

### 6) Capacity versus ideology

A second major difference is temporal.

**Early post-Qing China** often lacked the coercive-administrative capacity to fully transform frontiers, especially in the Republican period. Hence a frequent gap between **claim** and **reach**.
**The Soviet state**, after civil war consolidation, generally had greater capacity to redraw administrations, classify peoples, create elites, and enforce security regimes across vast territories. ([JSTOR][9])

This means the Soviet project was usually **more interventionist and socially engineering**, while the Chinese project—at least before 1949—was often **more aspirational, negotiated, and uneven**. After 1949, the PRC became far more capable, and the gap narrowed somewhat, but the institutional logic still differed.

### 7) Long-run outcome

Here the historical payoff is stark.

**China’s model** reduced the constitutional pathways by which peripheral minorities could transform recognized nationality into internationally legible statehood. That did not eliminate resistance or separatist sentiment, but it meant the state did not itself build a ladder from minority recognition to sovereign republichood. ([China Embassy in Ireland][4])

**The Soviet model** did build such ladders. Even though the republics were tightly controlled, the USSR left behind territorial units, titular elites, official languages, political mythologies, and constitutional forms that later became the infrastructure of decolonization and state breakup. In that sense, Soviet success at organizing nationality also helped script Soviet dissolution. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][10])

## Interpretation

The two post-imperial projects were not opposites; they were rival solutions to the same problem: **how to preserve a continental empire after dynastic legitimacy has collapsed**.

China’s answer was: **the state is one, history is continuous, and diversity is nested inside territorial unity**.
The Soviet answer was: **nations are real, so the empire must be rebuilt through them—but under one party, one center, one security apparatus**.

One could put it even more sharply:

* **Post-Qing China de-imperialized legitimacy more than space.**
* **The USSR de-imperialized language more than power.**

That is the cleanest compare-and-contrast.

## Brief red-team review

A weakness in this comparison is that “post-Qing” spans both the weak ROC and the much stronger PRC, while “post-tsarist” often collapses Leninist and Stalinist phases. That can blur important chronological shifts. The early ROC was far less capable than the mature Soviet state; the early Soviet 1920s were more pluralizing than the later Stalinist 1930s. The comparison is strongest when treated as **two evolving state traditions**, not two static models.

## Known unknowns

The answer changes depending on whether your real comparison target is:

1. **ROC vs early USSR**,
2. **PRC vs USSR**, or
3. **Long Chinese successor-state logic vs long Soviet logic**.

It also changes by frontier: Tibet, Xinjiang, and Mongolia do not behave the same way; nor do Ukraine, Central Asia, and the Caucasus.

## Reading the matrix

Three patterns stand out.

### 1) **China inherited first, institutionalized later**

Across **Mongolia, Tibet, Xinjiang, and Manchuria**, the initial post-Qing instinct was to preserve the Qing territorial shell. The problem was not lack of claim, but lack of consistent control. Early Republican China often governed frontiers through a mix of symbolism, local strongmen, and partial bargains, while insisting that the space remained non-negotiably Chinese. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][1])

### 2) **The USSR territorialized nationality far more aggressively**

Across **Central Asia, the Caucasus, the Baltic, and Ukraine**, the Soviet regime made nationality into an administrative principle. It drew republics and autonomous units, cultivated titular elites, and embedded national categories into the state itself. This strengthened control in one sense, but also created ready-made containers for later exit. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][6])

### 3) **Minority governance differed in constitutional depth**

Chinese minority governance generally stopped at **autonomy without constituent sovereignty**. Soviet minority governance often went further, granting at least formal republican or autonomous territorial status to named peoples. The Soviet system therefore generated more durable institutional nationalism at the periphery than the Chinese system usually allowed. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][5])

## Condensed takeaway

If reduced to a single formula:

* **Post-Qing frontier management:** preserve the imperial map; negotiate, recover, or consolidate control; recognize minorities, but deny them a constitutional road to separate statehood.
* **Post-Tsarist/Soviet frontier management:** reassemble empire through national territories; empower local nationhood in form; centralize power in practice; later pay the price when those forms become vehicles of disintegration. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][1])

**Confidence:** 0.89 for the structural comparison; lower for one-to-one frontier pairing, because Tibet/Baltic and Manchuria/Ukraine are analogical rather than direct matches.

1. **ROC (1912–1949):** high territorial claim, uneven frontier control.

## 1) ROC versus PRC

### ROC: territorial succession without full territorial command

The Republic of China largely claimed the **Qing imperial inheritance intact**, but effective control over frontier regions was often partial, mediated, or episodic. Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang were not treated as legitimately lost nations; they were treated as parts of the successor polity whose control had to be reasserted, negotiated, or at minimum symbolically maintained. The problem was that the state’s **capacity** rarely matched its territorial doctrine. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][4])

This is why the ROC frontier was often characterized by:

* sovereignty claims with weak administrative penetration,
* reliance on provincial or military intermediaries,
* porous borders,
* foreign encroachment and buffer politics,
* unresolved status conflicts rather than final constitutional settlement. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][4])

So the ROC model was not a coherent minority constitution. It was closer to **imperial inheritance under state weakness**.

### PRC: incorporation with stronger administrative depth

The PRC inherited much of the same territorial doctrine, but with much greater coercive, logistical, and administrative capacity. Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia were institutionalized as autonomous regions within the PRC framework, but these were not Soviet-style constituent republics. They were subordinate units inside a **unitary state**, not co-founding nations with meaningful constitutional exit options. Xinjiang is formally an autonomous region; Inner Mongolia is described as a testing ground for integrating Han and Mongols into a single political entity. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][1])

That is the major Chinese internal shift:

* **ROC:** frontier possession was often aspirational or negotiated.
* **PRC:** frontier possession became far more bureaucratically and militarily enforceable. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][1])

### Frontier implications

**Mongolia:** the ROC kept the claim but could not restore durable rule over Outer Mongolia; the PRC instead accepted the postwar geopolitical settlement rather than attempting restoration. The major rupture therefore belongs more to the ROC era than the PRC era. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][4])

**Tibet:** the ROC inherited the claim amid thin control; the PRC moved toward actual incorporation and sustained administrative integration. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][5])

**Xinjiang:** the ROC period was marked by mediated control and foreign entanglement; the PRC period by formal autonomous-region status combined with stronger central penetration. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][1])

**Manchuria:** under the ROC, this was the frontier whose vulnerability to external seizure was most visibly catastrophic; under the PRC it became fully reintegrated core industrial territory rather than an unstable imperial marches. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][6])

## 2) Lenin versus Stalin

### Lenin: nationality as a route to holding empire together

Leninist nationalities policy was not simple pluralism. It was a strategic answer to imperial collapse. The idea was that nations were politically real and could not just be dissolved by Russian centralism, so the new regime had to recognize them, cultivate local elites, and organize territory in ways that made Soviet rule look post-imperial rather than Great Russian. Britannica’s overview notes that Lenin’s policy assumed nations would choose to remain in close relationship with Russia, though many sought more independent paths. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][2])

This produced a frontier logic unlike the ROC or PRC:

* nationality was **institutionalized**, not merely acknowledged;
* local cadres and cultures were promoted in important settings;
* the federation was meant to secure empire by **repackaging** it. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][2])

So Lenin’s method was: **build loyalty by recognizing nationhood under socialist hegemony**.

### Stalin: from managed multinationalism to securitized centralism

Under Stalin, that earlier accommodation narrowed sharply. Britannica notes that Stalin’s nationality policy initially promoted native cadres and cultures but changed in the late 1920s, as non-Russian populations were increasingly viewed through a security lens. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][2])

The most decisive shift was that nationality categories remained on the map, but the regime became willing to:

* punish entire peoples,
* abolish autonomous territorial arrangements,
* deport large populations,
* subordinate local elites to centrally driven security priorities. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][3])

Britannica states that more than 50 nationalities, totaling about 3.5 million people, were deported under Stalin on security grounds, while wartime deportations involved entire peoples such as the Chechens, Ingush, Kalmyks, Crimean Tatars, and Volga Germans. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][3])

So Stalin’s method was: **retain the ethnofederal shell, but increasingly govern the periphery as a security problem**. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][3])

## 3) The sharper cross-comparison

### ROC and Lenin are not opposites, but they solve different immediate crises

* The **ROC** faced dynastic collapse and fragmentation with too little institutional reach; it therefore leaned on legal-historical inheritance of Qing space. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][4])
* **Lenin** faced imperial collapse plus civil war and responded by institutionalizing nationality inside a multinational revolutionary state. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][2])

Thus:

* ROC frontier rule = **claim first, govern later if possible**.
* Leninist frontier rule = **name, territorialize, and co-opt nations into the new state**. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][2])

### PRC and Stalin are closer in coercive capacity, but not in constitutional form

Both the **PRC** and **Stalinist USSR** had much stronger state capacity than the ROC or early Bolshevik regime. Both could penetrate frontiers, impose administrative order, and treat borderlands as security zones. But they still diverged structurally:

* the **PRC** did **not** create union-republic-style nation-states inside the polity; its autonomous regions remained subordinate units in a unitary framework. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][1])
* **Stalin’s USSR** preserved a more constitutionally differentiated, nationality-based territorial architecture even while coercively overriding it in practice. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][3])

That is why the Soviet system later generated stronger secession-capable national containers than the Chinese system did.

## Preamble

They overlap, but they are not the same thing.

**Chinese *minzu* (民族)** and **Soviet *natsional’nost’* (национальность)** were both state-backed ways of sorting populations into durable collective categories. Both drew on a modernist mix of ethnography, language, territory, and political utility. Both hardened fluid identities into administratively legible groups. But they operated at different levels of the state and produced different political effects. In blunt terms:

* **PRC *minzu*** was a regime for defining the population into a finite set of officially recognized groups inside one state.
* **Soviet *natsional’nost’*** was a regime for assigning each person an ethnic-national identity while also mapping many of those identities onto territorial units of the union. ([history.stanford.edu][1])

## Topic map

The main contrasts sit on six axes:

1. **What is being classified?**
2. **How fixed is the category?**
3. **What evidence counts?**
4. **How tightly is category tied to territory?**
5. **Where does the category live institutionally?**
6. **What political future does the category imply?**

## Deep analysis

### 1) Object of classification

In the PRC, *minzu* classification was primarily about deciding **which groups exist** as officially recognized nationalities/ethnic groups within the state. The major PRC project was *minzu shibie* (ethnic classification), especially from the 1950s, which reduced a far more heterogeneous social field into the official framework of **56 minzu**. That is a group-making regime before it is an individual-label regime. ([history.stanford.edu][1])

In the Soviet case, *natsional’nost’* referred to a person’s **ethnic-national belonging**, distinct from citizenship (*grazhdanstvo*). The key point is that in Soviet usage, *natsional’nost’* was not mainly one’s state membership; it was one’s ethnonational identity. This is why it could appear as an entry in identity documents and later become infamous as the “fifth line” in internal passports. ([New Left Review][2])

**Core difference:**

* *Minzu* asks: **what recognized peoples exist in China?**
* *Natsional’nost’* asks: **what ethnonational people does this individual belong to?**

### 2) Enumeration versus inscription

The PRC regime was heavily built around **enumeration and recognition**. The state’s problem was to collapse thousands of local names, lineages, speech communities, and upland/lowland distinctions into a manageable national schema. That meant combining ethnographic investigation with political standardization. The result was not neutral description; scholars explicitly characterize *minzu shibie* as a form of social engineering. ([Sage Journals][3])

The Soviet regime was more directly about **inscription onto persons**, though of course it also involved broader nation-making. Once *natsional’nost’* became fixed in internal documents, it reified ethnic identity in everyday administration, education, employment, and discrimination. Soviet nationality was not only counted; it was routinely **carried by the individual** in bureaucratic life. ([Peace Research Institute Oslo][4])

So the PRC’s classificatory drama is most visible at the level of **recognizing groups**. The Soviet drama is most visible at the level of **assigning persons**.

### 3) Criteria of classification

Both regimes used a mixture of criteria rather than a single essence. On the Chinese side, *minzu* classification drew on Stalin-influenced definitions of nationality, but in practice it was improvised through combinations of language, territory, historical memory, elite claims, self-identification, and administrative feasibility. The process was negotiated, contingent, and often messy rather than deductively scientific. ([history.stanford.edu][1])

The Soviet regime also did not rest on a pure linguistic or biological rule. *Natsional’nost’* linked descent, culture, historical community, and often territory, but the state increasingly treated the category as stable and documentable. Earlier fluidity gave way to harder ascription. As one Cambridge source notes, fixation in the internal passport reified what had been more fluid identification. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

**Interpretation:** both regimes were modern state taxonomies pretending to discover communities while actually helping produce them.

### 4) Relation to territory

This is the sharpest structural difference.

In the PRC, *minzu* classification was tied to territory through autonomous regions, prefectures, and counties, but the state did **not** generally turn recognized *minzu* into coequal state-bearing nations. Official recognition did not imply constituent sovereignty. The endpoint was inclusion inside a single multiethnic Chinese nation-state, not a federation of national homelands. ([history.stanford.edu][1])

In the Soviet Union, *natsional’nost’* connected much more deeply to an architecture of **ethnoterritorial federalism**. Many national categories corresponded to republics, autonomous republics, oblasts, or districts. Even when this system was coercively managed, it still embedded nationality in territorial state form. That is why Soviet classification had stronger long-run state-building and state-breaking consequences. ([New Left Review][2])

**Bluntly:**

* PRC *minzu* helped define diversity **inside one state**.
* Soviet *natsional’nost’* helped define persons **and** scaffold national territories inside a federal empire.

### 5) Fixity and mobility

PRC *minzu* could be contested and revised, especially in the earlier decades of classification, but the major political move was to settle populations into a closed list of officially recognized groups. That produces a bounded pluralism: recognition exists, but outside the recognized list there is limited room for new nationally meaningful categories to emerge. ([history.stanford.edu][1])

Soviet *natsional’nost’* was often more rigid once inscribed, because it became a durable administrative marker on the person. That rigidity mattered not just symbolically but materially, since it could shape educational, occupational, and political treatment. The famous “fifth point” encapsulates that hardening. ([Peace Research Institute Oslo][4])

So both regimes froze identities, but in different ways:

* PRC: **freeze the menu of recognized groups**.
* USSR: **freeze the individual’s assigned ethnonational belonging**.

### 6) Majority formation

A neglected point: both regimes also made the majority.

In China, classification did not only define minorities; it also consolidated the category **Han** as the majority *minzu*, the core around which the 56-group model is organized. The official pluralism is therefore asymmetrical from the start. ([uw.manifoldapp.org][6])

In the Soviet system, *natsional’nost’* likewise sorted Russians as one nationality among many, but the institutional effects differed because Soviet federalism formally distributed nationality across a multinational union rather than a single majority-framed national state. Even so, Russian predominance remained politically weighty. ([New Left Review][2])

### 7) Political function

The PRC classification regime’s main function was **controlled incorporation**: render diversity legible, governable, and representable without allowing it to become a sovereign alternative. Official recognition and minority rights discourse therefore coexist with a strong ceiling on territorial self-determination. ([history.stanford.edu][1])

The Soviet regime’s main function was more contradictory: it sought to **build socialism through nations**. That meant empowering nationality enough to mobilize and administer populations, but not enough to escape party control. The contradiction was structural: by institutionalizing national categories so deeply, the Soviet state also strengthened the infrastructure later used in post-Soviet secession and nation-state formation. ([New Left Review][2])

## Preamble

This matters because classification regimes do not just “identify minorities.” They also **manufacture the majority**.

The PRC and USSR both presented themselves as multiethnic polities, but their classificatory systems produced very different kinds of dominant group. In China, classification helped consolidate **Han** as the default civilizational-political center within a finite 56-*minzu* schema. In the USSR, classification made **Russians** one nationality among many inside a formally multinational federation, but one that was still disproportionately powerful demographically, linguistically, and institutionally. The result is an asymmetry: **Han became the state-centered majority of a unitary nation-state; Russians became the dominant nationality of a multinational imperial federation.** ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

## Deep analysis

### 1) Majority formation is not a demographic fact alone

Neither “Han” nor “Russians” should be treated as merely pre-political demographic blocs. In both cases, the state’s classificatory apparatus helped stabilize who counted as the majority and how that majority related to the rest of the polity. In the Soviet case, censuses and official nationality categories were central to “re-imagining” the former empire as a multinational state. In China, modern ethnic classification similarly converted a far messier field of local identities into official national categories. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][2])

**Interpretation:** the majority is not just the biggest group already sitting there. It is the group that classification makes appear natural, bounded, and politically central.

### 2) Han majority formation: bounded plurality around a civilizational core

The PRC’s classification project fixed the population into **56 officially recognized minzu**, of which **Han** became the overwhelmingly dominant majority category. That structure does two things at once: it recognizes minority difference, but it also sharply defines the outside of the majority by limiting the menu of legitimate ethnopolitical identities. In effect, classification does not only “discover” minorities; it consolidates Han as the central, normal, and integrative category around which the whole system is organized. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

The political effect is not simply numerical dominance. It is **normative centrality**. Han operates as the unmarked center of the state’s story about national unity, development, language hierarchy, and territorial integration. Minorities are recognized, but the recognition is nested inside a framework where Hanness anchors the national whole. That is why Chinese classification tends to produce a model of **plurality under Han-centered state integration**, rather than a federation of coequal national homelands. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

### 3) Russian majority formation: dominance without the same constitutional centrality

The Soviet case is structurally different. The USSR classified citizens by nationality and built a territorial order of union republics and autonomous units around those nationalities. Russians were the largest and most powerful group, but the constitutional language of the system did **not** define the USSR as a Russian nation-state. Rather, Russians appeared as one nationality among many in a formally equal union, with the Russian republic itself only one union republic, albeit by far the largest. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

That creates a distinct kind of majority formation. Russians were dominant in practice—through language, party hierarchy, administrative reach, and imperial inheritance—but their dominance was partly **disavowed by the official multinational grammar** of the state. This meant the Soviet regime could not simply say “the USSR is the state of the Russians” in the way modern majority nation-states often center a titular majority. Russians were hegemonic, but the hegemony was mediated through a multinational imperial-federal form. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

### 4) Han as integrative majority vs Russians as imperial majority

This is the cleanest comparison.

* **Han** became the **integrative majority**: the category around which difference is arranged and absorbed inside one state.
* **Russians** became the **imperial majority**: the most powerful nationality in a state that could not fully admit that it was ordered around Russian predominance. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

That difference matters because it shapes how minority recognition works:

* In the PRC, minority recognition tends to reinforce the legitimacy of the larger Chinese state by displaying inclusion under a unified national frame.
* In the USSR, minority recognition could strengthen republic-based national identities that were not reducible to Russianness and that later became bases for secession. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][2])

### 5) The role of territorial architecture

Chinese classification did not generally map recognized groups onto constitutionally sovereign or quasi-sovereign state units. There were autonomous regions and lower-level autonomous units, but not union-republic-type structures with real constituent-state logic. That meant Han majority formation occurred inside a system where the majority could remain both demographic and constitutional center. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

In the Soviet Union, by contrast, the territorial architecture prevented Russian majority formation from taking exactly the same form. Russians were central, but the union’s legal and symbolic structure distributed recognition across multiple national republics. This weakened the ability of Russianness to function as the sole legitimate idiom of the state, even while Russian predominance remained obvious. The Soviet system therefore produced an awkward split between **formal multinational equality** and **informal Russian primacy**. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

### 6) Majority formation also changes the meaning of assimilation

In the Chinese case, assimilation or integration tends to pull groups toward Han-coded norms of language, development, and state belonging, even when official discourse celebrates multiethnicity. In other words, the classificatory regime can preserve minority labels while still making Han the practical horizon of incorporation. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

In the Soviet case, assimilation often operated through Russian language and institutions, but because the state officially promoted national forms as well, assimilation was more contradictory. One could become heavily Russified in language and culture while still carrying a non-Russian *natsional’nost’*. That is one reason Soviet identity often showed a gap between ethnocultural identification and actual linguistic practice. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

### 7) Why the long-run outcomes diverged

The PRC system tends to stabilize a story in which minorities are permanent subcomponents of a larger political civilization centered on Han majorityhood. This limits the institutional space for minority categories to mature into alternative state-bearing national projects. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

The Soviet system, however, helped produce republic-based national majorities outside Russia itself while leaving Russians in the ambiguous position of being both dominant and not quite the sole constitutional people of the union. That ambiguity mattered after 1991: non-Russian republics could convert official nationality and territory into independent states, while post-Soviet Russia then had to reconstruct Russianness and Russian statehood in a new way. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

## Preamble

Do not collapse them. **Hanification** and **Russification** are both majority-centered incorporation processes, but they operate in different state forms, with different classification regimes, and different territorial consequences.

A Brubakerian reframing helps. In his triadic model, politics is organized around **national minorities**, **nationalizing states**, and **external national homelands**. That model was built for post-communist Europe, but it is still useful here if handled carefully: the PRC is best read as a **unitary nationalizing state** with weak tolerance for external homeland claims, whereas the Russian imperial/Soviet space produced a more unstable mix of **imperial center**, **territorialized minorities**, and in some cases real or imagined **external homelands**. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

## Topic map

The clean comparison turns on five distinctions:

1. what is being assimilated;
2. whether the dominant group is the explicit state core;
3. how classification works;
4. how territory is organized;
5. whether a minority has an external homeland or only an internal frontier status. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

## 1) Brubaker first: how to map the two cases

Brubaker’s “nationalizing state” is a state that is formally the state of all citizens but is experienced and often governed as the state of a “core nation” whose language, history, and culture deserve special promotion. The triadic nexus emerges when minorities inside that state are linked to an external homeland that claims them. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][2])

Applied here:

* **Hanification** fits a **nationalizing-state** model fairly well. The state is formally multiethnic, but the political center is structured around Han-coded norms of language, development, history, and territorial unity. Minority regions are recognized, yet not recognized as coequal state-bearing nations. ([Taylor & Francis Online][3])
* **Russification** fits less neatly if treated only as a nationalizing-state process, because in the tsarist and Soviet settings it was also an **imperial integration strategy**. Russians were dominant, but under the USSR the state was not straightforwardly declared a Russian nation-state; it was formally multinational. That means Russification often operated through an imperial-federal contradiction, not just a core-nation state promoting itself. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][4])

So the first correction is this: **Hanification is closer to nationalizing-state consolidation; Russification is closer to imperial-majority expansion that sometimes masquerades as multinational universalism.** ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

## 2) What exactly is being assimilated?

**Hanification** is not simply “becoming Chinese.” It is more specifically movement toward **Han-centered norms** within a state that already defines all recognized groups as part of China. The target is not merely loyalty to the state, but accommodation to a hierarchy in which Han language, cultural repertoire, developmental norms, and historical narratives sit at the center. In Xinjiang scholarship, official inclusion often coexists with lived ethnic hierarchy, with Han identity positioned as normative and frontier minorities marked as conditional or deficient participants in the nation. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][6])

**Russification** historically meant strengthening Russian language, administration, and often Orthodoxy or Russian cultural dominance in the empire’s borderlands. Britannica’s summary is basic but accurate on the core point: from the 1860s onward it aimed to strengthen Russian language and nationality in diverse borderlands. More detailed scholarship stresses that this was not always a uniform plan; it varied by region and period. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][4])

That yields a clean distinction:

* Hanification: **internal normalization around the majority of a unitary multiethnic state**.
* Russification: **expansion of the language/culture/administrative power of an imperial majority across differentiated borderlands**. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][4])

## 3) Classification regimes: why they do not produce the same assimilation process

The earlier distinction between **minzu** and **natsional’nost’** matters here.

In the PRC, classification fixed a bounded menu of officially recognized groups. That means Hanification usually works **through a recognized-difference framework**: minorities remain minorities on paper, but incorporation pulls them toward Han-centered state norms. The state does not need minorities to stop being classified as Uyghur, Tibetan, Hui, etc. It can preserve the label while narrowing the acceptable content of difference. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][7])

In the Russian imperial and Soviet context, *natsional’nost’* could remain non-Russian even under deep Russification. Someone could be linguistically Russified, institutionally integrated, or culturally Russian-coded while still carrying a non-Russian nationality label. That makes Russification especially contradictory under Soviet conditions: it often advanced via Russian language and institutions while preserving a multinational classificatory shell. ([JSTOR][8])

So:

* Hanification often works as **majority-centered incorporation without abolishing minority labels**.
* Russification often worked as **linguistic-administrative assimilation without fully erasing non-Russian nationality status**. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][7])

## 4) Territory: the biggest structural difference

Hanification unfolds in a state that does **not** generally grant minorities constituent sovereignty. Autonomous regions exist, but they are not union republics with meaningful state-bearing legitimacy. This matters because minority incorporation is territorially constrained from the start: difference is recognized, but not as an alternative sovereign project. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][6])

Russification operated in a space where, especially in the Soviet period, nationalities were often tied to territorial units. This made assimilation structurally unstable. The center could promote Russian language and institutions, but it had already helped build territorialized national containers—republics, autonomous units, national intelligentsias—that could resist or outlast Russification. ([JSTOR][9])

This is why Hanification is better understood as a process of **frontier incorporation inside a unitary state**, while Russification is better understood as **imperial-majority penetration across a territorially differentiated political order**. ([JSTOR][9])

## 5) Brubaker’s triad: homeland state, minority, nationalizing state

Here the contrast sharpens.

### Hanification in Brubakerian terms

The PRC behaves like a powerful **nationalizing state** vis-à-vis internal minorities, but many of those minorities lack an uncontested **external homeland state** in Brubaker’s sense. Tibetans, Uyghurs, Hui, Mongols in China may have diasporic, civilizational, religious, or cross-border affinities, but these do not usually map cleanly onto an internationally recognized homeland state able to play the external-homeland role the way Hungary, Russia, or Serbia could in Brubaker’s European cases. That weakens the external corner of the triangle. The result is often a compressed dyad: **nationalizing state vs internal minority**, with external actors treated more as security threats than as legitimate homelands. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][2])

### Russification in Brubakerian terms

Russification often occurred where the triad was much more active. In the late empire and especially after the Soviet breakup, Russians outside Russia could be framed as minorities in new nationalizing states, with Russia as an external homeland. Even inside the empire, the logic of Russian predominance interacted with populations that had stronger territorial or external referents than most PRC minorities. This makes Russification easier to analyze through Brubaker’s full triadic nexus. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

So, reframed through Brubaker:

* **Hanification** = mainly a **nationalizing-state project without a fully developed external-homeland triangle**.
* **Russification** = often an **imperial or post-imperial project entangled with the full minority–state–homeland nexus**. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][2])

## 6) Majority formation: Han versus Russians

This is the decisive conceptual difference.

**Han** functions as the majority of a unitary multiethnic state. Minority incorporation therefore tends toward a logic of **normalization into the core nation’s civilizational and developmental center**, even when formal minority categories remain. ([JSTOR][10])

**Russians** functioned as the dominant nationality of an empire and then a formally multinational federation. Russification therefore carried a permanent contradiction: it promoted Russian centrality while operating in a state that could not fully admit itself as simply the state of the Russians. ([JSTOR][9])

That is why the two processes should not be conflated:

* Hanification is more **integrative and centripetal**.
* Russification is more **imperial and layered**, often advancing through language, administration, and prestige rather than through a simple majority-nation-state formula. ([Taylor & Francis Online][3])

## Best short formulation

**Hanification and Russification are both majority-centered incorporation projects, but Hanification is best understood as the work of a nationalizing unitary state on internal minorities, while Russification is better understood as the work of an imperial majority across territorially differentiated and often triadically entangled borderlands.** ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][2])

## Red team review

The main weakness in this framing is that “Hanification” is less standardized as an analytical term in the literature than “Russification,” and some uses of it are polemical rather than tightly defined. So the comparison is strongest when talking about **Han-centered incorporation** rather than pretending there is a single fully stabilized doctrine called Hanification. ([Taylor & Francis Online][11])

Rogers Brubaker is a sociologist best known for work on **nationalism, ethnicity, citizenship, migration, and religion**. The concepts most associated with him come from ***Nationalism Reframed*** and ***Ethnicity without Groups***, with later extensions into diaspora, category-making, and religion. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

## Core concepts

### 1) **Nationalizing state**

A **nationalizing state** is a state that is formally the state of all its citizens but is understood by dominant elites as the state of a **“core nation”** whose language, culture, history, or demographic position deserves special protection or promotion. The state is not simply neutral; it tries to remake public life in the image of that core nation. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][1])

### 2) **National minority**

A **national minority** is not just any minority. In Brubaker’s usage, it is a population that sees itself, or is seen, as part of a nation that is **not fully identical with the state it lives in**. These minorities often have strong historical claims, territorial concentration, or cross-border ties. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][2])

### 3) **External national homeland**

This is the outside state that claims a special connection to a minority population in another state on the basis of shared ethnicity or nationhood. The homeland may act diplomatically, symbolically, legally, or politically on behalf of that minority. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][2])

### 4) **Triadic nexus**

Brubaker’s most cited framework is the **triadic nexus**:
**nationalizing state ↔ national minority ↔ external homeland**.
The point is that these are not isolated actors. They are **mutually reactive**. What one does changes the incentives and rhetoric of the others. This is a relational model, not a static typology. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][2])

### 5) **Groupism**

In ***Ethnicity without Groups***, Brubaker attacks what he calls **groupism**: the bad habit of treating ethnicities, races, nations, or religions as if they were naturally bounded, internally unified, durable “things.” He argues that scholars often mistake categories and political claims for real, coherent groups. ([Harvard University Press][3])

### 6) **Ethnicity without groups**

His alternative is to study ethnicity not as a fixed entity but as a set of **practical categories, cultural idioms, cognitive schemas, discursive frames, institutional routines, political projects, and contingent events**. This shifts analysis from “what groups are” to **how groupness gets produced, activated, or fails to emerge**. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

### 7) **Groupness**

For Brubaker, **groupness** is variable, not constant. People may carry an ethnic label for years without acting as a solidary group; under certain conditions—violence, mobilization, crisis, elite framing, institutions—groupness can suddenly intensify. So ethnicity is often **eventful and situational**, not permanently thick. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

### 8) **Categories vs groups**

One of his most useful distinctions is between **categories** and **groups**. A census box, passport label, legal status, or ethnic term can exist as a **category** even when the people inside it do not act collectively as a **group**. States often classify first; collective identity may or may not follow. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

### 9) **Institutionalized categories**

Brubaker pays close attention to how states and institutions harden identity through **censuses, passports, laws, territorial units, and administrative routines**. Classification is not passive description. It can shape the people it claims merely to record. ([Harvard University Press][3])

### 10) **Diaspora as a category of practice**

Brubaker is skeptical of using **diaspora** as a loose label for any dispersed population. He argues it should often be treated as a **category of practice**—something claimed, invoked, and mobilized—rather than assumed as an objective sociological fact. Later work on diaspora politics also builds on this view by emphasizing mobilization and “animation.” ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

## What ties the framework together

The common thread is methodological: Brubaker wants you to stop reifying nations, races, ethnicities, and diasporas. He pushes analysis toward **classification, institutions, claims-making, mobilization, and relational dynamics**. His question is rarely “what is this group really?” and more often “how is this category being made politically real, by whom, through what institutions, and with what effects?” ([Harvard University Press][3])

## Best short summary

Brubaker’s central move is this: **do not assume nations, ethnicities, or diasporas are solid things; study how states, elites, institutions, and events make them socially and politically real.** His best-known substantive model is the **triadic nexus** of **nationalizing state, national minority, and external homeland**. His best-known methodological intervention is **“ethnicity without groups.”** ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][2])
