## Abstracts

### English

This paper synthesises ancient DNA evidence, palaeoclimate proxy records, and archaeological stratigraphy to reconstruct the demographic and cultural history of the lower Yangtze River basin from the Neolithic to the Iron Age. The Yellow and Yangtze River basins originated as genetically distinct agricultural centres, marked by bidirectional gene flow but substantial differentiation. A critical demographic rupture at approximately 4,200 BP — coinciding with the global 4.2 ka hydroclimatic event and the collapse of Liangzhu culture through catastrophic flooding — triggered a sustained influx of Yellow River-related ancestry into the Yangtze zone, a signal detectable in contemporaneous Yellow River populations as well. The post-collapse sequence of Qianshanyang, Guangfulin, and Maqiao cultures represents a progressive cultural replacement, with Yellow River-origin pottery traditions displacing Liangzhu's jade-centred prestige economy. A partial cultural recovery through the Pishan-Tiaotou horizon fed into the Wu and Yue states of the Spring and Autumn period, which occupied Liangzhu's geographic heartland and maintained its rice-agricultural substrate. However, ancient DNA evidence indicates that by the LBA-Zhou horizon (~2,700 BP), Yangtze populations carried approximately 76% Yellow River-related ancestry, with a residual ~24% southern East Asian component representing attenuated Liangzhu-zone lineages. Song–Qing Dynasty genomes from Jiangsu confirm the trajectory's endpoint at 69–100% Yellow River ancestry. The Liangzhu genomic lineage itself dispersed southward, contributing foundational ancestry to proto-Austronesian and Kra-Dai populations across Southeast Asia and the Pacific — making the 4,200 BP collapse simultaneously a local demographic end-point and a global dispersal origin.

## Summary

This conversation traced a continuous intellectual thread from broad East Asian population genetics down to the granular genomics of a single Iron Age cultural zone. Six major topics were covered.

**1. Yangtze vs. Yellow River Genomic Divergence.** The conversation opened with the foundational split: two independent Neolithic agricultural centres — millet farmers on the Yellow River, rice farmers on the Yangtze — were genomically distinct but engaged in bidirectional gene flow. Yellow River populations showed long-term continuity with a southward drift over time; Yangtze populations linked to proto-Austronesians. The upper Yellow River carried the earliest known EPAS1 high-altitude adaptation haplotype.

**2. The 4,200 BP Event.** The second topic explained BP dating, mapped the global 4.2 ka climate event, and distinguished East Asia's experience from the Mesopotamian and Egyptian pattern. While drought collapsed the Akkadian Empire and Old Kingdom Egypt, the Yangtze zone experienced catastrophic monsoon flooding. Speleothem records pinned Liangzhu's abandonment to a precise 20-year wet window. Both Yangtze and Yellow River populations showed simultaneous genomic influx of southern East Asian ancestry at this horizon — a broad demographic reorganisation, not a local event.

**3. Post-Liangzhu Archaeological Replacement.** The third topic examined the material record of what followed collapse. The sequence ran: Liangzhu → Qianshanyang (transitional) → Guangfulin (cultural replacement, Yellow River pottery) → Maqiao (Bronze Age integration) → Pishan-Tiaotou (partial recovery). Guangfulin was explicitly labelled a "cultural replacement" by Chinese archaeologists. The one unbroken thread through all phases was rice cultivation. The Pishan-Tiaotou site in Hangzhou demonstrated that the Liangzhu heartland was continuously reoccupied and repurposed, feeding eventually into Wu-Yue.

**4. The Timeline Table.** A structured HTML artefact presented 11 chronological rows from ~10,000 BP to ~1,000 CE, integrating climate events, archaeological cultures, and genomic signals into a single reference document.

**5. Wu-Yue Cultural Continuity.** The fifth topic examined what the Wu and Yue states (770–473 BCE) inherited from the Liangzhu tradition. Geographic overlap with Liangzhu territory was exact. The Shaoxing excavations (2024–25) revealed a six-thousand-year stratigraphic column. Rice cultivation was unbroken; bronze swords replaced jade as the elite prestige object. The Pishan-Tiaotou recovery culture fed directly into Wu-Yue; the Wuyue Kingdom (907–978 CE) extended the nominal and spatial continuity into the medieval period.

**6. Wu-Yue aDNA.** The final topic confronted the genomic reality directly. No genome-wide aDNA samples are published for Wu or Yue State individuals. Reconstruction from three proxy datasets — Baligang LBA-Zhou layer (~2,700 BP, ~24% southern East Asian ancestry), Eastern Zhou Central Plains data (~89–91% Yellow River ancestry), and Song–Qing Jiangsu endpoint (69–100% Yellow River ancestry) — shows that by the Wu-Yue period the Yangtze delta population was genomically majority Yellow River-derived. The ~24% residual southern East Asian component is the attenuated genomic echo of Liangzhu-zone ancestry. Cultural memory and genetic composition had diverged: the Wu-Yue states occupied Liangzhu's landscape and invoked its rice-farming traditions, but the people were largely not Liangzhu's biological descendants. Those descendants had dispersed into Southeast Asia and the Pacific.

**Overarching Themes.** Three themes ran through all six topics. First, the persistent decoupling of cultural transmission from genomic continuity — pottery styles, ritual practices, and agricultural systems can persist or revive through a population that has been substantially replaced. Second, climate as a demographic forcing function — the 4,200 BP event reorganised both cultural and genomic landscapes simultaneously across multiple civilisations. Third, the Yangtze's long arc of subordination to Yellow River demographic dominance, beginning at 4,200 BP and completing by the Song Dynasty, while Liangzhu's biological legacy escaped southward into the Pacific rather than persisting in place.

## Two Independent Agricultural Genomes

The Yellow and Yangtze river basins were among the world's oldest independent agricultural centers — domesticating millet and rice respectively — and new genome-wide data from 74 Middle Neolithic individuals reveals they were markedly genetically differentiated, though with bidirectional gene flow, supporting a demic diffusion model of mixed farming. These were not simply regional variants of one population; they represent distinct expansionary lineages with separate downstream trajectories.

Yellow River populations show distinct genetic substructures from interactions with surrounding groups during millet agriculture's mid-Neolithic expansion, while upper Yellow River populations are genetically linked to Tibetan Plateau populations and carry the earliest known adaptive EPAS1 haplotype (~5,800 BP) among modern humans. This hypoxia-adaptation variant, found later in Tibetan populations, appears to have originated in the upper Yellow River millet-farming zone before spreading onto the plateau — a direct link between Neolithic demography and modern high-altitude genetics.

Yangtze River rice farmers, by contrast, show genetic affinity with Neolithic to present-day southeast coastal China and Austronesian populations, tracing the origins of proto-Austronesians farther north than previously thought — to the Yangtze River itself. This resolves a long-standing debate: Austronesian origins are not purely coastal southeastern Chinese but connect back to inland Yangtze rice farmers.

---

## The 4,200 BP Discontinuity

Analysis of 58 ancient genomes from Baligang — a long-term settlement on the northern rim of the Middle Yangtze — spanning the Middle Neolithic to the Late Bronze Age reveals that ~4,200 BP was a critical transition point, marked by a significant genomic influx of southern East Asian populations. This date aligns with the well-documented "4.2 kiloyear event," a global climate disruption associated with drought, flooding, and societal collapse across multiple civilizations, including the fall of Liangzhu culture in the Yangtze delta.

Parallel genetic evidence of a southern East Asian ancestry influx into ancient Yellow River populations around the Late Neolithic may suggest widespread climatic impacts on environmental and migration patterns at that time. In other words, the 4,200 BP signal appears in both basins, not just the Yangtze — pointing to a broad demographic reorganization, not a localized event.

Across northern China more broadly, Yellow River populations show a monotonic increase over time in genetic affinity with present-day southern Chinese and Southeast Asians, while West Liao River populations shifted toward Yellow River ancestry during farming intensification and toward Amur River ancestry when a pastoral economy was introduced in the Bronze Age — suggesting a direct link between subsistence strategy changes and human migration.

---

## Yellow River: Stability and Internal Heterogeneity

The Yellow River zone is commonly characterized as a core of genetic continuity, but this masks internal variation. Recent ancient genomic studies reveal long-term genetic continuity among Yellow River Basin populations alongside significant gene flow from southern China and Southeast Asia since the late Neolithic, though certain late Neolithic groups exhibit marked genetic heterogeneity and do not cluster with local Longshan-culture-related populations, possibly due to the absence of additional southern East Asian-related ancestry.

At key late Neolithic sites — Taosi, Shimao, and Lushanmao — individuals carried ancestry mainly related to Yangshao culture Central Plains populations, supplemented by Northeast Asian ancestry, with one outlier at Lushanmao carrying excess Northeast Asian ancestry similar to Hongshan culture populations, suggesting cross-regional population movement at the northern steppe frontier.

In Shandong on the lower Yellow River coast, two distinct pulses of gene flow from inland Yellow River populations are detectable: one during the Dawenkou cultural period (~6,000–4,000 BP) and another in the early dynastic period (~3,500–1,500 BP), with Shandong ancestry also explaining the mainland East Asian component in post-Yayoi Japanese populations, particularly in the Ryukyu Islands after ~2,800 BP.

---

## Historical Convergence: Yangtze Absorbed into Yellow River Genome

By the historical period, the genetic distinctiveness of Yangtze populations had largely been overwritten. Ancient genomes from the lower Yangtze basin spanning the Song to Qing Dynasties (960–1921 CE) show seven of eight individuals deriving 69.3–100% of their ancestry from ancient Yellow River-related populations, with the remainder attributed to a southern East Asian substrate, and contemporary Han Chinese in this region can be modelled as direct genetic descendants of these historical individuals.

Eastern Zhou period populations in the Central Plains derive 89.3–91.8% of ancestry from Yellow River-related sources, reinforcing long-term genetic stability there despite significant social and political transformations. The demographic conquest of the Yangtze basin by Yellow River-linked farming and state-building populations — accelerating through the Han, Tang, and Song expansions southward — has no parallel in terms of scale of genomic displacement in the region.

---

## Proto-Austronesian Dispersal and Southeast Asian Connections

Autosomal aDNA from the Yangtze River basin and Mainland Southeast Asia highlights the genetic links between southern Chinese farmers and Neolithic-to-Iron Age populations of Southeast Asia, with Yangtze River rice farmers closely related to Neolithic to present-day southeastern coastal and Austronesian-speaking Chinese populations, indicating that the latter derived much of their ancestry from the Yangtze River basin.

Y-chromosome haplogroups such as O1a, O1b1a1a1, and O2a2a1a2, which are now prevalent in Mainland Southeast Asia, exhibit patterns consistent with southward migration from the Yangtze River basin, as evidenced by findings from Liangzhu, Wucheng, and Daxi sites. This connects the Liangzhu cultural sphere — famous for its jade and hydraulic engineering, which collapsed around 4,300–4,000 BP — to populations that ultimately dispersed across maritime Southeast Asia and into the Pacific.

## BP — What Does It Mean?

**BP** = "Before Present," conventionally anchored to **1950 CE** as the reference year (chosen because radiocarbon dating became standard then). So **4,200 BP ≈ 2,200 BCE**. It is a calendar of elapsed years from 1950, not from today; a finding dated "4,200 BP" in a 2025 paper still means ~4,175 years before publication, but the BP anchor does not shift.

## The 4.2 Ka Event: Climate Mechanism

The 4.2-kiloyear event, occurring around 2,200 BCE, was characterized by abrupt aridification and cooling, manifesting as prolonged megadroughts in regions including the Levant, Mesopotamia, North Africa, and South Asia, with evidence from speleothems, lake sediments, and ice cores indicating weakened monsoon systems and reduced precipitation over approximately 100 to 300 years.

The exact cause remains debated but is hypothesized to involve a combination of factors including changes in the North Atlantic Oscillation and variations in solar activity. Critically, the signal was not uniform — some Northern Hemisphere regions show no comparable aridification, suggesting amplified monsoon variability rather than a synchronized global drought. In East Asia specifically, the pattern was the inverse of desiccation: the monsoon system produced catastrophic flooding rather than drought.

---

## Liangzhu: Flood, Not Drought

China's experience diverged from the Mesopotamian and Egyptian pattern. The Liangzhu culture — among the world's most advanced Neolithic civilizations — collapsed within a short, anomalously wet period between 4,345 ± 32 and 4,324 ± 30 years ago, with speleothem records supporting the hypothesis that the city was abandoned after large-scale flooding and inundation.

Massive monsoon rains likely led to severe flooding of the Yangtze and its branches such that even Liangzhu's sophisticated dams and canals — part of a hydraulic system often called "China's Venice of the Stone Age" — could no longer withstand the water masses, destroying the city and forcing people to flee.

After the flood-driven collapse, the Yangtze River Delta entered an extended period of aridity starting ~4,000 ± 45 years ago, with major hydroclimatic changes between 4,300 and 3,000 BP potentially resulting from increasing El Niño–Southern Oscillation frequency under weakened Northern Hemisphere summer insolation. Flood, then drought — a one-two climatic punch.

---

## Archaeological Signature: Yellow River Culture Fills the Vacuum

The post-collapse cultural record is unambiguous. Subsequent cultures in the Yangtze delta — Qianshanyang (4,400–4,200 BP) and Guangfulin (4,200–3,900 BP) — developed far fewer settlements on the coastal plain and carried pottery artefacts that originated in the lower Yellow River basin, indicating that Yellow River-affiliated populations physically moved into the depopulated zone rather than that Liangzhu descendants simply changed their ceramic traditions.

---

## Genomic Signal: Admixture at the Transition Point

Ancient DNA from Baligang on the northern Yangtze rim captures the biological record of this disruption. A significant difference in the composition of southern East Asian ancestry before and after the Liangzhu-Shijiahe (LN-SJH) period is detectable around 4,200 BP, with the LN-SJH period also showing the highest genetic diversity and the largest estimated effective population size — consistent with a substantial population influx rather than in-situ growth.

Parallel genetic changes were detected in contemporaneous Late Neolithic Yellow River populations as well, meaning the 4,200 BP genomic discontinuity was not local to the Yangtze — it reorganized both major population centers simultaneously, consistent with a broad climate-driven migration event.

---

## Global Parallels: Other Bronze Age Collapses

The 4.2-kiloyear event has been hypothesized to have caused the collapse of the Old Kingdom in Egypt, the Akkadian Empire in Mesopotamia, and the Liangzhu culture in the lower Yangtze, and may also have initiated the collapse of the Indus Valley Civilization, with some of its population moving southeast to follow shifting habitats, as well as possible migration of Indo-European-speaking peoples into India.

The Akkadian case is best documented climatically: severe drought in the Fertile Crescent caused agricultural failure, famine, and the abandonment of northern Mesopotamian cities ~2,200 BCE — a near-exact synchrony with the Yangtze flooding event, though opposite in hydroclimatic character. What unified these collapses was disruption to the specific hydrological regimes on which each civilization depended, not a single uniform global drought.

---

## Long-Run Genomic Consequence

The 4,200 BP event initiated a process of Yangtze genetic dilution that completed over the following three millennia. The displaced Liangzhu-related ancestry — the genomic source of proto-Austronesian populations — dispersed southward into coastal Southeast Asia and the Pacific, while Yellow River populations filled the Yangtze basin. By the Song Dynasty (~1,000 CE), that transformation was essentially total.

## Liangzhu at Its Peak: What Was Lost

Liangzhu culture was known for its flourishing rice-based agriculture, diverse and sophisticated jade articles, advanced pottery with complex decoration, and a highly complex social structure reflected in large-scale architectural engineering. The jade objects — cong tubes, bi discs, yue axes — are the archaeological calling card: found exclusively in elite burials, they signal a fully stratified society with ritual specialists and a ruling class. No post-Liangzhu culture in the Yangtze Delta replicated this for over a millennium.

The discovery of Liangzhu remains overthrew the prevailing theory that agricultural civilization originated at a single center in the Yellow River valley and diffused to the east coast only in historic times — Liangzhu was independently sophisticated, not derivative. That independent trajectory ended abruptly at ~4,300 BP.

---

## The Silt Layer: Physical Record of Collapse

A thin layer of clay or yellowish silt found widely overlying Liangzhu cultural layers across excavation sites is interpreted as a flood deposit — the physical marker of the collapse event. Its presence at multiple sites across the delta makes it the stratigraphic dividing line between Liangzhu occupation and post-Liangzhu cultural phases. Some debate continues about whether the deposit is fluvial or marine in origin, but the flooding interpretation is the dominant consensus.

---

## Qianshanyang: Transition, Not Rupture

Qianshanyang (~4,400–4,200 BP) partially overlaps with the end of Liangzhu and represents an ambiguous transitional phase. While the late Qianshanyang culture pottery was rich in variety and significantly influenced by cultures from other regions, its main features remained the same as earlier phases — a partial continuity signal. This suggests the collapse was not instantaneous across the entire delta and that some communities persisted in modified form before fuller replacement.

---

## Guangfulin: The Cultural Break

Guangfulin (~4,200–3,900 BP) is where the replacement becomes archaeologically unambiguous. Qianshanyang and Guangfulin cultures developed far fewer settlements on the coastal plain and carried typical pottery artefacts originating in the lower Yellow River basin, while not reaching the level of sophistication and technological innovation of Liangzhu.

Archaeological studies show that the Guangfulin culture was distinctively different from the Liangzhu culture and was instead strongly influenced by cultures outside the Taihu Lake region — a phenomenon described by some scholars as "cultural replacement." The jade tradition collapses. The hydraulic infrastructure is not maintained. Settlement pulls back from the inundated coastal plain to higher ground inland.

The large Qujialing-Shijiahe and Liangzhu settlements appear to have been abandoned in favor of small settlements mostly located in the northern Yangtze Valley, and the cause of the decline is possibly related to the expansion of late Longshan cultures from the Yellow River Valley — with the late Dawenkou culture expanding as far south as the northern bank of the Yangtze.

---

## Maqiao: Bronze Age Incorporation

In Shanghai, the post-Liangzhu period is most strikingly represented by Guangfulin culture (4,300–4,000 BP) and the subsequent Maqiao culture (3,900–3,200 BP) and post-Maqiao culture (3,200–2,900 BP). Maqiao is contemporaneous with the Xia and early Shang dynasties in the north. Northern bronze-metallurgy appears in the material record, indicating that Yangtze delta communities were now culturally integrated into a broader north-driven political and technological sphere rather than maintaining an independent developmental trajectory.

---

## Tiaotou and Pishan: Partial Recovery

The Tiaotou site in the Liangzhu heartland (Hangzhou) reveals a more complex picture than simple replacement. Occupied from the late Liangzhu period through the early first millennium BC, Tiaotou was repurposed from a late Liangzhu template and continually adapted through the post-Liangzhu transition, culminating around 1,200–1,000 BC in a new cultural phenomenon labeled the Pishan-Tiaotou Culture.

Sites like Pishan and Tiaotou not only integrated adapted Liangzhu cultural traits but borrowed heavily from northern bronze-metallurgy to form unique hybrid entities, suggesting that the cultural replacement was not total erasure — underlying Liangzhu tradition persisted in modified form and reasserted itself once environmental and political conditions stabilized. This eventually feeds into the Wu-Yue cultures of the first millennium BCE.

---

## Liangzhu's Genomic Legacy

DNA recovered from human remains at Liangzhu culture sites shows high frequencies of Y-chromosome haplogroup O1-M119, linking them to modern eastern and coastal Han Chinese and to Austronesian and Kra-Dai peoples. The people of Liangzhu did not simply vanish — they dispersed, carrying rice agriculture and distinctive genomic ancestry southward into coastal Southeast Asia and ultimately into the Pacific. The cultural collapse and the genomic dispersal are two faces of the same event.

## Summary

Wu and Yue were the Iron-Bronze Age successor states of the lower Yangtze, emerging in the 8th–5th centuries BCE in exactly the geography where Liangzhu and its post-collapse cultures had played out. They were not archaeologically invented from nothing — they drew on a 2,000-year chain of Yangtze tradition (Liangzhu → Guangfulin → Maqiao → Pishan-Tiaotou) while absorbing northern Zhou bronze culture. Yue in particular, centred on Shaoxing in Liangzhu heartland territory, carried the most direct ancestral thread.

## Geographic Continuity: Same Ground, New Polities

Yue Culture refers to the cultural remains of the Yue State in the Eastern Zhou Dynasty (770–256 BC), centred on Kuaiji Mountain in Shaoxing in east Zhejiang and south of Hangzhou Bay, influenced by Hemudu Culture and Liangzhu Culture. This is not an abstract cultural connection — it is literal geographic overlap. The Yue capital at Shaoxing sits within what was Liangzhu territory. The same alluvial plains, the same river systems, the same rice-growing landscape.

Yue State building foundations ~2,500 years old have been uncovered in Shaoxing, with Neolithic Majiabang cultural remains also identified within the ancient city — a discovery that extends the documented history of human activity in Shaoxing back approximately 3,500 additional years. This stratigraphy is itself the argument: Majiabang → Liangzhu → Maqiao → Yue, all layered beneath the same city.

---

## The Shaoxing Excavations: A Six-Thousand-Year Column

At the Tashan and Changfang sites in Shaoxing, Neolithic sand-tempered pottery, red pottery, jade artifacts and other relics were unearthed from three metres below the surface for the first time, with Yue State sacrificial layers clearly defined above them — the accumulation of relics from different eras described by the project leader as resembling a cross-section of an "underground history book" chronicling 6,000 years of regional evolution.

Inside Yue State sacrificial trenches, archaeologists found stamped hard pottery jars covered with woven fabrics, primitive porcelain bowls, tin ge weapons, and ritual vessels containing horse, cattle and deer bones, indicating multiple high-level sacrificial activities at the site. The ritual complexity is a clear step-up from Maqiao and Pishan-Tiaotou — Yue was not just a cultural remnant but a reassertion of state-level organization in the Yangtze delta.

---

## Rice: The One Unbroken Thread

From Shangshan at ~10,000 BP through to Yue State settlements two millennia ago, rice cultivation is the single material practice that never breaks. Excavations at Yue State rural sites in Shaoxing discovered large quantities of rice, rice husks, and rice spikelet alongside vegetable and fruit seeds, confirming rice remained the main food of Yue residents. Through every population disruption — the 4,200 BP collapse, the Guangfulin replacement, the Maqiao integration — the paddy field survived. It is the deepest thread of continuity connecting Liangzhu to Wu-Yue.

During the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods (770–221 BC), iron farming tools and ox-drawn plows enabled Yangtze Basin inhabitants to better harness water resources and the favorable climate, leading to the emergence of Wu-Yue cultural regions along the lower reaches of the Yangtze. Iron intensified what Liangzhu had built on stone and wood.

---

## Bronze Swords: The New Elite Marker

The jade cong and bi of Liangzhu found no direct successor in the post-collapse centuries. Instead, Wu-Yue elites signalled status through bronze swords — some of the most technically advanced in the ancient world. The States of Wu and Yue were regional hegemons in China's late Spring and Autumn Period centred in modern Jiangsu and Zhejiang, and their weapons stood at the pinnacle of the period — renowned for bronze swords including the Sword of Goujian, with rhombic-patterned and bimetallic swords as distinguished examples.

The Sword of Fuchai of Wu State, 58.3 cm in length, remains incomparably sharp after more than 2,400 years due to a two-stage casting process in which a higher copper-to-tin ratio was used for the body (for toughness) and a higher tin-to-copper ratio for the blade edge (for hardness). The technical sophistication is different in medium from Liangzhu jade — but the impulse to produce elite prestige objects of extraordinary craft quality runs directly through both.

---

## Mound Tombs: A Yangtze Signature That Spreads

One of the clearest archaeological markers linking Liangzhu tradition through Wu-Yue to the broader Southeast is the mound tomb. The tomb remains of mounded stone and paved pebble structure, along with funeral objects such as stoneware urns, pots, and primitive porcelain bowls, are identified as features of the Mound Tomb Culture of Wu and Yue, and bronze artifacts from Wu and Yue were diffused into Southeast China as models for local imitation. This cultural complex — not the Yellow River burial tradition — spread southward from the Yangtze delta, reaching Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, and the coast of Vietnam.

---

## Wu vs. Yue: Divergent Emphases

Wu Culture's influential region was the Taihu Lake area including south Jiangsu and north Zhejiang, with an obvious feature of sea transportation — the local opera, crafts, carving, painting and gardens became characteristic features, and Wu Culture reached its peak in the Ming Dynasty. Wu was the more maritime-oriented state, controlling the lower Yangtze estuary and developing significant naval capacity — the dimension that connects most directly to later coastal trade and dispersal networks.

Yue by contrast was the more conservative inheritor of deep Liangzhu-zone tradition. Centred on Shaoxing — nearly within sight of the Liangzhu City ruins — it drew on Hemudu and Liangzhu cultural memory more directly. The first group of bronze cultural elements in Southeast China originated from Shang and Zhou cultures of the Central Plain and from Wu and Yue states in the plain region of south Jiangsu and north Zhejiang — the pivotal transmission point between northern bronze culture and the southern Yangtze world.

---

## The Long Continuity: Wuyue Kingdom (907–978 CE)

The name itself recurs in medieval history. The Wuyue Kingdom — one of the Ten Kingdoms of the Five Dynasties period — controlled modern Zhejiang and parts of Jiangsu from 907 to 978 CE, with its capital in Hangzhou (Liangzhu heartland). Hangzhou's 2024 archaeological discoveries include the Yijincheng City Wall built during the late Tang Dynasty by King Qian Liu of Wuyue, with layered ruins tracing how Liangzhu's jade-wielding elites influenced southern China's cultural expansion. The geographic and nominal continuity from Liangzhu (3300–2300 BCE) through Wu-Yue states (770–473 BCE) to the Wuyue Kingdom (907–978 CE) spans five millennia in the same landscape.

## Summary

There are no published genome-wide aDNA samples directly labelled as Wu or Yue State individuals (~770–473 BCE). The genomic portrait of Wu-Yue has to be reconstructed by triangulating three converging datasets: Baligang's LBA-Zhou layer (~2,700 BP), the Eastern Zhou Central Plains data, and the Song–Qing Jiangsu data as a terminal endpoint. The picture that emerges is: by the Wu-Yue period, the Yangtze delta population was predominantly Yellow River-derived, with a residual ~24% southern East Asian substrate — the attenuated genomic echo of Liangzhu.

---

## The Core Problem: No Direct Wu-Yue Genomes

The gap is real and acknowledged. Analysis of ancient genomes from the lower Yangtze River basin acknowledges that the study lacks ancient genomes from rice-farming populations that lived in the region between the Neolithic and the historical dynasties — which is precisely the window that covers Guangfulin, Maqiao, Pishan-Tiaotou, and the Wu-Yue states. DNA preservation in the humid Yangtze delta is poor; the cremation practices common in Jiangsu through early historical periods destroy bone DNA entirely. What exists instead are proxy datasets from adjacent sites.

---

## Baligang LBA-Zhou Layer: The Closest Proxy

The most direct genomic window into the Wu-Yue period comes from Baligang on the northern Yangtze rim. During the LBA-Zhou period (~2,700 BP — falling precisely within the Spring and Autumn / early Warring States era of Wu-Yue — the gene influx at Baligang shifted northward again, diminishing the southern East Asian ancestry component to 24%, down from its post-4,200 BP peak of 35%.

The full admixture sequence at Baligang shows north-south gene flow present as early as 6,000 BP, northern influence dominating from 5,000–4,700 BP, a sharp southern reversal at ~4,300 BP (the collapse event), and then a renewed northward shift by the Bronze Age–Zhou period. The Wu-Yue period sits at the tail end of this northward re-convergence — a population carrying majority Yellow River ancestry with a significant but declining Yangtze substrate.

Critically, cultural innovation and genomic shift did not always align — changes in pottery style or farming practice often spread without major migrations, meaning the Wu-Yue material culture revival at Tiaotou and Pishan does not necessarily imply a genomic reversal toward Liangzhu-type ancestry. Culture moved faster than genes.

---

## Eastern Zhou Central Plains: The Northern Comparator

The most genomically well-sampled contemporaneous population is the Eastern Zhou Central Plains — the Zhou-state world that Wu and Yue were politically adjacent to and culturally borrowing from. Eastern Zhou individuals from the Shangshihe cemetery show predominantly Yellow River Basin-related ancestry with minor contributions from southern East Asian-related and Eurasian Steppe-related sources, and Zhou-period populations overall display increased southern East Asian ancestry compared to Neolithic populations — indicating intensified genetic interaction between Central Plains populations and southern groups during the Eastern Zhou period.

If the Central Plains itself was gaining southern admixture during the Eastern Zhou, populations in the actual Yangtze delta (Wu-Yue territory) — geographically closer to southern source populations — would plausibly carry a higher southern East Asian fraction than the Central Plains figure, but still within a Yellow River-dominant ancestry model.

---

## The Song–Qing Endpoint: Backward Extrapolation

The terminal anchor is the Song–Qing Jiangsu data, which gives the post-Wu-Yue trajectory's endpoint. Seven of eight Song-to-Qing Dynasty individuals from the lower Yangtze derived 69.3–100% of their ancestry from ancient Yellow River-related populations, with the remainder from a southern East Asian substrate, and modern Han Chinese in Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Shanghai can be modelled as direct descendants of these historical individuals.

Working backward: if Song–Qing Jiangsu is ~70–100% Yellow River, and Baligang at ~2,700 BP (Wu-Yue era) is at ~76% Yellow River / 24% southern East Asian, the trajectory is consistent — a slow continued northward drift between the Wu-Yue period and the Song Dynasty, driven by ongoing Han southward migration through the Qin unification, Han expansion, and later Tang–Song demographic flows.

---

## What the 24% Southern Substrate Represents

The residual ~24% southern East Asian ancestry at the LBA-Zhou Baligang horizon is genomically interpretable. This component, modelled against Fujian Early Neolithic populations, represents the attenuated signal of Liangzhu-zone ancestry — the same lineage that, in undiluted form in earlier millennia, connected to proto-Austronesians. By the Wu-Yue period it is a minority fraction, but it is non-trivial and regionally specific: Baligang individuals consistently show higher allele sharing with southern East Asians than contemporaneous Yellow River populations, confirmed by both outgroup-f3 values and F4 statistics. Wu-Yue populations almost certainly carried more of this component than the Central Plains — they were the southernmost major political entities, occupying the original Liangzhu landscape.

---

## Genomic Mismatch with Cultural Identity

The clearest takeaway from the data is a divergence between cultural memory and genetic composition. By the time the Yue kings at Shaoxing were invoking Liangzhu-zone landscapes and rice-farming traditions, their population was genomically majority Yellow River. The cultural continuity was real — spatial overlap, rice cultivation, water management, mound tomb tradition — but it was carried by a population that had been substantially replaced. Despite significant documentary migration in the lower Yangtze River basin, the population structure maintained long-term genetic stability and high affinity to Yellow River-related populations, suggesting that frequent historical migrations homogenized genetic profiles across the region.

The Liangzhu genomic signature was not preserved in the people who lived in Liangzhu's landscape during the Iron Age. It had dispersed — southward, into coastal Southeast Asia and the Pacific — centuries before the Wu-Yue states emerged.
