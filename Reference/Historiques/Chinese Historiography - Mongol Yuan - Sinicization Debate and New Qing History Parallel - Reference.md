## Summary

This analysis examines the Mongol Yuan dynasty (1271–1368) as a historiographical parallel to the Manchu Qing, arguing that the sinicization debate is a structural feature of conquest dynasty historiography rather than a peculiarity generated by Western NQH revisionism.

**Classical Chinese historiography** of the Yuan centered on the *zhengtong* (orthodox succession) problem — the *Yuan Shi* was compiled hastily in 1370 for political legitimation purposes, and Song loyalist historians like Hu Sansheng developed resistance commentary traditions that implicitly questioned Mongol legitimacy while operating within acceptable scholarly frameworks.

**Institutional distinctives** separating the Yuan from the Qing: the four-class ethnic stratification system (*Mongols → Semu → northern Chinese → southern Chinese*) formalized ethnic hierarchy more explicitly than any Qing policy; the examination system was suspended from 1237 to 1315, a rupture with Chinese institutional tradition the Qing never attempted; and Kublai Khan's bifurcated administration — Chinese Secretariat/Censorate structures alongside Mongol kuriltai deliberation and appanage systems — generated debates structurally identical to Crossley vs. Elliott on whether Yuan governance reflected genuine institutional synthesis or Chinese administrative tools serving a Mongol political core.

**Western historiography** produced an Inner Asian turn parallel to NQH. Thomas Allsen situated the Yuan as the eastern node of a Eurasian imperial system connecting China to Persia and Russia through the *Pax Mongolica* — a more radical decentering than NQH, making China one pole of a Eurasian world system rather than the gravitational center. David Robinson examined Yuan court cultural performance as hybrid synthesis neither straightforwardly Chinese nor Mongol. Morris Rossabi's Kublai Khan emerged as a genuinely bifurcated figure inhabiting multiple cultural worlds simultaneously — parallel to Crossley's Qianlong but more pragmatic and less ideologically theorized. Elizabeth Endicott-West found Yuan local administration a pragmatic hybrid paralleling Elliott's Qing findings. John Dardess argued for genuine Confucianization of Yuan governance ideology, creating the same tension with Inner Asian-focused scholarship that Ho's sinicization thesis creates against NQH.

**Chinese nationalist historiography** resolved the Yuan's inclusion problem through Fei Xiaotong's *Zhonghua minzu* (中華民族) framework — defining China as a multiethnic nation of fifty-six recognized peoples in a "pluralistic unity" (*duoyuan yiti*), making the Yuan a dynasty of one Chinese ethnic group rather than a foreign conquest. This framework avoids Han-centric sinicization claims while projecting a modern multiethnic nationalist category onto pre-modern periods. The independent Mongolian state creates a live geopolitical complication with no precise Qing parallel — both Chinese and Mongolian historians contest ownership of Chinggis Khan as a national historical figure, with China claiming him as part of Chinese multiethnic heritage and Mongolia claiming him as the foundation of Mongolian national identity.

**Where the Yuan-Qing parallel holds:** both generated sinicization debates with identical structure; both maintained parallel administrative systems for Chinese and non-Chinese constituencies; both are claimed by the *Zhonghua minzu* framework; both have generated debates where scholarly and political motivations are inseparable; and both demonstrate that conquest dynasty multiethnic governance reflects deliberate policy rather than incomplete assimilation.

**Where it breaks:** the Mongol Empire's Eurasian scale (*Pax Mongolica* connecting China to Persia, Russia, and Central Asia) has no Qing parallel; the Yuan's explicit legal ethnic stratification was more severe than Qing ethnic policy; examination system suspension represents an institutional rupture the Qing never attempted; the independent Mongolian state creates live geopolitical stakes without precise Qing equivalent; and the Yuan's shorter duration sharpens rather than reproduces Qing debates by limiting gradual sinicization arguments.

**Core conclusion:** The sinicization debate recurs structurally whenever a non-Han ruling house governs China for an extended period — it appeared for the Jurchen Jin, the Mongol Yuan, and the Manchu Qing. NQH's intervention added new sources, new theoretical frameworks from postcolonial and ethnic identity theory, and new political stakes around PRC territorial claims — but not a new question. The deepest insight the Yuan parallel enables is that conquest dynasty multiethnic governance sophistication is better understood as a general imperial strategy than as a specific cultural achievement of any one dynasty.

## Why the Yuan Is the Necessary Parallel

Before entering the historiography directly, the comparative logic requires stating. The Mongol Yuan dynasty (1271–1368) and the Manchu Qing dynasty are the two great conquest dynasties of Chinese history — foreign-origin ruling houses that governed China proper for extended periods while simultaneously administering vast non-Chinese territories. Both generated the same foundational historiographical question: was this a Chinese dynasty with foreign rulers, or a foreign empire that included China as one domain? Both generated sinicization debates, zhengtong controversies, and modern nationalist historiographical investments. Both have been reread by Western scholars using non-Chinese sources in ways that destabilize Han-centric narratives.

The Yuan case is in some respects more extreme than the Qing. The Mongols made fewer concessions to Chinese administrative tradition than the Manchus, maintained more visible ethnic stratification, ruled for a shorter period, and were expelled rather than gradually transformed. If the sinicization thesis has difficulty with the Qing, it has even greater difficulty with the Yuan — the Mongol case constitutes its most challenging test. And if NQH-style revisionism applies to the Qing, the question immediately arises: does it apply equally to the Yuan? The answers are illuminating precisely where they diverge.

## Classical Chinese Historiography of the Yuan: The Zhengtong Problem

### The Ming's Fraught Inheritance

The Ming dynasty (1368–1644), which expelled the Mongols and restored Han Chinese rule, faced an immediate historiographical problem: how to write the history of its predecessor. The *Yuan Shi* (元史, History of the Yuan), compiled with extraordinary speed in 1370 under Song Lian's supervision, was the fastest-produced of the official histories — completed in roughly a year — and consequently the most disorganized and least analytically developed. The haste reflected political urgency: the new Ming needed to formally close the Yuan's account and establish its own legitimacy as the dynasty that had received the Mandate of Heaven from Mongol hands.

The *Yuan Shi*'s treatment of the Yuan within the *zhengtong* framework was itself contested. The Mongols had overthrown the Southern Song in 1279, but the Song had already lost north China to the Jurchen Jin dynasty in 1127. Was *zhengtong* transmitted from the Jin to the Yuan, or did it skip the Jin and pass directly from the Song to the Yuan — and if so, how? The *zhengtong* debates around the Yuan were more complex than those around any other dynasty precisely because the Mongol conquest disrupted the continuous Chinese dynastic sequence so completely.

### Hu Sansheng and the Resistance Tradition

The *Zizhi Tongjian Zhu* (資治通鑑注) commentary tradition, particularly **Hu Sansheng's** (胡三省) fourteenth-century annotations written under Mongol rule, represents the most important classical Chinese historiographical response to the Yuan from within the period. Hu, writing as a Song loyalist under Mongol rule, developed a commentary practice that preserved Chinese historical memory and implicitly questioned Mongol legitimacy while nominally operating within an acceptable scholarly framework.

This resistance historiography — writing that maintained Chinese civilizational continuity against conquest dynasty rule through the medium of classical scholarship — became a template that Ming and later historians would invoke when assessing the Yuan's place in Chinese history. The Yuan was acknowledged as the dynasty that held political power while its cultural legitimacy as a Chinese dynasty remained permanently contested in ways that even the Qing, with its far greater accommodation of Chinese institutional norms, did not experience to the same degree.

## The Structure of Mongol Rule: What Made the Yuan Different

### The Four-Class System and Ethnic Stratification

The Yuan dynasty's most distinctive and most discussed institutional feature was its explicit ethnic stratification — the **four-class system** (*sì děng rén* 四等人) that ranked the population hierarchically: Mongols at the apex, **Semu** (色目人, "various-eyed people" — Central Asians, Persians, Turks, and others from western Eurasia) in the second tier, northern Chinese (*Hanren* 漢人, including Jurchen and Khitan as well as Han Chinese) in the third tier, and southern Chinese (*Nanren* 南人, former Song subjects) at the base.

This system is more extensively documented in later scholarship than it was systematically implemented in practice — recent historians have noted that the four-class system appears more as a legal and administrative reference point than as a rigidly enforced social hierarchy across all contexts. But its existence as an official framework represents something qualitatively different from Qing ethnic policy: where the Qing maintained Manchu distinctiveness through Banner system separation, the Yuan institutionalized ethnic hierarchy through explicit legal discrimination. The comparison immediately raises the question of whether both represent the same phenomenon (conquest dynasty ethnic boundary maintenance) or fundamentally different imperial strategies.

### The Examination System Suspended

The Mongols suspended the Chinese civil service examination system for most of the period from 1237 to 1315 — a rupture with no Qing parallel. The examination system was the primary institutional mechanism through which Chinese civilizational values were reproduced, the scholar-gentry class was formed, and the administrative elite was recruited. Its suspension for nearly eighty years had profound consequences for the Chinese literate class and for the relationship between Mongol rulers and Chinese administrative tradition.

When **Ayurbarwada (Emperor Renzong)** restored the examinations in 1315, he did so in a modified form that reserved quotas for Mongol and Semu candidates, explicitly preventing the Chinese literate class from dominating the bureaucracy as they had under the Song and would again under the Ming. This institutional compromise — restoring the examination form while limiting its Chinese civilizational consequence — is one of the clearest pieces of evidence that the Yuan was not simply failing to sinicize but was **deliberately managing** the relationship between Chinese institutional frameworks and Mongol ruling prerogatives.

### Kublai Khan's Dual Administration

**Kublai Khan** (r. 1260–1294), the founder of the Yuan dynasty proper, built an administration that was deliberately bifurcated between Chinese institutional frameworks (the Secretariat, the Censorate, examination-derived officials) and Mongol political structures (the kuriltai tradition of Mongol aristocratic deliberation, the appanage system distributing territory among Chinggisid princes, the Mongol-language administrative apparatus). 

This bifurcation has generated historiographical debates directly parallel to those around Qianlong's simultaneous sovereignty. Was Kublai genuinely operating within both frameworks as constituent elements of a new imperial synthesis — as Frederick Hok-lam Chan and others have argued — or was the Chinese administrative apparatus a pragmatic tool while the Mongol political structures remained the empire's real operating system — as Thomas Allsen and other Inner Asian historians have emphasized? The parallel to Crossley versus Elliott on the Qing is structurally identical.

## Western Historiography: The Inner Asian Turn

### Thomas Allsen and the Mongol World System

**Thomas Allsen's** work — particularly *Mongol Imperialism* (1987), *Commodity and Exchange in the Mongol Empire* (1997), and *Culture and Conquest in Mongol Eurasia* (2001) — represents the most important Western scholarly reorientation of Yuan studies away from China-centric frameworks. Allsen argues that the Yuan dynasty cannot be understood in isolation from the larger Mongol imperial system — the *Pax Mongolica* connecting China to Persia, Central Asia, and Eastern Europe through the Chinggisid appanage states (the Ilkhanate, the Golden Horde, the Chagatai Khanate).

The Yuan was, in Allsen's framework, the **eastern node of a Eurasian imperial system** — its economic policies, administrative practices, and cultural patronage were shaped by its connections to the other Mongol successor states as much as by its relationship to Chinese tradition. The enormous flows of craftsmen, administrators, scientists, and cultural objects across the Mongol world system meant that the Yuan court was as connected to Tabriz and Samarkand as to Hangzhou and Quanzhou. This is a more radical decentering of China than anything in NQH — NQH relocates the center from Han Chinese civilization to the Qing court's Inner Asian dimensions, while Allsen relocates it further still, to a Eurasian imperial system in which China is one pole among several.

### David Robinson and Yuan Court Culture

**David Robinson's** *Martial Spectacles of the Ming Court* (2013) and his work on Yuan court culture have examined how Mongol rulership performed its authority through specific cultural forms — hunting spectacles, martial displays, court entertainments — that were neither Chinese nor straightforwardly "Mongol" in any essentialist sense but were specific to the Yuan court's hybrid cultural environment.

Robinson's approach parallels Crossley's methodology in important ways: both focus on cultural performance as evidence of imperial identity, both use non-Chinese sources to recover dimensions of court life invisible in the Chinese record, and both find sophisticated cultural synthesis where older historiography saw either sinicization or resistance to it. Robinson is more attentive than Crossley to the **instability** of that synthesis — the Yuan court's cultural performances were contested, negotiated, and never fully resolved into a stable imperial identity.

### Morris Rossabi and the Kublai Khan Question

**Morris Rossabi's** *Khubilai Khan: His Life and Times* (1988) remains the standard biographical treatment of the Yuan founder in English. Rossabi's Kublai is a genuinely bifurcated figure — deeply engaged with Chinese civilization, employing Chinese advisors, sponsoring Chinese cultural projects, and governing China through adapted Chinese institutions, while simultaneously maintaining Mongol identity, Mongol political relationships, and Mongol cultural practices that distinguished him from any Chinese emperor.

Rossabi's interpretation steers between simple sinicization (Kublai became Chinese) and simple alien rule (Kublai remained purely Mongol) toward something more like the NQH simultaneous identity claim — Kublai inhabited multiple cultural worlds simultaneously without fully belonging to any. The difference from Crossley's Qianlong is one of degree and theorization: Kublai's simultaneity appears more pragmatic and less ideologically developed than the elaborate sovereign theory Crossley attributes to Qianlong.

## Chinese Historiography of the Yuan: The Nationalist Dilemma

### The Inclusion Problem

The Yuan dynasty presents Chinese nationalist historiography with a dilemma more acute than the Qing. The Qing can be incorporated into Chinese history through the sinicization narrative — Manchu rulers became Chinese. But the Mongols, who ruled for less than a century in China proper, maintained more explicit ethnic separateness, suspended Chinese institutions, and were eventually expelled by a Han Chinese uprising, resist the sinicization narrative more completely.

The PRC's solution has been to define the Yuan as a dynasty of the **Chinese nation** (*Zhonghua minzu* 中華民族) — a multiethnic category that includes Mongols as one of China's fifty-six recognized nationalities, making the Yuan a dynasty of one Chinese ethnic group ruling over a multiethnic Chinese state rather than a foreign conquest. This framework, developed most systematically by **Fei Xiaotong** (費孝通) in his theory of the Chinese nation as a unified multiethnic community, allows the Yuan to be claimed as part of Chinese history without making implausible claims about Mongol sinicization.

### Fei Xiaotong's Zhonghua Minzu Framework

**Fei Xiaotong's** (1910–2005) concept of *Zhonghua minzu* (中華民族) — developed in his influential 1988 essay and elaborated in subsequent work — is the theoretical foundation of contemporary Chinese historiography's treatment of both the Yuan and Qing as Chinese dynasties. Fei argued that China's fifty-six recognized ethnic groups constitute a single unified Chinese nation at a higher level of abstraction — a "pluralistic unity" (*duoyuan yiti* 多元一體) in which ethnic diversity exists within an overarching national unity.

Within this framework, Mongol, Manchu, Tibetan, and Uyghur peoples are all members of the Chinese nation regardless of their historical relationship to Han Chinese civilization — they are Chinese not because they became Han but because they are constituent members of the larger national community. The Yuan and Qing are therefore Chinese dynasties because their founders were members of the Chinese national community, even if they were not Han Chinese.

This framework is sophisticated enough to resist easy dismissal. It genuinely moves beyond Han-centric sinicization theory and acknowledges ethnic diversity within the Chinese state. Its critics — including Ge Zhaoguang, who engages it carefully — argue that it projects a modern multiethnic nationalist category backward onto pre-modern periods when the overarching national identity it posits did not exist as a meaningful political or cultural reality for the people concerned.

### The Mongol Independence Problem

Unlike the Manchu bannermen, who became a recognized Chinese minority after 1912 with no separate state, the Mongols present Chinese historiography with an acute contemporary problem: the existence of an independent **Mongolian state** (the Mongolian People's Republic, now simply Mongolia) whose population regards itself as the heir to the same Mongol civilization that produced the Yuan dynasty. Inner Mongolia remains part of China; Outer Mongolia does not. 

This means claiming the Yuan as a Chinese dynasty has a live geopolitical dimension — it implicitly contests Mongolian national claims to their own imperial heritage. Chinese and Mongolian historians have conducted genuinely bitter exchanges over the ownership of Chinggis Khan as a historical figure, with Chinese scholars emphasizing his significance for Chinese history and Mongolian scholars insisting he belongs to Mongolian national history. The parallel to NQH debates about whether Qing emperors were Chinese or Manchu rulers is structurally identical, with the added complication of a sovereign neighboring state actively contesting the historiographical claim.

## The Sinicization Debate Applied to the Yuan

### Elizabeth Endicott-West and Administrative Adaptation

**Elizabeth Endicott-West's** *Mongolian Rule in China* (1989) examined Yuan local administration in detail, finding a more complex picture than either the sinicization narrative or the alien rule narrative allows. The Mongols adapted Chinese administrative forms extensively while maintaining Mongol personnel preferences and political hierarchies — producing a hybrid system that neither fully reproduced Song administrative tradition nor operated on purely Mongol principles.

Her findings parallel Elliott's for the Qing: administrative adaptation to Chinese forms did not constitute sinicization in any deep cultural sense, but it also was not simply alien imposition. The Yuan local administration was a pragmatic hybrid whose logic was administrative efficiency rather than cultural statement. This parallel between Yuan and Qing administrative pragmatism suggests that what NQH identifies as specifically Qing multiethnic governance sophistication may be better understood as a general feature of successful conquest dynasty administration in China.

### John Dardess and the Confucianization Argument

**John Dardess** has argued, against the grain of recent Inner Asian-focused scholarship, for the genuine significance of **Confucianization** in Yuan governance — not sinicization in Ho's sense (Mongols becoming Chinese) but the adoption of Confucian political frameworks as governing ideology by Yuan emperors who found them administratively useful and politically legitimating.

His work on Yuan political thought and the role of Chinese literati advisors at the Mongol court finds evidence of genuine intellectual engagement with Confucian governance theory, not merely its surface adoption for Chinese audience consumption. This argument creates tension with the Inner Asian emphasis of Allsen and Robinson — and the parallel tension between Confucianization arguments and NQH Inner Asian arguments for the Qing suggests the debate's structure is inherent to the conquest dynasty situation rather than specific to either case.

### The Shorter Duration Problem

One significant disanalogy between Yuan and Qing historiographical debates concerns duration. The Qing ruled for 268 years — long enough for institutional evolution, generational change, and genuine cultural transformation to occur. The Yuan ruled China proper for less than a century in its effective phase. This means the sinicization debate for the Yuan is necessarily more compressed — there was simply less time for the gradual processes that Ho invokes for the Qing to operate.

Paradoxically, this makes the Yuan a cleaner test case for certain NQH arguments: if multiethnic governance sophistication appears in both a short-duration conquest dynasty (Yuan) and a long-duration one (Qing), it is more plausible as a structural feature of Mongol and Manchu imperial strategy than as a product of gradual evolution. The parallel strengthens the NQH claim that conquest dynasty multiethnic administration reflects deliberate policy rather than incomplete assimilation.

## The Mongol Empire as Comparative Frame: Allsen vs. NQH

### Eurasian Scale vs. Inner Asian Scale

The most significant divergence between Yuan historiography and NQH concerns **geographical scale**. NQH situates the Qing within an Inner Asian comparative frame — comparing Qing multiethnic governance to other steppe-origin dynasties (Liao, Jin, Yuan) and to Tibetan and Central Asian political traditions. Allsen situates the Yuan within a **Eurasian** comparative frame — comparing it to the Ilkhanate in Persia, the Golden Horde in Russia, and the Chagatai Khanate in Central Asia as constituent parts of a single imperial system.

The Eurasian frame, if applied to the Qing, would produce a very different picture than NQH's Inner Asian frame. It would ask: how did the Qing relate to the Russian Empire, the Ottoman Empire, and the Mughal Empire as contemporaneous Eurasian imperial formations? This question — which the historian **Laura Hostetler** and others have begun to address — reframes the Qing not as a specifically Inner Asian phenomenon but as one of several early modern Eurasian empires managing multiethnic territories through comparable strategies. The NQH emphasis on the Qing's Inner Asian specificity may be, in this larger frame, a form of parochialism.

### The Pax Mongolica and Global History

Allsen's work points toward a dimension of Yuan historiography with no real NQH parallel: the Yuan's role in what Janet Abu-Lughod and others have theorized as a **thirteenth-century world system** connecting Eurasia through Mongol-facilitated trade and cultural exchange. The Black Death's transmission across Eurasia, the movement of Chinese technological innovations (printing, gunpowder, paper money) westward, the Polo family's travels, and the exchange of astronomical and medical knowledge between Chinese and Islamic scientific traditions all occurred within the framework of Mongol imperial connectivity.

This global historical dimension transforms the Yuan from a conquest dynasty to be evaluated by its relationship to Chinese civilization into a **node in world history** whose significance extends far beyond China. No comparable argument exists for the Qing — the Qing's Inner Asian empire, however multiethnic, did not produce the same quality of Eurasian connectivity that the Mongol *Pax* created. This is perhaps the Yuan's most distinctive historiographical feature, making it simultaneously the closest parallel to the Qing and the case that most thoroughly escapes the frameworks that structure both Chinese nationalist and NQH historiography.

## Where the Parallel Holds and Where It Breaks

**Where it holds.** Both Yuan and Qing generated sinicization debates with the same structure: Han-centric historians emphasizing cultural absorption, Inner Asian-focused historians emphasizing deliberate ethnic boundary maintenance, with genuine evidence supporting both positions. Both dynasties maintained parallel administrative structures for Chinese and non-Chinese constituencies. Both present Chinese nationalist historiography with the problem of incorporating conquest dynasties into a continuous Chinese civilizational narrative. Both have been claimed by the PRC's *Zhonghua minzu* framework as dynasties of the multiethnic Chinese nation. Both have generated debates where scholarly and political motivations are inseparable.

**Where it breaks.** The Mongol Empire's Eurasian scale has no Qing parallel — the Yuan was one node in a connected imperial system that the Qing was not. The Yuan's explicit ethnic stratification (four-class system) was more severe and legally formalized than anything in Qing ethnic policy. The Yuan's suspension of the examination system represents a rupture with Chinese institutional tradition that the Qing never attempted. The existence of an independent Mongolian state creates a live geopolitical dimension to Yuan historiography that has no precise Qing parallel (though Taiwan's ROC claims create some analogous complications). And the Yuan's shorter duration limits the applicability of gradual sinicization arguments in ways that sharpen rather than reproduce the Qing debates.

The most productive conclusion the parallel enables is this: the sinicization debate is not a peculiarity of Qing historiography generated by Western academic revisionism. It is a **structural feature of conquest dynasty historiography** that recurs whenever a non-Han ruling house governs China for an extended period. NQH's intervention in Qing studies is the latest iteration of a debate that Chinese historians have been conducting, in different registers, since the Song dynasty lost north China to the Jurchen Jin in 1127. What NQH added was new sources, new theoretical frameworks, and new political stakes — not a new question.
