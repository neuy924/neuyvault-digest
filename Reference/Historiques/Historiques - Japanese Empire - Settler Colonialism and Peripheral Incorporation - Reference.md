# Settler Frontiers and Imperial Peripheries in Modern Japan

## Abstract in English

This essay examines how modern Japan incorporated peripheral peoples and territories through overlapping logics of settler colonialism, assimilation, and empire. Beginning with the history of 琉球（Ryūkyū） / 沖縄（Okinawa）, it expands to compare アイヌ（Ainu） / 北海道（Hokkaidō）, 樺太（Karafuto）, 千島列島（Chishima-rettō）, 小笠原諸島（Ogasawara-shotō）, 台湾（Taiwan）, 朝鮮（Chōsen / Korea）, and 南洋群島（Nan’yō Guntō）. The central argument is that Japan did not absorb its peripheries through a single model. Hokkaidō is best understood through settler colonialism, where land seizure, migration, and the suppression of Ainu lifeways were central. Okinawa is better read as an annexed kingdom transformed into an assimilated prefecture and later a militarized periphery. Taiwan and Korea belong more clearly to formal empire, although assimilationist and settler-colonial elements appeared within them, especially in Indigenous Taiwanese territories. Using Patrick Wolfe, Ann Laura Stoler, Benedict Anderson, and Rogers Brubaker, the essay shows how land, memory, mapping, classification, nationalization, and imperial residue interacted in the making of modern Japanese sovereignty. The result is a comparative framework that avoids flattening Ainu, Ryukyuan, Korean, Taiwanese, and Pacific Islander histories into one generic story of Japanese expansion.

## Okinawa History — intent: historical overview | lenses: empire, maritime trade, identity | 2026-04-26 22:39 Dublin | #Ryukyu #Okinawa #EastAsia

### Topic map

Okinawa’s history is best understood as **not simply “Japanese island history”**, but as the history of the **Ryukyu Islands** — 琉球諸島（りゅうきゅうしょとう, Ryūkyū-shotō; Okinawan: ルーチュー, Rūchū） — a maritime world between **Japan, China, Korea, Taiwan, and Southeast Asia**. Its key arc is:

**indigenous island societies → Ryukyu Kingdom → dual subordination to China and Japan → Japanese annexation → militarization and devastation in World War II → U.S. occupation → reversion to Japan → continuing base politics and identity debates.**

---

# 1. Early Okinawa before the Ryukyu Kingdom

The earliest human presence in the Ryukyu Islands goes back tens of thousands of years. One major prehistoric find is the **Minatogawa human remains** — 港川人（みなとがわじん, Minatogawa-jin） — discovered on Okinawa Island.

Early Okinawan societies were shaped by island ecology: coral reefs, fishing, shell exchange, small-scale agriculture, and maritime movement. Before the rise of centralized rule, the islands were divided into local communities led by regional chiefs.

A key archaeological-cultural phase is often called the **Gusuku period** — グスク時代（ぐすくじだい, Gusuku jidai）. The word **gusuku** refers to fortified sites or sacred-political enclosures, written in Japanese as 城（ぐすく, gusuku）. These were not merely castles in the European sense; they combined military, ritual, elite residence, and political functions.

| Period                             |      Approximate dates | Main features                                       |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------: | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Prehistoric / shell mound cultures | Before c. 10th century | Fishing, shell exchange, small communities          |
| Gusuku period                      | c. 11th–15th centuries | Fortified centers, regional chiefs, trade expansion |
| Early Ryukyu state formation       |    14th–15th centuries | Emergence of larger polities and overseas diplomacy |

---

# 2. The Three Kingdoms period: 三山時代（さんざんじだい, Sanzan jidai）

Before unification, Okinawa Island was divided into three major polities, known collectively as **三山（さんざん, Sanzan）**, “the Three Mountains”:

| Kingdom         | Native/Japanese name | Location         | Notes                                        |
| --------------- | -------------------- | ---------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Northern polity | 北山（ほくざん, Hokuzan）    | Northern Okinawa | More mountainous, less commercially dominant |
| Central polity  | 中山（ちゅうざん, Chūzan）    | Central Okinawa  | Became the main base of unification          |
| Southern polity | 南山（なんざん, Nanzan）     | Southern Okinawa | Rival to Chūzan                              |

The central polity, **Chūzan** — 中山（ちゅうざん, Chūzan） — became especially important because it developed diplomatic relations with Ming China, 明（みん, Min; Chinese: Míng）.

The Ming court incorporated Ryukyu into the **tribute system** — 冊封体制（さっぽうたいせい, sappō taisei; Chinese: cèfēng tǐzhì）. This did not mean direct Chinese rule. It meant formal recognition of the Ryukyu ruler by the Chinese emperor, in exchange for tribute missions and diplomatic-commercial privileges.

---

# 3. Unification under Shō Hashi: 尚巴志（しょうはし, Shō Hashi）

The key figure in Ryukyuan unification was **Shō Hashi** — 尚巴志（しょうはし, Shō Hashi）.

In the early 15th century, Shō Hashi conquered the rival northern and southern polities and unified Okinawa Island. In 1429, he is traditionally credited with founding the unified **Ryukyu Kingdom** — 琉球王国（りゅうきゅうおうこく, Ryūkyū Ōkoku; Okinawan: 琉球國, Ruuchuu-kuku / Rūchū-kuku）。

The capital developed around **Shuri** — 首里（しゅり, Shuri; Okinawan: スイ, Sui） — and its royal palace-fortress, **Shuri Castle** — 首里城（しゅりじょう / すいぐすく, Shuri-jō / Sui-gusuku）。

### Established point

Ryukyu was a **maritime kingdom**, not a land empire. Its power came from diplomacy, trade brokerage, ritual monarchy, and control over island networks.

---

# 4. Golden age of the Ryukyu Kingdom

From the 15th to 16th centuries, the Ryukyu Kingdom became a major intermediary in East and Southeast Asian trade.

Ryukyuan ships connected:

| Region                                    | Goods / role                          |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Ming China 明（みん, Min）                     | Silk, ceramics, investiture diplomacy |
| Japan 日本（にほん / にっぽん, Nihon / Nippon）      | Swords, silver, textiles              |
| Korea 朝鮮（ちょうせん, Chōsen）                   | Diplomatic and commercial contacts    |
| Southeast Asia 東南アジア（とうなんアジア, Tōnan Ajia） | Spices, aromatics, luxury goods       |
| Taiwan 台湾（たいわん, Taiwan） and island routes | Maritime movement and exchange        |

The kingdom’s identity was cosmopolitan. Its elite culture absorbed Chinese, Japanese, Buddhist, Confucian, and local Ryukyuan elements.

Important institutions and cultural forms included:

| Term             | Script + transliteration                   | Meaning                                |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| Ryukyu Kingdom   | 琉球王国（りゅうきゅうおうこく, Ryūkyū Ōkoku）             | Independent kingdom before annexation  |
| Shuri            | 首里（しゅり, Shuri / Okinawan: Sui）             | Royal capital                          |
| Shuri Castle     | 首里城（しゅりじょう / すいぐすく, Shuri-jō / Sui-gusuku） | Royal palace-fortress                  |
| Noro priestesses | 祝女（のろ, noro）                               | Female ritual specialists              |
| Kikoe-ōgimi      | 聞得大君（きこえおおきみ, Kikoe-ōgimi）                 | Highest-ranking priestess, often royal |

A major point: Ryukyu’s monarchy was sacral. Female ritual authority, especially through **noro** and **kikoe-ōgimi**, played a central role in legitimating royal power.

---

# 5. Satsuma invasion, 1609: 薩摩侵攻（さつましんこう, Satsuma shinkō）

A decisive rupture came in 1609, when the **Satsuma Domain** — 薩摩藩（さつまはん, Satsuma-han） — from southern Kyushu invaded Ryukyu.

Satsuma was ruled by the **Shimazu clan** — 島津氏（しまづし, Shimazu-shi）. After the invasion, Ryukyu did not disappear as a kingdom, but it became subordinated to Satsuma and, indirectly, to the Tokugawa shogunate — 徳川幕府（とくがわばくふ, Tokugawa bakufu）.

This produced Ryukyu’s famous **dual subordination**:

| External power                                           | Relationship                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ming/Qing China 明・清（みん・しん, Min/Shin; Chinese: Míng/Qīng） | Ryukyu continued tribute and investiture relations              |
| Satsuma / Tokugawa Japan 薩摩・徳川日本（さつま・とくがわにほん）            | Ryukyu paid tribute, taxes, and came under political constraint |

This was politically useful for Japan. By keeping Ryukyu outwardly “foreign,” Satsuma could profit from Ryukyu’s China trade while avoiding direct disruption of Sino-Japanese diplomatic sensitivities.

### Interpretation

Ryukyu after 1609 was neither fully independent nor fully absorbed. It was a **buffer kingdom**, a semi-autonomous tributary monarchy under Japanese coercive control while maintaining Chinese diplomatic rituals.

---

# 6. Meiji annexation: 琉球処分（りゅうきゅうしょぶん, Ryūkyū shobun）

The next rupture came with the modern Japanese nation-state.

After the **Meiji Restoration** — 明治維新（めいじいしん, Meiji Ishin） — Japan began converting ambiguous frontier zones into formal national territory. Ryukyu was targeted in this process.

Key steps:

|       Year | Event                                                                              |
| ---------: | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
|       1872 | Ryukyu Kingdom reclassified as **Ryukyu Domain** — 琉球藩（りゅうきゅうはん, Ryūkyū-han）       |
|       1879 | Kingdom abolished; **Okinawa Prefecture** — 沖縄県（おきなわけん, Okinawa-ken） — established |
| After 1879 | Last king, **Shō Tai** — 尚泰（しょうたい, Shō Tai） — removed to Tokyo                     |

This process is called **琉球処分（りゅうきゅうしょぶん, Ryūkyū shobun）**, often translated as the “Ryukyu Disposition.”

From the Japanese state’s perspective, this was administrative integration. From a Ryukyuan nationalist or postcolonial perspective, it was annexation and the destruction of a kingdom.

---

# 7. Assimilation into modern Japan

After annexation, Okinawa underwent policies of cultural and linguistic assimilation.

The Japanese state promoted standard Japanese — 標準語（ひょうじゅんご, hyōjungo） — and marginalized Ryukyuan languages, including Okinawan — 沖縄語（おきなわご, Okinawa-go; Okinawan: ウチナーグチ, Uchinaaguchi）。

A notorious symbol of assimilation was the **dialect tag** — 方言札（ほうげんふだ, hōgen fuda） — used in schools to punish children for speaking local languages.

| Domain            | Assimilation pressure                                             |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Language          | Suppression of Ryukyuan languages; promotion of standard Japanese |
| Education         | Imperial curriculum and loyalty to emperor                        |
| Religion / ritual | Local practices reclassified as backward or folkloric             |
| Identity          | Pressure to become fully “Japanese”                               |
| Economy           | Peripheral, poorer prefecture within Japan                        |

### Established point

Okinawans became Japanese citizens, but not on equal terms. They were often viewed by mainland Japanese as culturally backward, racially ambiguous, or insufficiently assimilated.

---

# 8. World War II and the Battle of Okinawa: 沖縄戦（おきなわせん, Okinawa-sen）

The most traumatic event in modern Okinawan history was the **Battle of Okinawa** — 沖縄戦（おきなわせん, Okinawa-sen） — in 1945.

It was one of the bloodiest battles of the Pacific War. Okinawa became the battlefield between Imperial Japan and the United States. Civilians were trapped between Japanese military coercion and American bombardment.

Key themes:

| Theme            | Significance                                                                                            |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Civilian deaths  | A large proportion of Okinawan civilians died                                                           |
| Militarization   | Okinawa was treated as a defensive buffer for mainland Japan                                            |
| Mass suicides    | Some civilians were pressured or coerced into suicide by Japanese military ideology and fear of capture |
| Memory politics  | Competing narratives over Japanese military responsibility remain contentious                           |
| Postwar identity | The battle deepened Okinawan distrust of both militarism and mainland state policy                      |

The Japanese term **捨て石（すていし, suteishi）**, “sacrificial stone,” is often used in Okinawan memory to describe how Okinawa was sacrificed to delay invasion of the Japanese mainland.

### Interpretation

The Battle of Okinawa turned a peripheral island into a catastrophic military frontier. This experience remains central to Okinawan pacifism and anti-base politics.

---

# 9. U.S. occupation: アメリカ統治（アメリカとうち, Amerika tōchi）

After 1945, Okinawa did not return immediately to Japanese administration. It came under U.S. control.

From 1945 to 1972, Okinawa was governed by the United States, eventually through the **United States Civil Administration of the Ryukyu Islands** — 琉球列島米国民政府（りゅうきゅうれっとうべいこくみんせいふ, Ryūkyū Rettō Beikoku Min Seifu）, often abbreviated **USCAR**.

During this period, the U.S. built and expanded military bases. Land was acquired, sometimes coercively, in what Okinawans remember as **銃剣とブルドーザー（じゅうけんとブルドーザー, jūken to burudōzā）**, “bayonets and bulldozers.”

| Period      | Political status                                                                                   |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1945–1952   | U.S. military occupation after Battle of Okinawa                                                   |
| 1952        | Treaty of San Francisco restored sovereignty to mainland Japan but left Okinawa under U.S. control |
| 1950s–1960s | Expansion of U.S. bases                                                                            |
| 1972        | Reversion to Japan                                                                                 |

The occupation created a paradox: Okinawans were culturally and historically linked to Japan, but were separated from postwar Japanese sovereignty for 27 years.

---

# 10. Reversion to Japan: 沖縄返還（おきなわへんかん, Okinawa henkan）

Okinawa reverted to Japanese administration on 15 May 1972. This is called **沖縄返還（おきなわへんかん, Okinawa henkan）**.

However, reversion did not remove the military base burden. A large proportion of U.S. military facilities in Japan remained concentrated in Okinawa.

This created the central contradiction of post-1972 Okinawa:

> Okinawa returned to Japan politically, but remained structurally militarized.

Major base-related issues include:

| Issue                  | Meaning                                                                    |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| U.S. bases             | Disproportionate concentration in Okinawa                                  |
| Futenma                | 普天間飛行場（ふてんまひこうじょう, Futenma Hikōjō）; controversial Marine Corps air station |
| Henoko                 | 辺野古（へのこ, Henoko）; planned relocation site opposed by many Okinawans        |
| Noise and accidents    | Aircraft crashes, pollution, safety concerns                               |
| Crime and jurisdiction | Tensions over crimes involving U.S. personnel                              |
| Democratic deficit     | Local opposition often overridden by Tokyo-Washington security policy      |

# 11. Okinawan identity today

Modern Okinawan identity is layered rather than simple.

| Identity layer     | Script + transliteration                       | Meaning                              |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Okinawan           | 沖縄人（おきなわじん, Okinawa-jin） / ウチナーンチュ（Uchinānchu） | Local Okinawan identity              |
| Ryukyuan           | 琉球人（りゅうきゅうじん, Ryūkyū-jin）                      | Broader historical-cultural identity |
| Japanese           | 日本人（にほんじん, Nihon-jin）                          | National citizenship identity        |
| Diasporic Okinawan | 世界のウチナーンチュ（せかいのウチナーンチュ, Sekai no Uchinānchu）   | Global Okinawan diaspora             |

The term **ウチナーンチュ（Uchinānchu）** means Okinawan person. The mainland Japanese are often called **ヤマトンチュ（Yamatonchu）**, from 大和（やまと, Yamato）, referring to mainland Japan.

Okinawan identity is not necessarily anti-Japanese. Many Okinawans are both Okinawan and Japanese. But the historical memory of annexation, language suppression, wartime sacrifice, and base concentration gives Okinawan identity a distinct political edge.

# Core interpretation

Okinawa’s history is a history of **frontier incorporation**.

It was first a maritime kingdom between larger powers. Then it became a **buffer state** between China and Japan. Then it was absorbed into the Japanese nation-state. Then it became a battlefield. Then it became a U.S. military platform. Today, it is formally a Japanese prefecture but historically and politically carries the memory of a former kingdom, a colonized periphery, and a militarized frontier.

The central tension is this:

> Okinawa is legally Japanese, historically Ryukyuan, culturally hybrid, and geopolitically treated as a military threshold.

Yes. Okinawa / Ryukyu was one major case, but not the only one. The modern Japanese state incorporated several **peripheral peoples, regions, and borderlands** into a mainland-centered national order. The most important comparison cases are:

1. **Ainu / Ezochi / Hokkaidō** — アイヌ（Ainu）, 蝦夷地（えぞち, Ezochi）, 北海道（ほっかいどう, Hokkaidō）

5. **Karafuto / Sakhalin** — 樺太（からふと, Karafuto）
6. **Taiwan and Korea** as formal colonies — 台湾（たいわん, Taiwan）, 朝鮮（ちょうせん, Chōsen）

A cleaner distinction is: **internal frontier incorporation** versus **overseas empire**.

# 1. Ainu and Hokkaidō: アイヌ（Ainu） and 北海道（Hokkaidō）

The closest comparison to Okinawa is the incorporation of the **Ainu** — アイヌ（Ainu） — and their lands in what Japanese sources historically called **Ezochi** — 蝦夷地（えぞち, Ezochi）, “land of the Ezo/Ainu.”

Before modern annexation, northern Honshū and Hokkaidō were frontier zones of trade, conflict, and dependency between the Japanese polity and Ainu communities. The **Matsumae Domain** — 松前藩（まつまえはん, Matsumae-han） — controlled trade with Ainu groups but did not initially govern all of Hokkaidō in the way a modern state would.

In 1869, after the Meiji Restoration, Ezochi was renamed **Hokkaidō** — 北海道（ほっかいどう, Hokkaidō） — and brought under direct state colonization through the **Hokkaidō Development Commission** — 開拓使（かいたくし, Kaitakushi）.

| Dimension                | Ainu / Hokkaidō case                                                                       |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Pre-incorporation status | Indigenous societies in northern island and borderland networks                            |
| Japanese frontier agent  | 松前藩（Matsumae-han）                                                                          |
| Modern incorporation     | 1869 renaming and settler-colonial development                                             |
| Assimilation method      | Land seizure, agriculture policy, Japanese-language education, suppression of Ainu customs |
| Key issue                | Indigenous dispossession and cultural assimilation                                         |

### Interpretation

Hokkaidō is best understood as Japan’s clearest case of **settler colonialism**. Unlike Okinawa, which had a centralized kingdom, Ainu society was not organized as a single kingdom-state. Incorporation therefore took the form of **indigenous land absorption**, resource extraction, and settler migration.

---

# 2. Ryukyu and Okinawa: 琉球（Ryūkyū） and 沖縄（Okinawa）

Ryukyu was different from Hokkaidō because it had a literate monarchy, diplomacy, taxation, court culture, and long-standing relations with China.

| Dimension                | Ryukyu / Okinawa case                               |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Pre-incorporation status | Kingdom: 琉球王国（りゅうきゅうおうこく, Ryūkyū Ōkoku）             |
| First subordination      | 1609 薩摩侵攻（さつましんこう, Satsuma shinkō）                  |
| Formal annexation        | 1879 琉球処分（りゅうきゅうしょぶん, Ryūkyū shobun）                |
| Assimilation method      | Education, language suppression, prefecturalization |
| Key issue                | Former kingdom absorbed into Japanese nation-state  |

# 3. Ogasawara / Bonin Islands: 小笠原諸島（Ogasawara-shotō）

The **Ogasawara Islands** — 小笠原諸島（おがさわらしょとう, Ogasawara-shotō）, also known historically as the **Bonin Islands** — are another unusual case.

They were remote Pacific islands with a mixed population before firm Japanese incorporation, including people of European, American, Pacific Islander, and later Japanese descent. Japan formally incorporated them in the 19th century.

| Dimension                | Ogasawara case                                                      |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pre-incorporation status | Sparsely populated Pacific islands with mixed settler population    |
| Japanese incorporation   | Meiji-period assertion of sovereignty                               |
| Cultural issue           | Mixed local population gradually absorbed into Japanese nationality |
| Strategic role           | Pacific maritime frontier                                           |
| Later history            | U.S. occupation after WWII; return to Japan in 1968                 |

### Interpretation

Ogasawara is not exactly like Ryukyu or Hokkaidō. It is better seen as **maritime frontier incorporation**: small population, strategic geography, and nationality reclassification.

The **Kuril Islands** — 千島列島（ちしまれっとう, Chishima-rettō） — were another northern frontier zone involving Ainu, Japanese, and Russian expansion.

The southern Kurils had Ainu populations and became entangled in Japanese-Russian boundary-making. In the 19th century, treaties between Japan and Russia defined control over the Kurils and Sakhalin.

### Interpretation

The Kuril case is partly about **indigenous incorporation**, but also about **imperial border-making** between Japan and Russia. The indigenous Ainu presence is often overshadowed by the modern territorial dispute.

**Karafuto** — 樺太（からふと, Karafuto）, southern Sakhalin — was another northern imperial frontier. It involved Ainu, Nivkh, Uilta/Orok, Japanese settlers, and Russian/Soviet power.

| Population policy    | Japanese settlement, resource extraction, assimilation of indigenous groups |
| End                  | Soviet takeover in 1945                                                     |

### Interpretation

Karafuto resembles a **colonial resource frontier** more than a stable internal periphery. It was Japanese-administered but not incorporated into postwar Japan.

# 6. Taiwan: 台湾（Taiwan）

Taiwan — 台湾（たいわん, Taiwan） — was Japan’s first major overseas colony, acquired after the First Sino-Japanese War through the Treaty of Shimonoseki — 下関条約（しものせきじょうやく, Shimonoseki Jōyaku） — in 1895.

Taiwan included Han Chinese settlers and multiple Indigenous peoples, called in Japanese colonial terminology **高砂族（たかさごぞく, Takasago-zoku）**, now an outdated and colonial term.

| End                      | 1945, after Japan’s defeat                                             |

### Comparison with Okinawa

Taiwan was not turned into an ordinary Japanese prefecture in the same way as Okinawa. It was governed as a colony. But the assimilation logic had similarities: Japanese language education, imperial subject formation, and pressure toward loyalty to the emperor — 天皇（てんのう, Tennō）.

# 7. Korea: 朝鮮（Chōsen）

Korea — 朝鮮（ちょうせん, Chōsen; Korean: 조선, Joseon） — was annexed by Japan in 1910 after a period of protectorate control.

| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

Korea differs from Okinawa because Korea was a large neighboring state with its own long literary, bureaucratic, and diplomatic tradition. Its annexation was far more geopolitically central to Japanese imperial expansion.

### Interpretation

Korea was not merely a “peripheral group incorporated by mainland Japan.” It was a colonized nation-state. It belongs to the history of **Japanese imperialism**, not just internal incorporation.

# Core interpretation

Okinawa was part of a broader Japanese pattern of **frontier consolidation and imperial expansion**. But each case differed.

**Hokkaidō / Ainu** was the clearest indigenous settler-colonial case.
**Ryukyu / Okinawa** was the annexation of a former maritime kingdom.
**Ogasawara** was maritime frontier absorption.
**Karafuto and the Kurils** were northern borderland-imperial cases.
**Taiwan, Korea, and Micronesia** were formal overseas colonies or mandates.

The mistake would be to treat all these cases as identical. The better model is a spectrum:

> **internal frontier → annexed kingdom → colonial borderland → formal overseas empire**

## Comparative History of Japanese Peripheral Incorporation — intent: prose explanation | lenses: empire, assimilation, frontier | 2026-04-27 20:04 Dublin | #ComparativeHistory #JapaneseEmpire #Peripheries

Comparative history of Japanese peripheral incorporation asks how the modern Japanese state absorbed, annexed, colonized, or subordinated peoples and territories that stood at the edges of the mainland-centred polity. The topic includes 琉球（りゅうきゅう, Ryūkyū）／沖縄（おきなわ, Okinawa）, アイヌ（Ainu） lands in 北海道（ほっかいどう, Hokkaidō）, 樺太（からふと, Karafuto / southern Sakhalin）, 千島列島（ちしまれっとう, Chishima-rettō / Kurils）, 小笠原諸島（おがさわらしょとう, Ogasawara-shotō）, 台湾（たいわん, Taiwan）, 朝鮮（ちょうせん, Chōsen / Korea）, and 南洋群島（なんようぐんとう, Nan’yō Guntō / South Seas Mandate）. These cases should not be flattened into one generic story of “Japanese expansion.” They represent different mechanisms of incorporation: settler colonialism, annexation, prefecturalization, formal empire, military occupation, and assimilation.

The central comparison begins with the distinction between **internal frontier incorporation** and **formal overseas empire**. 北海道（Hokkaidō） and 沖縄（Okinawa） became parts of the Japanese state in a domestic administrative sense, although by very different routes. Taiwan and Korea, by contrast, were ruled as formal colonies of the Japanese empire. 樺太（Karafuto） and the Kurils occupied a more unstable borderland position, shaped by Japanese-Russian rivalry, settlement, extraction, and eventual loss after 1945. The South Seas Mandate was neither a prefecture nor a conventional colony in the same way as Taiwan or Korea; it was a mandate territory that Japan increasingly militarized as part of its Pacific empire.

The Ainu/Hokkaidō case is the clearest example of Japanese settler colonialism. Before Meiji incorporation, the northern island was commonly referred to as 蝦夷地（えぞち, Ezochi）, a frontier region associated with Ainu societies and trade relations mediated through domains such as 松前藩（まつまえはん, Matsumae-han）. After 1869, the Meiji state renamed the region 北海道（Hokkaidō） and developed it through settler migration, land reclassification, resource extraction, and military frontier planning. The Ainu were not simply “assimilated” in a cultural sense; their relationship to land, fishing, hunting, mobility, ritual, and community autonomy was structurally attacked. This is why Patrick Wolfe’s framework of settler colonialism is especially useful here: the problem was not only cultural difference, but the removal of Indigenous sovereignty so that land could become Japanese settler space.

Ryukyu/Okinawa followed a different path. 琉球王国（りゅうきゅうおうこく, Ryūkyū Ōkoku / Ryukyu Kingdom） had been a maritime kingdom with diplomatic links to China, Japan, Korea, and Southeast Asia. After the 1609 invasion by 薩摩藩（さつまはん, Satsuma-han）, Ryukyu existed in a condition of dual subordination: outwardly maintaining tributary relations with Ming and Qing China while being constrained by Satsuma and Tokugawa Japan. The decisive modern transformation came with 琉球処分（りゅうきゅうしょぶん, Ryūkyū shobun / Ryukyu Disposition） in 1879, when the Meiji state abolished the kingdom and created 沖縄県（おきなわけん, Okinawa-ken / Okinawa Prefecture). Okinawa was therefore not primarily a settler colony in the Hokkaidō sense. Its core historical wound was the abolition of sovereignty, followed by administrative absorption, linguistic stigmatization, and cultural subordination.

Assimilation was a common tool across these cases, but it operated differently depending on context. In Okinawa, the promotion of 標準語（ひょうじゅんご, hyōjungo / standard Japanese） and the stigmatization of Ryukyuan languages such as ウチナーグチ（Uchinaaguchi / Okinawan） were central. The 方言札（ほうげんふだ, hōgen fuda / dialect tag） became a symbol of linguistic discipline. In Ainu contexts, assimilation was tied more directly to land loss and the destruction of subsistence practices. In Korea and Taiwan, assimilation intensified under imperial rule, especially through 皇民化（こうみんか, kōminka / imperial-subjectification), which aimed to make colonial subjects into loyal members of the emperor-centred empire while preserving unequal colonial hierarchy.

Taiwan and Korea clarify the difference between incorporation and empire. 台湾（Taiwan） was acquired in 1895 after the First Sino-Japanese War and became Japan’s first major overseas colony. 朝鮮（Chōsen / Korea） was annexed in 1910 after a period of protectorate control. Both were subject to colonial bureaucracy, policing, education, economic extraction, and assimilationist pressure. Yet they differed from Okinawa because they were not simply converted into ordinary prefectures. They were ruled through imperial institutions and legal hierarchies. Korea especially was not a peripheral island kingdom or an Indigenous frontier; it was a long-established state with its own monarchy, literate bureaucracy, diplomatic tradition, and national consciousness. Its annexation therefore belongs more clearly to the history of imperial conquest.

Karafuto and the Kurils complicate the picture because they were borderland frontiers rather than stable domestic peripheries or straightforward colonies. 樺太（Karafuto / southern Sakhalin） became Japanese after the Russo-Japanese War and was administered as a resource frontier with settler and extractive dimensions. Indigenous peoples including Ainu, Uilta/Orok, and Nivkh were caught between Japanese and Russian/Soviet imperial pressures. 千島列島（Chishima-rettō / Kurils） similarly involved Japanese-Russian boundary-making, Indigenous displacement, and later territorial dispute. These cases show that Japanese expansion was not only directed southward toward Okinawa, Taiwan, and the Pacific, but also northward into a contested Russo-Japanese frontier zone.

The South Seas Mandate, 南洋群島（Nan’yō Guntō）, represents another form of imperial incorporation. Japan acquired these Pacific islands after World War I under a League of Nations mandate, then gradually transformed them into plantation, settlement, and military spaces. This was empire through international mandate, settler migration, economic extraction, and strategic militarization. It differs from Hokkaidō because it never became a settled domestic frontier fully naturalized as “Japan”; it differs from Korea and Taiwan because its legal basis and population structure were different; and it differs from Okinawa because it was not an annexed kingdom incorporated into the prefectural system.

Theorists help separate these patterns. Patrick Wolfe is strongest for Ainu/Hokkaidō, where settler colonialism involved the structural elimination of Indigenous autonomy for the sake of land. Ann Laura Stoler is especially useful for Okinawa, Korea, Taiwan, and the South Seas because she directs attention to imperial afterlives: military bases, ruins, memory, administrative residues, archives, environmental damage, and durable inequality. Benedict Anderson helps explain how states made peripheries imaginable through maps, censuses, schools, museums, and printed categories. Rogers Brubaker is useful for thinking about nationalizing states and minority management, especially in Korea and Taiwan, though his model fits Ainu and Okinawa less cleanly because Indigenous sovereignty and annexed-kingdom histories are not identical to national-minority politics.

The most important comparative lesson is that Japanese peripheral incorporation was not a single process. It was a variable toolkit. Where land and settlement were central, as in Hokkaidō, the logic was settler-colonial. Where sovereignty had to be abolished and identity remade, as in Ryukyu/Okinawa, the logic was annexation plus assimilation. Where Japan ruled unequal external territories, as in Taiwan and Korea, the logic was formal empire. Where frontier resources and border security mattered, as in Karafuto and the Kurils, incorporation took the form of militarized and extractive borderland rule. Where Pacific reach and strategic depth mattered, as in the South Seas Mandate, empire operated through mandate administration and militarization.

The sharper conclusion is that modern Japan became an empire not only by conquering external territories, but also by redefining internal margins as national space. 北海道（Hokkaidō） became Japanese through settler development. 沖縄（Okinawa） became Japanese through the abolition of Ryukyu sovereignty and the remapping of a kingdom into a prefecture. Taiwan and Korea became Japanese imperial possessions through war, treaty, annexation, and colonial rule. These were different histories, but they shared a common grammar: naming, mapping, schooling, policing, land reform, military use, and the ranking of cultures around a mainland Japanese norm.

# 1. Settler colonialism: land, settlement, replacement

## Definition

**Settler colonialism** is not just foreign rule. It means the colonizing population comes to stay, reorganizes land ownership, and often displaces or marginalizes indigenous inhabitants. The slogan in settler colonial studies is that settler colonialism is a **structure**, not merely an event.

The strongest Japanese case is **アイヌ（Ainu） / 北海道（Hokkaidō）**.

## Ainu / Hokkaidō

Before modern incorporation, Japanese authorities referred to much of the northern island as **蝦夷地（Ezochi）**, associated with Ainu peoples and frontier trade. After the Meiji Restoration, the state renamed it **北海道（Hokkaidō）** in 1869 and developed it through the **開拓使（Kaitakushi / Hokkaidō Development Commission）**.

The logic was recognizably settler-colonial:

| Mechanism                   | Hokkaidō example                                                                                       |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Renaming**                | 蝦夷地（Ezochi） → 北海道（Hokkaidō）                                                                            |
| **Land conversion**         | Ainu land and hunting/fishing zones absorbed into state property and settler agriculture               |
| **Settler migration**       | Mainland Japanese settlers moved north                                                                 |
| **Economic transformation** | Fishing, farming, forestry, mining, military frontier development                                      |
| **Cultural suppression**    | Ainu language, ritual, tattoos, hunting practices, and social structures were targeted or marginalized |
| **Security rationale**      | Northern frontier defense against Russia                                                               |

### Comparison with Okinawa

沖縄（Okinawa） was not settler-colonial in the same pure form. Japan did not replace most Okinawans with mainland settlers. Instead, it abolished a kingdom and converted it into a prefecture. Okinawans remained the majority population. So the Okinawa case is better described as **annexation plus assimilation**, with later militarized internal colonial features.

## Karafuto and Kurils

**樺太（Karafuto / southern Sakhalin）** and the **千島列島（Chishima-rettō / Kuril Islands）** also had settler-colonial elements. Japan promoted settlement, extraction, and frontier administration in territories with indigenous peoples including Ainu, Uilta/Orok, and Nivkh communities. But because Japanese control over Karafuto ended in 1945, it was less fully naturalized into postwar Japan than Hokkaidō.

---

# 2. Assimilation: turning difference into imperial sameness

## Definition

**Assimilation** means the state tries to convert culturally distinct populations into loyal subjects of the dominant nation. It operates through schooling, language, names, law, ritual, military service, and everyday discipline.

Japan used assimilation across nearly all incorporated territories, but intensity and purpose varied.

## Ryukyu / Okinawa

In 琉球（Ryūkyū） / 沖縄（Okinawa）, assimilation followed annexation. The former **琉球王国（Ryūkyū Ōkoku / Ryukyu Kingdom）** was abolished in 1879 through **琉球処分（Ryūkyū shobun / Ryukyu Disposition）**, and **沖縄県（Okinawa-ken / Okinawa Prefecture）** was created.

Assimilation worked through:

| Domain                     | Assimilation pressure                                                    |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Language**               | Standard Japanese 標準語（hyōjungo） promoted; Ryukyuan languages stigmatized |
| **Schooling**              | Imperial education and emperor-centered loyalty                          |
| **Identity**               | Okinawans pressured to become proper 日本人（Nihon-jin / Japanese）           |
| **Status hierarchy**       | Okinawa treated as culturally backward or insufficiently modern          |
| **Military incorporation** | Okinawans later mobilized into the Japanese war state                    |

The notorious **方言札（hōgen fuda / dialect tag）** symbolizes linguistic discipline. Pupils who spoke local languages such as ウチナーグチ（Uchinaaguchi / Okinawan） could be shamed or punished.

## Ainu assimilation

Ainu assimilation was harsher in land terms because it accompanied dispossession. The Japanese state did not merely ask Ainu to become culturally Japanese; it reorganized their subsistence base. Assimilation here was tied to the destruction of independent indigenous lifeways.

## Korea and Taiwan

In **台湾（Taiwan）** and **朝鮮（Chōsen / Korea）**, assimilation had a different status because these were formal colonies, not ordinary prefectures.

Late colonial policies intensified into **皇民化（kōminka / imperial-subjectification）**, especially in the 1930s–1940s. This included Japanese-language promotion, shrine worship, name-changing pressures, and mobilization for empire.

| Case               | Assimilation target                                  | Difference from Okinawa/Hokkaidō                                          |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 沖縄（Okinawa）        | Become Japanese within the nation-state              | Prefecturalized, formally internal                                        |
| アイヌ（Ainu）          | Become Japanese by abandoning indigenous lifeways    | Indigenous dispossession plus cultural erasure                            |
| 台湾（Taiwan）         | Become loyal imperial subjects                       | Colonial hierarchy remained                                               |
| 朝鮮（Chōsen / Korea） | Become imperial subjects under annexed colonial rule | Larger, literate, state-bearing society; deeper anti-colonial nationalism |

# 3. Empire: unequal rule, extraction, and strategic periphery

## Definition

**Empire** means rule over unequal territories and peoples, usually justified by civilization, security, development, or destiny. It need not always involve mass settlement. Empire can rule through bureaucracy, police, military bases, railways, extraction, education, and symbolic hierarchy.

Japan’s empire included both **formal colonies** and **ambiguous internal peripheries**.

## Formal empire: Taiwan and Korea

**台湾（Taiwan）** was acquired in 1895 after the First Sino-Japanese War. **朝鮮（Chōsen / Korea）** was annexed in 1910 after protectorate rule. These were not treated the same as ordinary Japanese prefectures.

| Case               | Type of empire              | Key features                                                                                    |
| ------------------ | --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 台湾（Taiwan）         | First major overseas colony | Policing, infrastructure, sugar/rice extraction, indigenous pacification, education             |
| 朝鮮（Chōsen / Korea） | Annexed colony              | Governor-General rule, land surveys, cultural suppression, industrial and military mobilization |
| 南洋群島（Nan’yō Guntō） | Pacific mandate empire      | Plantation economy, settlement, strategic militarization                                        |

Korea differs sharply from Okinawa and Hokkaidō because it had been a recognized state with its own monarchy, bureaucracy, diplomatic history, literary culture, and national consciousness. Its annexation was not frontier incorporation; it was imperial conquest of a neighboring polity.

## Okinawa as internal empire?

Okinawa is analytically awkward. Legally, after 1879, it became a prefecture. But politically and culturally, it retained features of an internal colony: late incorporation, cultural stigmatization, poverty, and disproportionate military burden.

After 1945, Okinawa’s case changes again. It was under U.S. rule until 1972, then returned to Japan, but the U.S. base burden remained heavily concentrated there. This makes postwar Okinawa a case of **militarized periphery** rather than classic Japanese settler colonialism.

## A. Hokkaidō vs Okinawa

## B. Okinawa vs Korea

Both involved annexation and assimilation, but the scale and category differ.

Taiwan and Okinawa both sit south of Japan and both were linked to maritime frontier logic, but their legal statuses differed.

Taiwan was a showcase colony of empire. Okinawa was an incorporated but unequal periphery.

Both were formal colonies, but Korea was politically more threatening to Japan because it had been an independent state adjacent to Manchuria, China, and Russia.

**Core contrast:**
Taiwan was Japan’s first colonial laboratory. Korea became the core continental colony of imperial Japan.

# 9. Known unknowns and debates

The biggest uncertainty is not chronology; it is **classification**.

**Established:** Japan incorporated or colonized multiple peripheral regions and peoples through different mechanisms.
**Interpretive:** These cases can be arranged on a spectrum from settler colonialism to formal empire.
**Debated:** Whether Okinawa should be called a colony, internal colony, annexed kingdom, or marginalized prefecture remains politically and academically contested.

A strong analytical model should avoid flattening the cases. **Ainu/Hokkaidō** should not be treated as just another prefectural story. **Korea** should not be reduced to regional assimilation. **Okinawa** should not be collapsed into either Korea-style colonialism or Hokkaidō-style settler colonialism. It occupies its own category: **annexed kingdom turned assimilated prefecture turned militarized periphery**.

---

# Conclusion

Japan’s peripheral incorporation can be understood as a layered system:

> **Settler colonialism** explains land seizure and indigenous displacement, especially in アイヌ / 北海道.
> **Assimilation** explains how the state tried to convert Ainu, Ryukyuans, Taiwanese, Koreans, and others into Japanese imperial subjects.
> **Empire** explains unequal rule, extraction, militarization, and hierarchy across Taiwan, Korea, Karafuto, the South Seas, and arguably Okinawa’s later strategic role.

The strongest contrast is this:

| Case      | Core historical wound                                          |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| アイヌ / 北海道 | Land dispossession and indigenous erasure                      |
| 琉球 / 沖縄   | Lost sovereignty, cultural marginalization, militarized burden |
| 台湾        | Colonial rule and developmental extraction                     |
| 朝鮮        | National annexation, cultural suppression, imperial domination |
| 樺太 / 千島   | Borderland displacement and imperial rivalry                   |
| 南洋群島      | Mandate empire, settlement, and militarization                 |

So the answer is not “Japan annexed peripheral groups in one standard way.” It used a **variable toolkit**: settlement where land was central, assimilation where identity had to be remade, and empire where hierarchy, extraction, and strategic control were the priority.

### Topic map

The four theorists give different answers to the same problem:

# 1. Patrick Wolfe: settler colonialism and the logic of elimination

Patrick Wolfe’s key claim is that settler colonialism is **“a structure rather than an event”**; his framework focuses on the continuing logic by which settler societies seek to eliminate Indigenous peoples as peoples, not necessarily always by physical killing. He also stresses that settler colonialism is “inherently eliminatory but not invariably genocidal.” ([Taylor & Francis Online][1])

Applied to Japan, Wolfe is strongest for cases where the state wanted **land** more than labour.

## Best case: アイヌ（Ainu） / 北海道（Hokkaidō）

The Ainu case fits Wolfe unusually well. The Meiji state transformed 蝦夷地（えぞち, Ezochi） into 北海道（ほっかいどう, Hokkaidō）, moved settlers northward, reorganized landholding, restricted Ainu subsistence, and imposed Japanese categories of property, agriculture, schooling, and identity.

| Wolfean variable              | Ainu / Hokkaidō                                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Land centrality               | Very high                                                                       |
| Settler migration             | High                                                                            |
| Indigenous sovereignty erased | High                                                                            |
| Assimilation as elimination   | High                                                                            |
| Continuing structure          | Yes: recognition, land, language, and cultural survival remain political issues |

Wolfe helps explain why “assimilation” is not benign here. For Ainu communities, becoming Japanese was tied to losing autonomous land, subsistence systems, and collective political status.

## Partial case: 樺太（Karafuto） and 千島列島（Chishima-rettō）

樺太（からふと, Karafuto / southern Sakhalin） and 千島列島（ちしまれっとう, Chishima-rettō / Kurils） also fit a Wolfean lens, but less cleanly because Japanese control was time-limited and entangled with Russian/Soviet expansion. Indigenous peoples such as Ainu, Uilta/Orok, and Nivkh were caught between imperial border regimes.

## Weak case: 沖縄（Okinawa）

Okinawa is not primarily Wolfean. Japan did not replace Okinawans demographically in the way settler-colonial models usually emphasize. The main wound was not demographic replacement but **abolition of sovereignty**, linguistic stigmatization, cultural subordination, and later militarized burden.

**Wolfe’s use for Okinawa is limited but not zero:** if “elimination” means eliminating Ryukyuan sovereignty as a separate political identity, then Okinawa has a related logic. But if we keep Wolfe’s land-and-settlement emphasis, Okinawa is not the strongest case.

---

# 2. Ann Laura Stoler: imperial formations, ruins, and durable inequalities

Ann Laura Stoler is useful because she avoids treating empire as something that simply “ended.” Her work on **imperial formations**, **imperial debris**, and **duress** examines how imperial hierarchies continue through institutions, landscapes, affect, archives, bodies, and memory after formal rule changes. In “Imperial Debris,” she explicitly shifts focus from empire as a past object to ongoing processes of decimation and ruination. ([AnthroSource][2])

Stoler is especially strong for **Okinawa**.

## Best case: 沖縄（Okinawa）

Okinawa’s history is layered: 琉球王国（りゅうきゅうおうこく, Ryūkyū Ōkoku / Ryukyu Kingdom） was subordinated by 薩摩藩（さつまはん, Satsuma-han） in 1609, annexed by Meiji Japan in 1879, devastated in 沖縄戦（おきなわせん, Okinawa-sen / Battle of Okinawa） in 1945, governed by the U.S. until 1972, and then returned to Japan while retaining a disproportionate U.S. military base burden.

A Stolerian analysis would not ask only, “Was Okinawa a colony?” It would ask: **what imperial arrangements remain active in the landscape, law, military geography, memory, and everyday life?**

| Stolerian variable  | Okinawa                                                                    |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Imperial debris     | Battle sites, bases, unexploded ordnance, land seizures, memorial politics |
| Ruination           | Civilian trauma, ecological damage, militarized land use                   |
| Durable hierarchy   | Tokyo-Washington security priorities overriding local opposition           |
| Affective afterlife | resentment, pacifism, anti-base politics, ambiguous Japanese belonging     |
| Archive problem     | Whose memory counts: Tokyo, Washington, or Okinawan civilians?             |

Stoler is probably the best theorist for understanding why Okinawa’s incorporation cannot be reduced to 1879 annexation. Okinawa is not only an annexed kingdom; it is a **palimpsest of Japanese imperial incorporation, wartime sacrifice, U.S. military occupation, and post-reversion security externalization**.

## Taiwan and Korea

Stoler also works well for 台湾（たいわん, Taiwan） and 朝鮮（ちょうせん, Chōsen / Korea), but differently. The key issue is not simply colonial rule from 1895–1945 or 1910–1945, but the afterlives of infrastructure, law, education, memory, violence, and imperial nostalgia.

---

# 3. Benedict Anderson: imagined communities, census, map, museum

Benedict Anderson is useful for the symbolic and administrative machinery of nation-building. His famous argument is that nations are **imagined communities**; in his “Census, Map, Museum” chapter, he shows how colonial states classified people, bounded territory, and curated history in ways that later shaped nationalist imagination. ([NAU][3])

Anderson helps explain how Japan made peripheries legible as “Japan.”

## Census: classifying people

Peripheral populations had to be classified. Were they Japanese? Indigenous? Former Ryukyuans? Colonial subjects? Imperial subjects? Household registers, schools, censuses, and legal categories helped convert plural border populations into administratively knowable populations.

| Case                    | Andersonian classification problem                              |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| アイヌ（Ainu）               | Indigenous people reclassified within a Japanese national frame |
| 琉球人（Ryūkyū-jin）         | Former subjects of a kingdom reclassified as Okinawan/Japanese  |
| 台湾人（Taiwan-jin）         | Colonial subjects governed by differential imperial law         |
| 朝鮮人（Chōsen-jin）         | Annexed colonial subjects inside empire but not equal nationals |
| 南洋群島民（Nan’yō guntō-min） | Mandate islanders placed inside Japanese imperial geography     |

## Map: bounding territory

The map is central for Meiji Japan. 蝦夷地（Ezochi） became 北海道（Hokkaidō）; 琉球（Ryūkyū） became 沖縄県（Okinawa-ken）; 樺太（Karafuto), 千島（Chishima), 台湾（Taiwan), and 朝鮮（Chōsen） became mapped into imperial space.

This is where Anderson complements Brubaker. Anderson explains the **imaginary technology**: maps and education make imperial space feel natural. Brubaker explains the **political tension**: minorities and borderlands remain unstable inside nationalizing states.

## Museum: curating ancestry

The museum logic appears in how Japan curated Ainu, Ryukyuan, and colonial cultures as folklore, ethnography, or backward survivals. Once a living people is framed as “heritage,” the state can celebrate culture while neutralizing political claims.

**Anderson’s sharpest insight for Japan:** incorporation is not just military or legal. It is semiotic. The state teaches people how to imagine Hokkaidō, Okinawa, Taiwan, Korea, and the South Seas as parts of a Japanese spatial-historical order.

---

# 4. Rogers Brubaker: nationalizing states, minorities, and homelands

Rogers Brubaker’s key model is the **triadic nexus** linking:

1. a **nationalizing state**,
2. a **national minority** within that state, and
3. an **external national homeland** that may claim concern for that minority. ([IRHIS][4])

His framework was developed for post-Soviet and East European cases, so it does not transfer perfectly. But it is very useful for Japan’s borderlands and colonies if adapted carefully.

## Nationalizing Japan

Meiji Japan was a nationalizing state: it tried to build a unitary Japanese nation out of heterogeneous lands and peoples.

| Brubaker term       | Japanese case                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Nationalizing state | 明治日本（Meiji Nihon / Meiji Japan） and later imperial Japan                                                                               |
| National minority   | Ainu, Ryukyuans/Okinawans, Koreans, Taiwanese, Micronesians                                                                            |
| External homeland   | China for Ryukyu/Taiwan in some periods; Korea for Koreans after 1945; Russia/Soviet Union in northern borderlands; ambiguous for Ainu |

## Where Brubaker works well

Brubaker works especially well for **Korea** and **Taiwan** because the question of external homelands, anti-colonial nationalism, and state-minority relations is obvious.

For 朝鮮（Chōsen / Korea), the Japanese empire tried to make Koreans into imperial subjects, but Korean nationalism persisted and later became tied to liberated Korean states. For 台湾（Taiwan), the external homeland question is more complex because Qing, Chinese nationalist, Japanese colonial, Indigenous, and Taiwanese identities overlap.

## Where Brubaker works less well

Brubaker is less clean for アイヌ（Ainu） because there is no obvious external national homeland. Ainu are better framed through indigeneity and settler colonialism than through Brubaker’s national-minority triad.

For 沖縄（Okinawa), Brubaker is useful but must be modified. Ryukyuans had historical relations with China, but modern Okinawan identity does not map neatly onto an external homeland. Okinawa is better seen as a **minority-region within a nationalizing state**, not a classic national minority backed by an external kin-state.

# 6. What each theorist reveals — and hides

## Patrick Wolfe

**Reveals:** land seizure, settler permanence, Indigenous erasure.
**Best for:** Ainu/Hokkaidō, Karafuto, Indigenous Taiwan.
**Hides:** cases where sovereignty and culture are targeted without demographic replacement.

Wolfe is essential but insufficient. If overused, he makes Okinawa look like a weaker Hokkaidō rather than its own historical formation.

## Ann Laura Stoler

**Reveals:** afterlives, ruins, affect, military landscapes, unfinished empire.
**Best for:** Okinawa, Taiwan, Korea, South Seas.
**Hides:** precise legal distinctions if used too broadly.

Stoler is the best antidote to simplistic “empire ended in 1945” narratives.

## Benedict Anderson

**Reveals:** how states make peoples and territories imaginable through census, map, museum, school, print, and administrative repetition.
**Best for:** all cases.
**Hides:** material violence if treated too culturally.

Anderson is excellent for showing how Ryukyu becomes Okinawa, Ezochi becomes Hokkaidō, and colonies become mapped into Japanese imperial common sense.

## Rogers Brubaker

**Reveals:** nationalizing state pressure, minority instability, homeland politics.
**Best for:** Korea, Taiwan, Okinawa with modification.
**Hides:** Indigenous cases without external homelands.

Brubaker is powerful for national conflicts, less powerful for Indigenous sovereignty unless modified.

# 9. Bottom line

The best theoretical reframing is:

**Ainu/Hokkaidō** is the clearest **Wolfean** case: settler colonialism, land seizure, Indigenous elimination-as-assimilation.
**Okinawa/Ryukyu** is the clearest **Stolerian-Andersonian** case: a former kingdom remapped into Japan, then left with durable imperial and military debris.
**Korea** is the clearest **Brubakerian** case: a colonized nation subjected to nationalizing imperial rule, with homeland politics central after 1945.
**Taiwan** needs all four: colonial state-building, Indigenous dispossession, imperial residue, and unstable national categorization.
**Karafuto/Kurils** combine Wolfean frontier settlement with Brubakerian borderland politics.
**South Seas Mandate** is best read through Stoler and Anderson: imperial imagination, mandate rule, settlement, and militarized ruin.
