## Abstract — English

This conversation surveys the intellectual history of 東洋史 (Japanese Oriental History) as a scholarly discipline, examining its foundational concepts, major theorists, and the theoretical tensions that have structured it from the Meiji period to the present. Beginning with the field's tripartite institutional structure and its embedded political ambiguities, the discussion traces how Japanese historians developed frameworks for understanding Chinese civilization — the Tang-Song transition thesis, steppe-agrarian dynamics, multiethnic empire theory, maritime Asian networks, and East Asian world systems — that were simultaneously rigorous philological achievements and ideologically situated productions shaped by imperial context. Extended attention is given to debates over historical periodization in both Japanese and Chinese historiography, including Marxist stagism, the sprouts of capitalism controversy, and the structural problems of defining Chinese modernity through the 1840 Opium War. The conversation culminates in Mizoguchi Yūzō's epistemological challenge — his demand that Chinese historical experience generate analytical concepts adequate to its own dynamics rather than receiving theory from European or Japanese frameworks — situating 東洋史 within global debates about postcolonial knowledge production and the decentering of Eurocentric historiography.

## Summary

This conversation was a sustained exploration of 東洋史 — Japanese Oriental History — as an academic discipline, tracing its intellectual architecture from its Meiji-era founding through its postwar reconstruction and contemporary theoretical challenges. The discussion opened by establishing the field's foundational tripartite structure: Japan's division of historical study into national history (*kokushi*), Eastern history (*tōyōshi*), and Western history (*seiyōshi*), and the political and epistemological instabilities embedded in that structure from the outset.

The conversation then moved through a systematic mapping of the field's core concepts — the Sino-centric tributary order (華夷秩序), the sinographic cultural sphere (漢字文化圏), the steppe-agrarian dynamic, maritime Asia, the Silk Road, and the constructed nature of "the East" as a category. Each concept was examined not only as an analytical tool but as a historically situated intellectual production shaped by Japan's imperial ambitions, anxieties about modernity, and need to position itself between China and the West.

A central thread running through the entire discussion was the **question of historical agency and the politics of periodization**. The conversation examined how Japanese theorists — Shiratori, Naitō, Miyazaki, Haneda, Kuwabara, Tsuda — developed frameworks for understanding Chinese civilization that were simultaneously rigorous scholarly achievements and ideologically embedded productions. Naitō Konan's Tang-Song transition thesis received extended treatment as the field's most generative and contested contribution: its claims about Chinese early modernity, its reception by American social historians, its challenge by postcolonial critics, and its ultimate status as a productive research agenda even where its interpretive claims remain disputed.

The conversation devoted substantial attention to **periodization debates** — how Chinese historians constructed their own frameworks through dynastic succession, Marxist stagism, the five-stages debates, the sprouts of capitalism controversy, and the post-1978 fragmentation into regional and thematic approaches. This section examined how the 1840 Opium War boundary for Chinese modernity embedded a trauma narrative that made Western imperialism the protagonist of Chinese historical development, and how historians have struggled to escape this structural dependence.

Mizoguchi Yūzō's framework received the deepest theoretical treatment — his critique of sympathetic Eurocentrism in even the best 東洋史 scholarship, his analysis of the immanentization of *li* across Ming-Qing thought, his reconstruction of *gong/si* as a non-liberal framework for collective life, and his programmatic demand in *China as Method* that Chinese historical experience become a *source* of analytical theory rather than merely an object of theorization. This connected the conversation to broader global debates about postcolonial epistemology and the decentering of European knowledge production.

The final major sections provided detailed treatments of Okada Hidehiro's anti-Sinocentric revisionism — his argument that "China" as a continuous entity is nationalist fiction, and the Mongol Empire rather than Chinese civilization is the proper protagonist of East Asian world history — alongside Nishijima Sadao's East Asian World System framework and Haneda Tōru's reconstruction of Tang multiethnic empire and Sogdian network history. A reference table synthesized all nine theorists across dimensions of institutional affiliation, major theories, methodological contributions, and critical legacies.

## What Is 東洋史?

東洋史 (*Tōyōshi*) means "Oriental History" or "Eastern History" — a scholarly discipline that emerged in Meiji-era Japan (late 19th century) as a distinct field separate from both Japanese national history (*Kokushi* 国史) and Western history (*Seiyōshi* 西洋史). It studied China, Central Asia, and the broader Asian world as its subject matter.

---

## The Three-Tradition Framework

Japan's modern historical discipline split into three tracks:

- **国史** — Japan's own national history
- **東洋史** — history of Asia (primarily Sinocentric)
- **西洋史** — Western/European history

This tripartite structure was institutionalized in universities from the 1890s onward and remains the basic architecture of Japanese history departments today.

---

## The "Third Tradition" Problem

The phrase "third tradition" refers to 東洋史 occupying an *unstable middle position*. It is neither the national self-narrative nor the globally dominant Western historiography. It was built partly to assert Asian civilizational dignity, partly to serve imperial Japan's ideological needs in justifying expansion into China and Korea.

---

## Intellectual Tensions

東洋史 developed its own methodology — rigorous philology, source criticism of Chinese texts — but always faced the question: *whose Asia is being described, and for whom?* Japanese scholars defined "the East" in ways that often centered Japan as Asia's modern leader, marginalizing Korea and Southeast Asia.

---

## Legacy and Critique

Post-1945 scholars questioned whether 東洋史 was genuinely comparative or simply imperial cartography dressed as scholarship. Today it is being renegotiated — some push toward a decentered "Asian history" (*Ajia-shi*) that resists both Japanese nationalist framing and Western universalism.

# 東洋史 Core Concepts | #T1 #T2 #T3 | 🔭 [conceptual cartography lens] |

---

## 1. 華夷秩序 — Sino-centric World Order (Tributary System)

The idea that China functioned as the civilizational center of Asia, with surrounding states arranged in hierarchical tribute relationships. Korea, Vietnam, Japan, and Central Asian polities periodically sent envoys bearing gifts; China reciprocated with legitimacy, trade access, and symbolic investiture.

東洋史 scholars examined how this order structured diplomacy, trade, and cultural diffusion across millennia. The system was not purely coercive — peripheral states often manipulated it strategically for their own benefit.

---

## 2. 漢字文化圏 — Sinographic Cultural Sphere

The zone of societies that used Classical Chinese as a written lingua franca — Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and parts of Central Asia. This was not linguistic unity but *textual* unity: elites across very different spoken-language communities shared a common scriptural and philosophical inheritance.

東洋史 treated this sphere as its primary unit of analysis. It raised deep questions about how ideas, institutions, and aesthetics traveled across political boundaries through text rather than conquest.

---

## 3. 遊牧民族 — Nomadic Peoples and the Steppe Frontier

A major corrective emphasis in 東洋史 was taking steppe nomads seriously as historical actors. The Xiongnu, Turks, Mongols, and Manchus were not mere disruptors of Chinese civilization but builders of their own polities with distinct administrative logic.

Scholars like Shiratori Kurakichi and the Kyoto School argued that the dynamic interaction between agricultural China and nomadic Inner Asia was the *engine* of East Asian historical change — a fundamentally different model than sedentary-civilization-centric narratives.

---

## 4. 中華思想 — Sinocentrism as Ideology

Distinct from the structural tributary system, 中華思想 refers to the cultural and philosophical belief in Chinese civilizational supremacy. It shaped how Chinese dynasties conceptualized legitimacy, ethnicity, and the moral geography of the world.

東洋史 examined this as both an object of study and a problem for methodology — because Japanese scholars had to decide whether to adopt, critique, or replace this framework with their own. Post-Meiji 東洋史 often subtly repositioned Japan as the *true* heir of East Asian civilization, displacing China.

---

## 5. 王朝交替 — Dynastic Cycle and Political Legitimacy

The pattern of dynastic rise, consolidation, decline, and collapse was a central structuring concept. 東洋史 asked what forces drove this cycle: peasant rebellion, fiscal exhaustion, nomadic pressure, bureaucratic corruption, or a combination.

This also opened questions about the *transfer of the mandate* across ethnic lines — Mongol Yuan, Manchu Qing — forcing analysis of how non-Han dynasties adapted or rejected Chinese institutional models.

---

## 6. 海域アジア — Maritime Asia

A later but important conceptual development: shifting focus from the continental Sinocentric landmass to the seas — South China Sea, Indian Ocean littoral, maritime Southeast Asia. Trade networks, port cities, and diasporic Chinese merchant communities became central.

This reoriented 東洋史 away from dynastic court history toward economic and social history, and brought Southeast Asia — long marginal in the field — into the picture as an active zone of exchange.

---

## 7. 東西交渉 — East-West Exchange and the Silk Road

How did goods, religions, technologies, and peoples move between China, Central Asia, Persia, and the Mediterranean? 東洋史 placed major emphasis on the Silk Road not as a single road but as a network of overland and maritime corridors.

Buddhism's transmission from India through Central Asia into China and then Japan was a paradigm case — showing how 東洋史 traced cultural diffusion as a historical force in its own right, separate from political or military history.

---

## 8. 近代化と東洋 — Modernity and "the East"

A recurring anxious question: why did Western-style modernity emerge in Europe and not Asia? Was China's Confucian order an obstacle? Was Japan's modernization an exception or a model?

This was never a neutral scholarly question — it was politically charged, feeding both Japanese imperial ideology (Japan as Asia's modernizer) and later postcolonial critiques. Much of 東洋史's internal tension lives here, at the intersection of scholarship and geopolitics.

---

## 9. 地域概念としての「東洋」— "The East" as Constructed Category

Increasingly since the 1980s, scholars have examined "東洋" itself as a constructed, unstable category. Who drew its boundaries? What was excluded — Southeast Asia, South Asia, the Pacific?

This meta-critical turn revealed that 東洋史 was never a neutral description of an objective region but a *political act of naming* — shaped by Meiji modernization anxieties, imperial ambition, and the need to define Japan's position relative to both China and the West.

---

## 10. 歴史主体性 — Historical Agency and Subjectivity

Who are the *subjects* of East Asian history? For much of its early history, 東洋史 focused on states, dynasties, and elites. Later scholarship pushed toward peasants, merchants, women, minority peoples, and colonial subjects.

This shift also raised the postcolonial question of *whose voice* narrates East Asian history — Japanese imperial scholars, Chinese nationalist historians, Western Orientalists, or the communities themselves.

# 東洋史 Theorists on China | #T1 #T2 #T3 | 🔭 [intellectual biography lens] |

---

## 白鳥庫吉 Shiratori Kurakichi (1865–1942) — Founder of Scientific 東洋史

Shiratori is the foundational figure. Trained in German historicism, he applied rigorous philological method to Asian sources, insisting 東洋史 must be empirical science, not Confucian moral narrative. He established the discipline institutionally at Tokyo Imperial University.

On China specifically, Shiratori was skeptical and demythologizing. He subjected Chinese foundational myths — the Yellow Emperor, ancient sage-kings — to source criticism and argued they were largely fabrications or later accretions. This was radical: Chinese historiography treated these as historical fact. Shiratori treated them as ideology.

His deeper argument was that China was fundamentally *reactive* — shaped by constant pressure from northern nomadic peoples rather than developing through internal logic alone. Chinese civilization was not self-generating but forged in confrontation with the steppe. This positioned Inner Asia, not China, as the true engine of East Asian history.

---

## 内藤湖南 Naitō Konan (1866–1934) — The Tang-Song Transition Theory

Naitō is arguably the most intellectually influential 東洋史 thinker on Chinese civilization. A Kyoto School founder, he developed the **Tang-Song transition thesis** — one of the most debated frameworks in East Asian historiography.

His argument: the Tang-Song transition (roughly 8th–10th centuries) was a rupture as significant as any in world history. Tang China was aristocratic, closed, and medieval. Song China was something new — a commercial economy, a meritocratic bureaucracy via expanded civil examinations, urbanization, paper money, and popular culture. China in effect entered its own "early modernity" around 1000 CE.

This had radical implications. It meant China's modernity was *endogenous*, not dependent on Western contact. It also implied that by the time Europe was modernizing, China had already passed through an equivalent transformation — raising the question of why China did not sustain that trajectory. Naitō's framework directly challenged Eurocentric linear progress narratives.

---

## 宮崎市定 Miyazaki Ichisada (1901–1995) — World History and Chinese Universalism

Naitō's greatest student extended the Tang-Song thesis into a comprehensive theory of Chinese historical development. Miyazaki argued that China should be understood through genuine *world-historical* comparison — not as exotic Other but as a parallel civilization whose developmental stages illuminate universal historical processes.

On the examination system (*科挙*), Miyazaki wrote its definitive study. He argued the *keju* was the world's first meritocratic institution — a revolutionary social technology that continuously recycled elites and prevented permanent aristocratic entrenchment. This made Chinese imperial governance more dynamic and socially open than contemporary European feudalism.

Miyazaki also theorized Chinese economic history seriously — Song monetization, Ming-Qing commercial expansion — arguing China had proto-capitalist dynamics centuries before Weber's Protestant capitalism. His work implicitly challenged the "why not capitalism in China?" question by showing capitalism's preconditions *were* present.

---

## 桑原隲蔵 Kuwabara Jitsuzō (1871–1931) — China-Islam Connections and Maritime Trade

Kuwabara focused on the intersections between Chinese civilization and the Islamic world, particularly through Tang-Song maritime trade. His meticulous study of Pu Shougeng — a Muslim merchant official who controlled Quanzhou's trade and defected to the Mongols — opened the history of China's cosmopolitan port cities.

His broader argument was that Chinese civilization was never hermetically sealed. The Tang capital Chang'an hosted Nestorian Christians, Zoroastrians, Manicheans, and Muslim merchants. China's historical openness to foreign religious and commercial communities was systematically underexamined. Kuwabara made the case for a China deeply embedded in Eurasian networks.

---

## 津田左右吉 Tsuda Sōkichi (1873–1961) — Radical Source Criticism

Tsuda applied the most corrosive philological skepticism to Chinese classical texts. He argued that the *Confucian classics* as historical sources were largely retrospective constructions — the Zhou dynasty's political philosophy projected backward onto a mythologized antiquity.

This was intellectually explosive. If the classics were not reliable historical records, the entire edifice of Chinese civilization's self-understanding was built on constructed tradition. Tsuda's method was more destructive than constructive — he was better at dismantling claims than building alternative narratives — but his rigor forced the field to confront its source problems directly.

He applied similar criticism to Japanese sources, which eventually got him prosecuted under wartime nationalist laws — revealing how politically dangerous rigorous source criticism could become.

---

## 羽田亨 Haneda Tōru (1882–1955) — Central Asia and the Multiethnic Empire

Haneda specialized in Central Asian history and Islamic sources, expanding 東洋史 beyond its Sinocentric default. His central argument about China was that the great Chinese empires — Han, Tang, Yuan, Qing — were multiethnic, multilingual imperial formations that cannot be understood as simply "Chinese."

The Tang dynasty in particular, he argued, was substantially non-Han in its ruling elite, cultural influences, and military structure. "China" as an ethnic-civilizational category was a retrospective nationalist imposition onto what were actually fluid, cosmopolitan imperial systems. This was an early version of what would later become the "New Qing History" debate.

---

## 西嶋定生 Nishijima Sadao (1919–1998) — The East Asian World System

Nishijima developed the concept of an integrated **East Asian world** (*東アジア世界*) structured around four elements: Chinese writing, Confucianism, Buddhism, and the tributary system. These four formed a coherent civilizational package that defined belonging in the East Asian order.

His framework was integrative — it explained why Japan, Korea, and Vietnam shared deep structural similarities despite being distinct polities. But critics noted it was still fundamentally Sinocentric: China remained the radiating center, and peripheral civilizations were defined by their degree of absorption of Chinese cultural elements.

---

## 宮川尚志 and the Marxist Current — Class and Mode of Production

A significant strand within postwar 東洋史 applied Marxist historical materialism to China. Scholars debated which mode of production characterized Chinese society — was it an Asiatic mode, feudal, or something sui generis? The "debate on Chinese society" (*中国社会論争*) ran through the 1930s–50s.

The core question: why did China not independently generate capitalism? Answers ranged from hydraulic despotism (Wittfogel, influential in Japan) to structural features of the landlord-tenant system to the absence of specific legal institutions protecting merchant capital. These debates directly engaged with Weber and Marx, placing China inside comparative world-historical frameworks rather than treating it as exceptional.

---

## 岡田英弘 Okada Hidehiro (1931–2017) — Anti-Sinocentric Revisionism

Okada was deliberately provocative. He argued that "China" as a continuous civilizational entity is largely a myth — a retrospective construction by Chinese nationalist historiography. What actually existed was a series of distinct, often nomad-founded polities that periodically unified the agrarian north China plain.

He pushed the Shiratori-Haneda line to its limit: the Mongol Yuan and Manchu Qing were not "Chinese dynasties" in any meaningful sense — they were conquest empires that happened to administer Chinese-speaking populations. The Chinese nationalist narrative of 5,000 years of continuous civilization was, for Okada, ideological fiction requiring systematic deconstruction.

---

## 溝口雄三 Mizoguchi Yūzō (1932–2010) — China on Its Own Terms

A late but critical figure who argued the entire 東洋史 tradition — including its most sophisticated theorists — still evaluated China *against* either Western or Japanese standards. Even Naitō's "early modernity" thesis measured China by whether it resembled European modernity.

Mizoguchi demanded a methodology that took Chinese conceptual categories seriously as analytical tools in their own right — not merely as objects of analysis. Chinese concepts of *li* (理), *gong* (公/private), and historical change should generate their own theoretical frameworks, not be forced into Western or Japanese comparative boxes. This was a genuine epistemological challenge to the entire discipline.

# Naitō's Tang-Song Thesis: Debate & Critique | #T1 #T2 #T3 | 🔭 [historiographical controversy lens] |

---

## The Original Thesis — Core Claims

Naitō Konan formulated the Tang-Song transition argument primarily in lectures between 1909–1910, published systematically later. The claim was structural and periodizing: Tang and Song represent two fundamentally different civilizational formations, not merely successive dynasties.

Tang China was characterized by aristocratic government — powerful clans monopolizing high office, a closed elite culture, the emperor as first among aristocratic equals. The economy was predominantly agrarian and redistributive. Culture was formal, courtly, and restricted to narrow educated strata.

Song China broke this open. Aristocratic clans collapsed. The civil examination system expanded dramatically, drawing officials from broader social bases. Commercial economy deepened — paper money, credit instruments, long-distance trade networks, specialized markets. Cities became genuinely urban rather than administrative. Popular culture — vernacular literature, drama, woodblock printing — emerged as a mass phenomenon. For Naitō this was *kinsei* (近世) — early modernity — centuries before Europe.

---

## Miyazaki's Extension and Systematization

Naitō's student Miyazaki Ichisada elaborated and defended the thesis across decades of work. He deepened the economic history dimension — Song monetization and fiscal sophistication were not incidental but constitutive of the transition. He also sharpened the comparative claim: Song China's social mobility through examination, its commercial complexity, its urban culture, were structurally analogous to early modern Europe.

Miyazaki added a melancholy counterargument: the Mongol conquest of the 13th century interrupted and partially reversed Song's developmental trajectory. What had been an endogenous modernizing dynamic was disrupted externally. This became a key explanatory move — China's failure to sustain its early modern momentum was not civilizational failure but historical contingency.

---

## The American Challenge — Hartwell and Hymes

American Song specialists from the 1980s onward conducted detailed regional and local studies that complicated the thesis significantly. Robert Hartwell's 1982 demographic and elite study argued that the Song elite was *not* as open or meritocratically fluid as Naitō claimed.

Robert Hymes's work on Fuzhou elites showed that by Southern Song, local elite families had *re-entrenched* — shifting from pursuing central court careers to dominating local society through marriage networks, landholding, and local religious institutions. The examination system did not produce permanent circulation of elites but a new form of local aristocratic consolidation. The modernity thesis looked shakier at the local level.

---

## Peter Bol — The Cultural Counter-Argument

Peter Bol's *This Culture of Ours* (1992) approached the Tang-Song transition from intellectual history rather than social or economic history. He largely accepted that a major transformation occurred but reframed what was central to it.

For Bol, the crucial shift was in how educated men defined their own identity and purpose — from Tang aristocrats whose culture was inherited and performative to Song literati who grounded identity in personal moral cultivation and classical learning. This was a shift in *subjectivity*, not just institutional structure. The examination system mattered not primarily as a social mobility mechanism but as a new framework for defining what it meant to be educated and morally serious.

This enriched rather than refuted Naitō but moved the center of gravity from economic-social structure to cultural-intellectual history.

---

## The Modernity Problem — Postcolonial Critique

A deeper critique emerged from postcolonial and comparative modernities scholarship. Even granting everything Naitō claimed, the thesis still operates within a framework where "modernity" is a universal telos — a standard developmental stage that civilizations either reach or fail to reach.

Critics argued this reproduces Eurocentric assumptions in inverted form. Saying China achieved early modernity first is still measuring China against a European-derived concept. Mizoguchi Yūzō pressed this hardest: the *kinsei* framework forces Chinese history into a developmental narrative borrowed from European historical periodization, however generously applied. China deserves conceptual frameworks generated from its own historical dynamics, not borrowed categories applied in flattering ways.

---

## The Continuity Counter-Thesis — Tang-Song as Exaggerated Rupture

Some historians argued Naitō overstated the break. Dunhuang manuscript studies revealed commercial and popular cultural complexity in Tang that the court-centered sources obscure. Tang markets, merchant networks, and popular Buddhism were more developed than the aristocracy-focused narrative allowed.

Similarly, recent Tang social history has shown aristocratic families were more adaptive and commercially engaged than Naitō's framework suggested. The transition appears less as abrupt rupture and more as accelerated intensification of dynamics already present. This does not eliminate the transition thesis but deflates its periodizing sharpness.

---

## The Yuan Interruption — Contested Explanatory Weight

Miyazaki's move of blaming Mongol conquest for derailing Song modernity has been heavily scrutinized. Critics note it functions as a *rescue device* — when the thesis predicts modernization should have continued, and it didn't, the Mongols absorb the explanatory burden.

But Mongol Yuan governance also stimulated certain commercial and interregional developments. The Grand Canal system, Eurasian trade connectivity, and silver monetization under Yuan created conditions that fed directly into Ming-Qing commercial expansion. The Mongol period was not simply destructive interruption — which complicates the clean narrative of derailed modernity.

---

## Kenneth Pomeranz and the Great Divergence

Pomeranz's *The Great Divergence* (2000) engaged directly with the Naitō tradition's implicit question — why didn't China sustain its early modern trajectory into industrial capitalism? His answer shifted the question entirely: as late as 1750, the Yangzi Delta was economically comparable to the most advanced regions of Europe. Divergence was late, contingent, and driven substantially by Europe's access to coal geography and Atlantic colonial resources — not by anything deficient in Chinese civilization.

This vindicated the Naitō-Miyazaki intuition that China was not developmentally backward while simultaneously dissolving the "failed modernity" framing. There was no failed Chinese modernity — there was a global divergence driven by specific material and geographic contingencies.

---

## Chinese Historiographical Reception

Chinese historians had complicated relationships with the Naitō thesis. Republican-era Chinese historians were often hostile — a Japanese scholar periodizing Chinese history in terms derived from European developmental stages felt like another form of Orientalist appropriation.

Post-1978 Chinese historiography engaged more seriously. The Tang-Song transition became a major research framework within Chinese academia, though often stripped of its comparative modernity implications and treated as internal periodization. The thesis's most radical claim — that China achieved modernity independently and earlier than Europe — was somewhat domesticated into conventional dynastic historiography.

---

## Current Status of the Thesis

The Tang-Song transition as a *descriptive* claim — that major structural transformations occurred across this period — is broadly accepted. The commercial revolution, examination expansion, urbanization, and popular culture shifts are well-documented.

The *interpretive* claim — that this constitutes early modernity analogous to early modern Europe — remains contested and increasingly questioned on methodological grounds. The productive legacy of the thesis is not its periodization scheme but the research agenda it generated: economic history, social mobility studies, urban history, popular culture — all underdeveloped before Naitō forced them onto the field's agenda.

# PART I — HOW CHINESE HISTORIANS PERIODIZE THEIR OWN HISTORY

## The Classical Framework — Dynastic Succession

The oldest and most persistent Chinese periodization is simply dynastic. History is Shang, Zhou, Qin, Han, Tang, Song, Ming, Qing — punctuated by interregna. This was not naive: dynastic transitions marked genuine ruptures in governance, elite composition, fiscal systems, and often ethnic ruling groups.

The dynastic frame carried deep ideological freight. Each dynasty received the Mandate of Heaven (*tianming* 天命), governed morally or immorally, and eventually lost legitimacy through misrule, natural disaster, or rebellion — confirming heaven's withdrawal. History was simultaneously empirical record and moral cosmology. The official dynastic histories (*zhengshi* 正史) — twenty-four in the standard canon — institutionalized this framework across two millennia.

The problem is that dynastic periodization is political, not social or economic. It tells you who held power, not how agrarian structures, commercial networks, or family systems changed. It also embeds the Mandate logic as explanatory framework, which modern historians find ideologically loaded and analytically limiting.

---

## The Republican Revolution — Linear Progress and Social Darwinism

The collapse of the Qing and the Republic of China period (1912 onward) forced a rupture with dynastic periodization. Liang Qichao and his generation, influenced by Social Darwinism and Western progressive historiography, demanded a *new history* (*xin shixue* 新史學) — history of the *people*, not of dynasties and courts.

Liang argued that traditional Chinese historiography was the emperor's history — recording reign dates, court intrigues, official biographies — while the actual Chinese people, their society, economy, and culture, were invisible. Real history required new subjects: the nation, social forces, economic development, cultural evolution.

This generated a fundamental periodization problem: if dynasties were no longer the unit, what was? Liang and contemporaries reached for civilizational stages — ancient, medieval, modern — borrowed from European historiography but applied to China. China now had to be located on a universal developmental timeline, which immediately raised the anxious question of where exactly it stood relative to the West.

---

## Marxist Periodization — The Dominant 20th-Century Framework

After 1949, Marxist historical materialism became the official framework of mainland Chinese historiography. History moved through universal stages: primitive communism → slave society → feudalism → capitalism → socialism. Chinese history had to be mapped onto this sequence.

This generated the famous **"Five Stages" debates** (*五种社会形态论*) that dominated Chinese historiography from the 1950s through 1970s. The central controversy: when did Chinese feudalism begin and end? When did capitalism emerge?

The **slave-feudal boundary debate** was fierce — did feudalism begin with the Zhou dynasty (traditionalist position favored by Guo Moruo) or the Qin-Han unification? Guo Moruo's argument that Shang-Western Zhou was a slave society became quasi-official but was never universally accepted.

The **sprouts of capitalism debate** (*资本主义萌芽*) asked whether indigenous capitalist development appeared in late Ming-early Qing commercial expansion — roughly 16th–17th centuries. This was politically critical: if China had its own capitalist sprouts, Western imperialism *interrupted* rather than initiated Chinese modernity. China was not passive recipient of modernity but had generated its own developmental dynamic that was externally derailed — a Chinese version of Miyazaki's Mongol interruption argument, politically reconfigured.

## The Ancient/Medieval/Modern Triad — *Gudai/Zhonggu/Jindai*

Parallel to Marxist staging ran a chronological triad borrowed and adapted from Western historiography:

- **古代** (*gudai*) — antiquity, roughly pre-Qin through Han
- **中古** (*zhonggu*) — medieval, Han through Tang or Song depending on scholar
- **近代** (*jindai*) — modern, typically from the Opium War (1840) onward
- **现代** (*xiandai*) — contemporary, from May Fourth (1919) or 1949

The 1840 boundary for *jindai* is politically significant and heavily loaded. It defines Chinese modernity as beginning with Western imperial intrusion — the Opium War as origin point. This embeds a trauma narrative: Chinese modernity is coerced, reactive, wound around foreign aggression rather than endogenous development.

Post-Mao historians increasingly questioned this. Beginning modern Chinese history in 1840 made Western imperialism the protagonist of Chinese modernity — structurally repeating the colonial gaze even in nationalist historiography. Some pushed the boundary back to Ming-Qing commercial expansion, others to the 17th-century internal crisis.

---

## The 1980s Rupture — Kulturgeschichte and Social History Turn

Post-Cultural Revolution, Chinese historians began systematic engagement with Western social science and European historiography — Annales school, Weber, Habermas, Wallerstein. This produced a fragmentation of periodization frameworks.

Cultural history approaches focused on longue durée continuities cutting across dynastic and Marxist stage boundaries. Regional history emphasized that "China" was not a single developmental unit — the Yangzi Delta, the Pearl River Delta, the north China plain, and Yunnan had dramatically different historical rhythms. A periodization valid for Jiangnan merchants was inapplicable to northwest pastoralists.

This plurality did not produce a new consensus framework. It produced productive fragmentation — multiple periodizations coexisting for different analytical purposes, which many historians considered honest rather than problematic.

---

## Contemporary Chinese Historiography — Tensions and Positions

Current mainland historiography operates in tension between several pulls. Official ideology still invokes a version of the 5,000-year continuous civilization narrative — politically useful for nationalism but analytically untenable to most working historians. The *zhengshi* tradition retains prestige and institutional weight.

Meanwhile professional historians increasingly use thematic and regional frameworks that implicitly abandon universal periodization. Economic historians use commodity cycles. Social historians use lineage and kinship structures. Environmental historians use climate and ecological periodization that crosses all political boundaries.

The deepest unresolved tension is between **civilizational continuity** — the nationalist claim of an unbroken Chinese civilization — and the empirical evidence of radical discontinuities, foreign conquest dynasties, demographic catastrophes, and regional divergence that make "continuous China" a political construct rather than historical description.

# PART II — MIZOGUCHI YŪZŌ'S ALTERNATIVE CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK

## The Fundamental Critique

Mizoguchi Yūzō's starting point was an epistemological complaint: all existing frameworks for understanding China — Western Orientalism, Japanese 東洋史, Chinese nationalist historiography, Marxist stagism — evaluate China using conceptual categories that are not Chinese in origin.

Even sympathetic frameworks like Naitō's *kinsei* thesis measure China against a European developmental template and award China points for resembling Europe early. This is generous Eurocentrism, but Eurocentrism nonetheless. The analytical categories — feudalism, modernity, civil society, capitalism — were generated from European historical experience and then applied universally. China perpetually appears as either precursor, parallel, or failed version of European trajectories.

Mizoguchi demanded something more radical: use Chinese conceptual categories as *analytical tools*, not merely as objects of analysis. Chinese thought should be a source of historical theory, not just historical data.

---

## 理 (*Li*) — Principle as Historical Force

Mizoguchi's most sustained conceptual work examined the Chinese philosophical category *li* (理) — variously translated as principle, pattern, coherence, or reason — and argued it undergoes a genuine historical transformation from Song through Ming-Qing that has profound implications for understanding Chinese social and political development.

In Song Neo-Confucianism (Cheng-Zhu school), *li* was transcendent — universal moral principle existing above and prior to the material world, accessed through individual moral cultivation and study. The self-cultivating scholar-official internalized universal principle and thereby legitimized governance.

Mizoguchi tracked how *li* was gradually **immanentized** across Ming-Qing — pulled down from transcendence into the actual texture of social relationships, material life, and historical process. By late Ming thinkers like Wang Yangming's followers and especially the Taizhou school, principle was not above social life but *within* it — accessible not only through elite scholarly cultivation but through everyday practice, including by ordinary people.

This was not mere philosophical shift. It had social consequences: it potentially democratized moral authority, undermined the exclusive claim of orthodox Confucian scholarship to access truth, and opened space for popular moral agency. Mizoguchi read this as a distinctively Chinese form of what Europeans might call subjectivization — but generated through Chinese conceptual dynamics, not borrowed from European individual rights theory.

---

## 公私 (*Kō-Shi / Gong-Si*) — Public and Private Reconceived

A second major conceptual intervention was Mizoguchi's analysis of the *gong/si* (公/私) distinction — public versus private. He argued this binary does not map onto the European public/private distinction and that forcing it to do so distorts Chinese political and social history.

In European liberal theory, the public/private distinction structures civil society: a private sphere of individual rights exists prior to and as a limit on the public/state sphere. Habermas's bourgeois public sphere emerges from this private sphere to contest state power.

Chinese *gong* did not mean the same thing. *Gong* referred to what is common, shared, collective — but not in the liberal sense of aggregated private interests. It carried cosmological weight: *gong* was alignment with the coherence of the whole, with the natural order, with communal moral life. *Si* was partiality, selfishness, factional interest — always negatively coded.

Mizoguchi argued that Chinese political thought developed its own concept of legitimate collective life — rooted in *gong* — that was neither liberal individualism nor state despotism but a third formation that Western comparative frameworks consistently misread as either proto-liberalism or failed democracy. Understanding Chinese political development requires working *with* this conceptual structure, not replacing it with European categories.

---

## The Late Ming as Mizoguchi's Pivot

Mizoguchi focused heavily on late Ming thought — roughly 16th–17th century — as the moment when these conceptual transformations became most visible and socially consequential. The Taizhou school, Li Zhi's radical individualism, the commercialization of print culture, the expansion of popular moral philosophy — these constituted for Mizoguchi a genuine transformation in Chinese subjectivity and social thought.

This overlapped chronologically with Miyazaki's "early modernity" but was analytically distinct. Miyazaki's early modernity was socioeconomic — commercial revolution, examination meritocracy. Mizoguchi's was conceptual and philosophical — a transformation in how Chinese thought about principle, moral agency, collective life, and the relationship between self and society.

Crucially, Mizoguchi did not call this "Chinese modernity." He resisted the term precisely because it would immediately invite comparison with European modernity and subordinate his analysis to that comparison. He preferred to describe the transformation in Chinese terms and leave the comparative implications open.

---

## China as Method — 中国を方法として

Mizoguchi's most programmatic statement was the 1989 essay and book *China as Method* (*方法としての中国*). The argument operated on two levels simultaneously.

First, methodologically: China should be studied using conceptual tools adequate to Chinese historical experience rather than tools imported from European or Japanese frameworks. This requires deep engagement with Chinese philosophical and historical texts as theoretical sources, not just empirical data.

Second, and more ambitiously: Chinese historical experience, properly understood on its own terms, could *generate new analytical concepts* applicable beyond China — contributing to genuinely universal historical theory rather than receiving universal theory from Europe. China was not a case study illustrating Western theory but a source of theory in its own right.

This was a direct challenge to the structure of global knowledge production — where theory is generated in Western academic centers and applied to non-Western cases. Mizoguchi wanted to reverse the epistemological flow: let Chinese experience generate concepts that illuminate not only China but other civilizations including Europe.

---

## Critique of Mizoguchi

The framework attracted serious objections. First, the risk of essentialism: emphasizing distinctively Chinese concepts like *li* and *gong* can easily slide into treating Chinese civilization as a fixed, bounded essence — ironically reproducing the Orientalist move of radical alterity it sought to escape.

Second, the political ambiguity: arguing that China has its own conceptual tradition for understanding public life and collective governance can be appropriated by authoritarian nationalism to argue that liberal democratic categories are culturally inappropriate for China. Mizoguchi did not intend this but the framework is not immune to it.

Third, the question of who speaks: Mizoguchi was a Japanese scholar claiming to recover Chinese conceptual autonomy. Chinese scholars asked whether this was genuine methodological solidarity or a more subtle form of Japanese intellectual appropriation — defining authentic Chinese thought from outside.

---

## Legacy — Subimperial Epistemology and Global Theory

Mizoguchi's influence spread beyond Japanese 東洋史 into broader debates about provincializing Eurocentric knowledge — connecting with Dipesh Chakrabarty's *Provincializing Europe*, Partha Chatterjee's work on colonial modernity, and the broader postcolonial theory movement.

His insistence that non-Western civilizations are sources of theory — not just objects of theorization — remains the most important epistemological contribution of the 東洋史 tradition. It reframes the entire discipline: 東洋史 at its best is not Japan explaining Asia to itself or to the West, but a site where Asian historical experience generates concepts that expand the possibilities of historical understanding globally.

# 岡田英弘 Okada Hidehiro (1931–2017)

## Biography

Born in Tokyo, Okada studied at Tokyo University under the postwar 東洋史 establishment before developing increasingly heterodox positions that put him at odds with mainstream Japanese and Chinese historiography alike. He held a position at the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies for most of his career. His work spanned Mongolian, Manchu, and Chinese sources with genuine polyglot range — he read primary sources in classical Chinese, Mongolian, and Manchu, which was unusual and gave his revisionism empirical bite rather than merely polemical force. In later life he became associated with revisionist and nationalist Japanese intellectual circles, which complicated reception of his scholarship. He died in 2017 leaving a body of work that remains provocative, underengaged, and difficult to categorize cleanly.

---

## "China" Does Not Exist as a Continuous Entity

Okada's foundational and most radical claim was that "China" — understood as a continuous civilizational and political entity persisting across 5,000 years — is a retrospective ideological construction, not a historical reality. What actually existed across the territory conventionally called China was a series of distinct, often ethnically and institutionally discontinuous polities that periodically unified the north China agricultural plain under centralized bureaucratic control.

These polities differed fundamentally from one another. Qin-Han, Tang, Song, Ming, and Qing were not iterations of the same civilizational project but distinct state formations with different ruling ethnic groups, administrative logics, military systems, and relationships to surrounding peoples. The narrative thread connecting them — the continuous Chinese nation receiving and transmitting a unified civilization — was constructed retrospectively, primarily by Qing and Republican-era intellectuals responding to Western imperial pressure by fabricating a national continuity that legitimized modern Chinese territorial claims.

The practical implication was historiographically explosive: you cannot write "Chinese history" as a single coherent narrative because the subject does not exist. You can write the history of the north China plain, the history of specific dynasties, the history of the Chinese language and literary tradition — but these are different histories that do not automatically constitute a unified civilizational story.

---

## Conquest Dynasties as the Real Protagonists

Following and radicalizing the Shiratori-Haneda tradition, Okada argued that the major transformative moments in what is conventionally called Chinese history were driven not by endogenous Chinese civilizational development but by conquest from the steppe. The Xianbei Northern Wei, the Sui-Tang (substantially Xianbei in aristocratic composition), the Mongol Yuan, and the Manchu Qing were all fundamentally non-Han political formations.

Okada pushed further than predecessors: he argued that even the Tang dynasty — conventionally celebrated as a high point of Chinese civilization — was in its ruling house, military aristocracy, and cultural orientation substantially a non-Chinese steppe-derived formation that happened to govern Chinese-speaking populations and employ Chinese administrative methods. "Tang culture" was Eurasian steppe culture filtered through Chinese administrative apparatus, not Chinese civilization expressing itself.

This reframing inverted the conventional narrative. Instead of nomadic peoples periodically disrupting Chinese civilization and then being absorbed and sinicized, Okada saw nomadic and semi-nomadic peoples as the primary political actors who built the large empires, while "Chinese civilization" was largely a administrative-cultural toolkit that conquerors adopted instrumentally for governing agrarian populations. Sinicization was real but it was a tool of governance, not a civilizational gravitational force inevitably absorbing all it contacted.

---

## The Mongol Empire as World-Historical Pivot

Okada accorded the Mongol Empire a centrality unusual even within 東洋史. For him, Chinggis Khan's unification of the steppe and subsequent conquest of Eurasia was the single most important event in premodern world history — more consequential than any development within Chinese civilization proper.

The Mongol Empire created the first genuinely Eurasian political and commercial network, enabling the movement of peoples, technologies, diseases, and ideas across previously separated civilizational zones. The Black Death, the transmission of Chinese technologies to Europe, the administrative innovations of the Il-Khanate and Golden Horde — all were Mongol-generated phenomena. Chinese history was one provincial chapter in a Mongol world-historical story, not the other way around.

This also meant that the post-Mongol period — Ming restoration of Han rule, Qing Manchu reconstitution of steppe-agrarian empire — needed to be understood in relation to the Mongol template rather than as return to Chinese normalcy. Ming was a reaction against Mongol universalism; Qing was a partial reconstruction of it. Neither could be understood primarily through the lens of Chinese civilizational continuity.

---

## The Historiographical Politics

Okada was explicit that Chinese nationalist historiography was not merely mistaken but actively dangerous — a political tool for territorial expansion dressed as historical scholarship. Claims to Tibet, Xinjiang, Mongolia, and Taiwan all rested on the fiction of continuous Chinese sovereignty over these regions across millennia. Dismantling the myth of continuous Chinese civilization was therefore not academic pedantry but political necessity.

This made his work useful to Japanese nationalist circles who found in Okada scholarly legitimization for skepticism about Chinese territorial claims and historical grievances. Okada himself was not straightforwardly nationalist in the conventional sense — his argument equally dismantled Japanese nationalist myths about cultural continuity — but the political appropriation of his work was real and affected how seriously mainstream historians engaged with it. The genuinely important methodological points risked being discredited by association.

# 西嶋定生 Nishijima Sadao (1919–1998)

## Biography

Nishijima was born in Hyogo Prefecture and studied at Tokyo Imperial University, completing his education in the immediate postwar period — a formative context that shaped his integrative, non-imperialist approach to East Asian history. He spent his career at Tokyo University, becoming one of the dominant figures of postwar Japanese sinology. Unlike the prewar generation whose careers were entangled with imperial ideology, Nishijima worked explicitly to reconstruct 東洋史 on non-imperialist foundations — understanding East Asian civilizational coherence without making Japan its apex or center. His work on silk, early Chinese economic history, and the East Asian world concept established him as a foundational postwar figure. He died in 1998 having shaped an entire generation of Japanese and international scholars of early Chinese history.

---

## The East Asian World — Four Defining Elements

Nishijima's most influential contribution was the concept of an integrated **East Asian world** (*東アジア世界*) defined by four shared cultural-institutional elements: Chinese writing (*kanji* / 漢字), Confucianism, Buddhism, and the tributary system. Societies that participated in all four constituted a coherent civilizational zone — Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and at various times other polities in the region.

The framework was deliberately structural rather than ethnic or racial. Membership in the East Asian world was not determined by descent or language but by participation in shared institutional and cultural practices. This allowed Nishijima to discuss Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese history as part of a common civilizational framework without subordinating them to China as ethnic or racial derivatives — they were participants in a shared system, not copies of a Chinese original.

The four elements were not merely decorative cultural similarities but functional integrating mechanisms. Chinese writing enabled bureaucratic communication, canonical learning, and diplomatic exchange across language barriers. Confucianism provided shared frameworks for governance, social ethics, and political legitimacy. Buddhism created transnational networks of monks, texts, and pilgrimage that crossed political boundaries. The tributary system structured formal political relationships and the ritual acknowledgment of hierarchy.

---

## The Tributary System as Civilizational Architecture

Nishijima analyzed the tributary system not as mere diplomatic convention or Chinese imperial vanity but as the structural backbone of East Asian international order. When states sent tribute missions to the Chinese court, they were not simply acknowledging inferiority — they were entering a system that conferred legitimacy, enabled trade, and positioned them within a recognized civilizational order.

The system created what Nishijima called a *centripetal* civilizational dynamic: peripheral polities were pulled toward Chinese institutional forms because participation in the system rewarded adoption of Chinese administrative methods, written culture, and ritual practices. Japan's periodic engagement with Tang and Song courts, Korea's sustained tributary relationship, Vietnam's complex negotiation of formal submission — all were strategic as well as symbolic. Rulers who participated gained legitimacy at home and access to Chinese goods, texts, and technologies.

Crucially, Nishijima argued the system was not purely coercive or unilateral. Peripheral states manipulated it for their own purposes — sending tribute when convenient, withholding it during periods of Chinese weakness, adapting Chinese institutions selectively rather than wholesale. The East Asian world was a structured system with a clear center but with peripheral agency that shaped how the system actually functioned.

---

## Silk and the Political Economy of Early China

Before his world-system work, Nishijima produced foundational economic history of early imperial China centered on silk. His argument was that silk was not merely a luxury commodity but a political-economic instrument — used by Han and pre-Han states to bind dependent populations, reward subordinate elites, and structure political relationships.

Silk functioned as a form of political currency: the state collected it as tax, distributed it as reward, and used it in diplomatic gift exchange with steppe peoples. The silk-for-peace agreements (*heqin* 和親) with the Xiongnu were not tribute paid in humiliation but rational political economy — silk for border stability, a calculated exchange that acknowledged the military reality of steppe power while preserving the fiction of Chinese superiority through the tributary framing.

This economic-political analysis of silk connected Nishijima's early work to his later world-system framework. The East Asian world was not merely a cultural community but an economic zone structured by specific commodities and exchange relationships. Cultural transmission and economic exchange were inseparable — silk moved with Confucian texts, Buddhism traveled along trade routes, the tributary system was simultaneously diplomatic ritual and commercial regulation.

---

## Critiques — Persistent Sinocentrism

The most sustained critique of Nishijima's framework was that despite its structural framing, it reproduced Sinocentrism in a sophisticated form. The four defining elements — Chinese writing, Confucianism, Buddhism, tributary system — were all defined by their relationship to China. Japan, Korea, and Vietnam appeared in the framework primarily as recipients and adapters of Chinese cultural-institutional exports.

This meant that societies outside the four-element framework — Southeast Asian polities, Tibetan and Central Asian societies, the steppe peoples — were marginal or absent. The East Asian world was coherent but narrow, and its coherence was purchased by defining civilizational membership through proximity to Chinese cultural forms. Critics argued this was Japanese 東洋史 reproducing the 華夷秩序 worldview it claimed merely to analyze — taking the Sinocentric tributary order as an analytical framework rather than treating it as an object of critique.

Later scholars, particularly those working on maritime Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean world, argued that Nishijima's framework made it structurally difficult to see the alternative regional integrations — Islamic maritime networks, Sanskrit cosmopolis, Theravada Buddhist exchange circuits — that organized large parts of Asia outside or alongside the Chinese-centered system.

# 羽田亨 Haneda Tōru (1882–1955)

## Biography

Haneda was born in Kyoto and studied under Naitō Konan at Kyoto Imperial University, becoming one of the Kyoto School's most distinguished second-generation figures. He specialized in Central Asian history, Islamic sources, and the historical linguistics of Turkic and Iranian peoples — fields requiring mastery of Arabic, Persian, Uighur, and other languages beyond the Sinitic sources that dominated Tokyo-school 東洋史. He spent his career at Kyoto Imperial University, eventually becoming its president. His institutional position gave him significant influence in shaping the Kyoto School's distinctive emphasis on Inner Asian and Islamic history as correctives to Tokyo's more Sinocentric orientation. He died in 1955, having trained the postwar generation of Kyoto-based Central Asian specialists.

---

## The Multiethnic Empire — Against Ethnic Chinese History

Haneda's central methodological argument was that the major empires conventionally classified as "Chinese" were in fact multiethnic, multilingual, and institutionally composite formations that cannot be reduced to Chinese ethnic or civilizational history. Tang is his paradigm case.

The Tang imperial house — the Li family — had substantial Xianbei descent and was embedded in the mixed Sino-steppe aristocratic culture of the northwest frontier. The Tang military was built around non-Han commanders and troops — Sogdian, Turkic, and other Inner Asian soldiers formed the backbone of Tang expansion. The capital Chang'an was cosmopolitan in a genuine and not merely decorative sense: Zoroastrian fire temples, Nestorian Christian churches, Manichaean communities, and large Muslim merchant populations coexisted with Buddhist monasteries and Confucian academies.

This composite character was not incidental but constitutive. Tang power rested on its ability to integrate steppe military capacity with agrarian administrative depth — a synthesis that was neither purely Chinese nor purely nomadic but genuinely hybrid. Treating Tang as an expression of Chinese civilization missed what was most distinctive and powerful about it.

---

## Sogdian Networks and Eurasian Connectivity

Haneda's archival and linguistic range allowed him to reconstruct the Sogdian merchant networks that connected Tang China to Central Asia, Persia, and the Byzantine world. The Sogdians — Iranian-speaking people from the Samarkand region — were the primary commercial and cultural intermediaries of the Silk Road during the Tang period.

Sogdian merchant colonies existed throughout Tang China — in Chang'an, Luoyang, and along the northwest corridor. They carried not only silk and spices but religious texts, artistic styles, musical instruments, and agricultural techniques. The Tang cosmopolitanism that Chinese historians celebrated as Chinese cultural openness was substantially Sogdian-mediated — it was the result of active Sogdian agency in building and maintaining Eurasian exchange networks, not simply Chinese receptivity.

This reframing had implications for how the Silk Road should be understood. It was not a Chinese-managed commercial corridor radiating from Chang'an outward but a network with multiple active nodes — Samarkand, Merv, Dunhuang, Chang'an — in which Chinese imperial power was one organizing force among several. The Sogdians, Turks, Tibetans, and Tibetan frontier peoples were not peripheral to this network but constitutive of it.

---

## Islam and the Eastern Islamic World

Haneda was among the first Japanese historians to integrate Islamic historical sources seriously into East Asian history — reading Arabic and Persian chronicles, geographical texts, and merchant accounts alongside Chinese dynastic histories. This allowed him to reconstruct the Tang-Abbasid contact zone from both sides, producing a more complete picture than either Chinese or Islamic sources alone could provide.

His argument was that the Abbasid caliphate and Tang empire were not merely distant contemporaries but participants in a shared Eurasian political and commercial system. The Battle of Talas (751) — where Abbasid and Tang forces clashed in Central Asia — was not a peripheral border skirmish but a moment of direct contact between the two dominant Eurasian empires of the 8th century, with lasting consequences for Central Asian political alignment and the spread of papermaking technology westward.

More broadly, Haneda established that understanding Chinese history from the Tang onward required fluency in Islamic sources. Muslim geographers described Chinese cities, Muslim merchants operated in Chinese ports, Muslim officials served in Chinese administrations. The boundary between "Chinese history" and "Islamic history" was an artifact of modern academic disciplinary division, not a historical reality.

---

## The Corrective to Naitō — Inner Asia as Subject, Not Context

Haneda's relationship to his teacher Naitō was respectful but corrective. Naitō's Tang-Song transition thesis, for all its sophistication, remained centered on the Chinese agrarian-bureaucratic world — the transition from aristocratic to examination-based governance, the commercial revolution of the Song Yangzi Delta. Inner Asian peoples appeared in Naitō primarily as external pressure — the nomadic forces whose periodic interventions disrupted or catalyzed Chinese development.

Haneda insisted Inner Asian peoples needed to be studied as historical subjects in their own right, with their own internal dynamics, institutions, and historical logics — not as a backdrop to Chinese history. The Turkic Khaganates, the Sogdian city-states, the Tibetan Empire, the Uighur confederation were not supporting cast in China's story but protagonists of their own historical dramas that intersected with Chinese history at specific points.

This seemingly methodological point had broad consequences. It meant the proper unit of analysis for much of Eurasian history was neither China nor the Islamic world nor Europe but the Inner Asian corridor connecting them — a space that generated its own political formations, cultural syntheses, and historical dynamics. Haneda laid the institutional and methodological foundation for what would later become the field of Eurasian history, anticipating by decades the work of scholars like David Christian, Christopher Beckwith, and Nicola Di Cosmo.
