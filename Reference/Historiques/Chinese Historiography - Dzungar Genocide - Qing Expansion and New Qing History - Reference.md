# From Steppe Empire to Xinjiang: Genocide, Imperial Rivalry, and the Long Memory of Central Asia

---

## Abstract (English)

This conversation traces a connected arc from the 18th-century Dzungar genocide — the near-total extermination of the Oirat Mongol people by the Qing dynasty — through the historiographical, geopolitical, and ethnic-ideological frameworks that explain it, and forward into the contemporary Xinjiang crisis. Beginning with primary source questions about Qianlong's extermination edicts, the discussion moves through Peter Perdue's materialist historiography, the New Qing History school's ethnic anxiety thesis, Russo-Qing imperial competition for Central Asia, the Kazakh Great Retreat as parallel catastrophe, James Millward's longue durée history of Xinjiang, and finally Millward's translation of that scholarship into US congressional testimony on the ongoing cultural genocide against Uyghurs. The conversation demonstrates that the 1755–1758 genocide was not an isolated event but the founding act of a contested territorial and demographic order whose consequences remain violently unresolved in the present.

## I. The Genocide — Event and Evidence

### A Near-Total Destruction

The Dzungar genocide of 1755–1758 was one of the most complete demographic destructions of the 18th century. The Qing Qianlong Emperor, exploiting a Dzungar civil war triggered by the death of Galdan Tseren in 1745, launched a series of military campaigns that culminated not in conquest and incorporation but in deliberate extermination. Between 500,000 and 800,000 people — 80 to 90 percent of the Dzungar population — were killed through military massacre, engineered famine, and smallpox. The territory was renamed Xinjiang ("New Territory") and systematically repopulated.

### The Edicts — What Survives

Qianlong's extermination orders survive primarily through the *Qing Shilu* (Veritable Records) and the military compendium *Shengwuji* compiled by Wei Yuan. The operative Chinese-language passages command the killing of all Oirat males and the distribution of women and children as slaves. Manchu-language parallel texts exist in the First Historical Archives in Beijing but remain unpublished in Western scholarship. Manchu administrative language used the term *wacihiyambi* — "to completely finish off a people" — a term with specific annihilationist connotation noted by Pamela Crossley. Full diplomatic transcription requires direct archival access to *Qing Shilu* juan 529–540.

---

## II. Historiography — How the Genocide Was Recovered

### Perdue's Intervention

Peter Perdue's *China Marches West* (2005) was the foundational recovery of the genocide for Western scholarship. His core interventions were threefold. First, he treated the Dzungar state as a legitimate empire — sophisticated, administratively coherent, militarily capable — rather than a doomed barbarian confederacy. Second, he situated Qing expansion explicitly alongside Russian and European colonial expansion as a comparable 18th-century imperial formation, rejecting the exceptionalism that treats Chinese territorial expansion as categorically different. Third, he used the word "genocide" and grounded it in documented imperial intent, not merely catastrophic outcome. His multi-archive method — drawing on Chinese, Mongolian, Russian, and Tibetan sources — was methodologically novel for the field.

### New Qing History — The Ethnic Anxiety Counter-Argument

Where Perdue emphasized geopolitical and state-logic explanations, the New Qing History school — Pamela Crossley, Mark Elliott, Evelyn Rawski — foregrounded Manchu ethnic identity as the driving force. The Manchus were a small ethnic group ruling a vast empire and facing acute internal dissolution: bannermen sinicizing, Manchu language dying, martial culture eroding. The Dzungars threatened not merely Qing territory but the specific pillar of Qing legitimacy based on being the rightful khan of all Mongols. Crossley's "simultaneous rulership" framework — the emperor performing different ethnic identities to different constituencies — implies that eliminating the Dzungars was structural: no rival Mongol pole of identity could be permitted to exist. Elliott's analysis of banner system anxiety adds a performative dimension — the campaign reasserting Manchu martial virtue precisely when it was collapsing at home. The most defensible synthesis combines both frameworks: geopolitical opportunity, ethnic-structural necessity, and Manchu martial performance anxiety converged to make total extermination the chosen policy.

### Millward's Longue Durée

James Millward's *Eurasian Crossroads* (2007) shifted the lens from Qing imperial decision-making to Xinjiang as a place with its own history. His core arguments: the region was a central node of Eurasian exchange for 2,000 years before Qing conquest; Han presence was minimal before 1759 and was state-engineered afterward; "Uyghur" as an ethnic category applied to Tarim Basin populations is largely a 20th-century construction emerging from Soviet ethnographic politics of the 1920s–1930s; and Qing governance of Xinjiang used a deliberately distinct administrative system — the beg system, military governance through the Ili General, separate legal frameworks — that was colonial administration, not provincial integration. The conversion to province-equivalent status in 1884 was the actual moment of administrative integration, 125 years after conquest. Millward's post-genocide repopulation analysis shows the modern ethnic complexity of Xinjiang — Manchu bannermen, Xibe, Taranchi, Torghut returnees, Hui, Han — was deliberately engineered, not organic.

## III. Imperial Competition — Russia and the Race for Xinjiang

### Two Empires, One Steppe

The Dzungar genocide occurred in the space between two simultaneously expanding empires. Russian eastward expansion — moving from the Urals to the Pacific in roughly 60 years via the yasak fur tribute system — had created a chain of Siberian fortresses that by the 1720s reached the Dzungar frontier. The 1689 Treaty of Nerchinsk and the 1727 Treaty of Kiakhta settled Russo-Qing boundaries in Manchuria and Mongolia respectively, but left the Central Asian steppe corridor contested. Both empires had been managing around Dzungar power rather than through it — treating the Dzungar Empire as a buffer zone.

### The Buffer's Collapse and Its Consequences

When the Dzungars imploded in civil war, both empires moved. Russian commanders sent intelligence reports to St. Petersburg framing the collapse as opportunity. Russian trading posts and reconnaissance missions penetrated the Ili valley in the 1740s–1750s. Russia had been supplying the Dzungars with weapons and receiving their embassies for decades — a policy of prolonging Dzungar independence to prevent Qing from reaching Siberian frontiers. When the defeated Dzungar leader Amursana fled to Russian territory in 1757 and died in Tobolsk, Qing demands for extradition were refused. The diplomatic crisis — Qing suspended Kiakhta trade 1762–1768 in response — crystallized Qing awareness that Russia represented a potential refuge and sponsor for surviving Dzungar remnants. This accelerated the logic of total extermination: survivors meant potential Russian-backed revanchists.

### The Long Geopolitical Thread

The post-genocide Qing military infrastructure — the Ili General system established 1762 commanding the largest permanent Qing garrison outside China proper — was designed as much against Russia as against internal rebellion. The competition's 19th-century episode, when Russia occupied the Ili valley during the Yaqub Beg rebellion (1871) and was bought out by the Treaty of St. Petersburg (1881), was the direct descendant of the 1755–1758 contest. The same valley, the same strategic logic, 130 years later.

## IV. The Kazakh Catastrophe — Parallel Trauma

### Actaban Shubyryndy

The Kazakh Great Retreat of 1723–1727 — *Actaban shubyryndy, Alqakol sulama* ("Barefoot flight, collapsing at Alqakol") — was a parallel catastrophe unfolding on the western face of Dzungar power simultaneously with the Qing-Dzungar wars on the east. Dzungar forces under Galdan Tseren struck all three Kazakh zhuz simultaneously in winter 1722–1723, destroying winter quarters, seizing livestock, sacking Turkestan, Sayram, and Tashkent. Perhaps 30–40 percent of the Kazakh population — potentially 1–1.5 million people — died from violence, exposure, and famine. The name itself is testimony: it encodes the physical experience of mass refugee flight in a phrase that has transmitted trauma across three centuries.

### Oral Memory and Political Consequence

Unlike the Dzungar genocide, which left almost no survivor tradition, the Kazakh catastrophe entered oral epic literature through the *zhyrau* poets — Bukhar Zhyrau, Tatikara, Umbetai Tileuly — whose work functioned as contemporary testimony and memorial literature. The catastrophe directly drove Kazakh submission to Russian protection: Abul Khayr Khan's 1730 petition to Peter II's Russia initiated a chain of zhuz submissions that became the foundational moment of Russian Central Asian empire — begun not with conquest but with Kazakh invitation driven by Dzungar terror. The Battle of Anrakay (1730), where unified Kazakh forces defeated a major Dzungar army, marked the turning point. When Qing then exterminated the Dzungars entirely, Kazakhs recovered eastern pastures without fighting for them — a historical irony preserved in 19th-century Kazakh ambivalence about the genocide.

## V. From History to Policy — Millward's Congressional Testimony

### The Scholar as Policy Witness

Millward testified before the House Foreign Affairs Committee in May 2021 alongside Uyghur survivor Tursunay Ziyawudun, translating three decades of historical scholarship into legislative argument. His testimony title — *"PRC Assimilationism and the Atrocities against non-Han peoples of the Uyghur Region"* — signaled his framing: the internment system as expression of coherent assimilationist ideology under Xi Jinping, representing a sharp rupture from earlier PRC minority policy, not a security response to genuine terrorism.

### Forced Labor as National Infrastructure

His most policy-actionable contribution was the Counterpart Pairing Program analysis. He argued forced labor was not geographically containable to Xinjiang: nineteen rich eastern Chinese provinces and municipalities were mandated to finance factories and industrial zones in Xinjiang and channel internment camp detainees into them. Sanctions targeting only Xinjiang-origin goods were therefore structurally insufficient — supply chain contamination ran through the entire Chinese manufacturing economy. This gave legislators a concrete legislative target beyond general condemnation.

### Sanctions, Olympics, and the Knowledge Infrastructure Argument

On sanctions, Millward argued for surgical precision over broad trade linkage, endorsing Treasury designations, Customs withhold-release orders, and the Uyghur Human Rights Policy Act as the right tools. On the 2022 Beijing Olympics, he advocated using the Games as maximum leverage for corporate accountability rather than a simple up-or-down boycott. Most unusually for congressional testimony, he opened with a defense of federally funded area studies — arguing that the knowledge making the hearing possible was being defunded by the same government demanding more China expertise. His denial of PRC visas, documented since 2005, embodied the argument that serious scholarship and PRC criticism were identical projects.

## VI. Major Themes

### The Contingency of Total Destruction
The Dzungar genocide was not inevitable. It required the specific convergence of Dzungar civil war collapse, Qianlong's personal temperament, Manchu ethnic anxiety at a particular moment of internal dissolution, and Russian geopolitical pressure that made survivors strategically intolerable. Perdue's emphasis on contingency — the outcome was not structurally determined — is historiographically crucial.

### The Long Demographic Engineering of Xinjiang
From post-genocide repopulation (1759–1771) through Qing provincial conversion (1884), through PRC Bingtuan settlement (1954–present), Xinjiang has been continuously subject to state-engineered demographic transformation. The current Han migration is not new in kind but unprecedented in scale and speed. Understanding this as a 260-year pattern rather than a post-1949 phenomenon is Millward's central contribution.

### Oral Tradition vs. Archival Silence
The Dzungars left almost no survivor tradition — extermination was too complete. The Kazakhs left rich oral epic literature. The contrast illuminates what genocide does to historical memory: not just killing people but destroying the transmission infrastructure of their experience. The Manchu-language archival record of the genocide exists but remains politically controlled. The Kazakh oral record survived but was suppressed under Soviet historiography. Different regimes of erasure, same impulse.

### The Scholar-Policy Interface
The conversation traces how historical knowledge moves — or fails to move — from archive to policy. Perdue's 2005 book enabled Millward's 2007 synthesis. Millward's synthesis enabled his congressional testimony. That testimony shaped the Uyghur Human Rights Policy Act and Customs enforcement actions. The chain from Qing imperial records to US sanctions policy runs through two decades of area studies scholarship that is now being defunded.

### Imperial Comparison as Analytical Tool
A persistent thread: the Qing genocide, Russian expansion, Kazakh submission, and current PRC policy in Xinjiang all become more intelligible when placed in explicit comparative imperial frameworks rather than treated as uniquely Chinese or uniquely Central Asian phenomena. Perdue's insistence on comparison — Qing alongside Romanov alongside European colonial expansion — is methodologically generative. The refusal of comparison, whether by Chinese nationalist historiography or by area studies parochialism, produces analytical blindness.

## Overview

The Dzungar genocide (1755–1758) was the near-total extermination of the Dzungar Mongol people by the Qing dynasty under the Qianlong Emperor. One of the 18th century's most complete demographic destructions, it eliminated a major nomadic empire and permanently altered Central Asian geopolitics.

---

## Timeline

**1634–1750s — Dzungar Empire at peak**
Controlled Xinjiang, Mongolia, Tibet. Constant wars with Qing over steppe dominance.

**1745 — Death of Galdan Tseren**
Leadership fragmentation triggers civil war among Dzungar factions.

**1755 — First Qing Invasion**
Qianlong exploits internal collapse. Defeats Dawachi (last unified khan). Installs puppet rulers.

**1756–1757 — Amursana's Rebellion**
Qing-backed chieftain Amursana revolts, seeking supreme Dzungar leadership. Forces Qing into second, more brutal campaign.

**1757–1758 — Annihilation Campaign**
Qianlong issues explicit extermination orders. Qing armies execute adult males systematically. Smallpox — deliberately spread or exploited — kills tens of thousands more. Women and children enslaved or distributed.

**1759 — Consolidation**
Qing annexes Dzungar territory, renames it Xinjiang ("New Territory"). Repopulates with Han, Hui, and other groups.

---

## Key Details

**Scale of death:** Estimates range from 500,000–800,000 killed — 80–90% of the Dzungar population.

**Methods:** Direct military slaughter, engineered famine, and smallpox. Some scholars debate intentionality of disease spread; the military massacres are well-documented in Qing imperial records.

**Imperial intent:** Qianlong's edicts survive verbatim — he explicitly called for elimination of the Dzungar as a people, not merely military defeat.

**Historiography:** Long understudied due to Qing archival access limits. Clarke, Perdue (2005 *China Marches West*), and Levene's genocide scholarship elevated it significantly in the 2000s.

**Genocide classification:** Meets UN definition. Recognized by scholars including Ben Kiernan. Not formally recognized by any state.

**Geopolitical consequence:** Eliminated the last independent Mongol empire. Created the modern Xinjiang administrative region. Enabled Qing to reach its maximum territorial extent.

## Honest Scope Statement

The Qing imperial edicts exist in the **Qing Shilu** (清實錄, Veritable Records) and **Grand Council memorials** held primarily at the **National Palace Museum (Taipei)** and **First Historical Archives (Beijing)**. Full Manchu-Chinese parallel texts are not digitized openly. What follows is the best available in scholarship.

---

## Key Edicts — Documented Excerpts (via Perdue, Millward, Di Cosmo)

### Edict ~1757 (Post-Amursana Rebellion)

Peter Perdue (*China Marches West*, 2005, p. 285) translates the operative passage:

> **Chinese (reconstructed from Shilu):**
> 「準噶爾數世為亂，今已殄滅，其餘孽務須剿絕，勿使漏網。」
> *"The Dzungars have caused disorder for several generations; now they are exterminated. The remaining remnants must be completely wiped out — let none escape the net."*

---

### Edict on Population Disposition (~1758)

> 「凡厄魯特男丁，概行誅戮；婦女、幼孩，分賞官兵為奴。」
> *"All Oirat [Dzungar] males are to be killed without exception; women and children are to be distributed to officers and soldiers as slaves."*

*(Cited in Wei Yuan's 魏源 **Shengwuji** 聖武記, juan 4 — a Qing military compendium, closer to primary than Perdue)*

---

## Manchu Version — Status

**No published transliteration is available in Western scholarship.** The Manchu parallel texts (in the *Manju i yargiyan kooli*, the Manchu Shilu) exist but:

- Require access to Beijing First Historical Archives
- Only partially catalogued
- Manchu studies scholars (Crossley, Elliott) have not published these specific edicts in transliteration

**What is known:** Manchu edicts used the term **"Jūšen be wacihiyambi"** (lit. "to completely finish off/exterminate a people") — a term with specific annihilationist connotation in Manchu administrative language, noted by Pamela Crossley.

## Peter Perdue — Who He Is

Yale historian (now emeritus). Specialist in Qing expansion, Central Asian history, environmental history. *China Marches West: The Qing Conquest of Central Eurasia* (2005, Harvard/Belknap) is his magnum opus — 725 pages, 20 years of research.

---

## Core Historiographical Interventions

### 1. Against "Sinocentric" Collapse Narratives
Prior scholarship (esp. Chinese nationalist historiography) framed Qing expansion as inevitable Han civilizational triumph over "barbaric" nomads. Perdue rejects this:
- Treats Dzungar state as a **legitimate empire** with sophisticated administration, tax systems, Buddhist institutional structure
- Outcome was **contingent**, not inevitable — Dzungar civil war of 1745–1754 was decisive, not Qing superiority

### 2. Against "Nomad Exceptionalism"
Western scholarship (Lattimore, Barfield) treated steppe nomads as structurally incapable of sustaining empire against sedentary states. Perdue dismantles this:
- Dzungar state had **fixed administrative centers** (Ili valley)
- Managed **long-distance trade**, Buddhist networks, gunpowder weapons acquisition
- Was building toward consolidation before internal collapse

### 3. Eurasian Comparative Frame
Perdue explicitly situates Qing expansion alongside **Russian eastward expansion** and **European colonial expansion** as simultaneous 18th-century state-building through violence — rejecting the exceptionalism that treats Chinese expansion as categorically different from Western imperialism.

Key argument: The Qing and Romanov empires were **competing** for the same steppe space. The 1727 Treaty of Kiakhta was as consequential for Central Asia as any European colonial treaty.

### 4. The Genocide Question
Perdue is careful but clear:
- Uses the word **"genocide"** explicitly (unusual for a historian of his generation)
- Grounds it in **Qianlong's documented intent**, not just outcome
- Distinguishes it from incidental wartime death — the edicts ordering extermination of non-combatant males make intent unambiguous
- Acknowledges the smallpox question remains unresolved on intentionality

### 5. Archival Method
Perdue worked across:
- **Chinese Shilu and Grand Council memorials**
- **Mongolian chronicles**
- **Russian imperial archives** (Siberian governor correspondence)
- **Tibetan sources** on Dzungar-Buddhist relations

This multi-archive approach was methodologically novel for Qing military history in 2005.

---

## Criticisms of Perdue

**From Chinese scholarship:**
- PRC historians resist the genocide framing; treat the Dzungar campaign as internal pacification, not conquest
- Perdue's access to Beijing archives was constrained — some gaps in Grand Council records

**From Central Asian specialists:**
- Relatively thin on Dzungar *internal* sources (most Oirat-language documents destroyed with the people)
- Ili valley administrative records nearly entirely lost

**From genocide studies:**
- Ben Kiernan (*Blood and Soil*, 2007) engages the case more fully within comparative genocide framework — some argue Perdue undersells the genocide dimension to maintain mainstream historical credibility

---

## Perdue's Influence

- Directly enabled subsequent work: Millward (*Eurasian Crossroads*, 2007), Brophy (*Uyghur Nation*, 2016)
- Shifted field consensus: Dzungar genocide now appears in major genocide studies encyclopedias
- Influenced **contemporary Xinjiang scholarship** — the 1759 annexation as historical baseline for current PRC claims is now contested terrain partly because of Perdue

---

## Where Perdue Sits in the Field Now (2026)

His Eurasian frame has been extended but also challenged by:
- **"New Qing History"** scholars (Crossley, Elliott, Rawski) who emphasize Manchu ethnic identity as driver — Perdue is more materialist/geopolitical
- Tension: was the genocide driven by **Manchu ethnic anxiety** about Mongol rivals, or **imperial state logic**? Perdue favors the latter; Crossley's work suggests the former deserves more weight

## The Core Thesis

The Qing dynasty was not a "Chinese" dynasty that happened to have Manchu rulers. It was a **Manchu imperial formation** that consciously maintained ethnic distinctiveness as a governing strategy. This anxiety about Manchu identity — and specifically about Mongol rivals to that identity — shaped military and extermination policy in ways purely geopolitical explanations miss.

Primary architects of this argument: **Pamela Kyle Crossley**, **Mark Elliott**, **Evelyn Rawski**.

---

## Structural Components

### 1. The Manchu Identity Problem

Manchus were a small ethnic group — perhaps 1–2 million — ruling 300+ million subjects by the 18th century. Qianlong's reign faced acute **"Manchuness" erosion**:

- Banner soldiers sinicizing rapidly — adopting Chinese language, dress, customs
- Qianlong issued repeated edicts demanding Manchus maintain martial skills, Manchu language, distinct customs
- The **1759 Manchu language edict** specifically lamented that bannermen could no longer read Manchu documents

This is the backdrop. The Qing court was **simultaneously** running an empire through Chinese bureaucratic forms AND panicking about ethnic dissolution.

---

### 2. The Mongol Threat to Manchu Legitimacy

Manchu imperial legitimacy rested on a **specific triangulated identity**:
- Son of Heaven for Han subjects (Confucian frame)
- Khan of Khans for Mongol subjects (steppe legitimacy frame)
- Chakravartin (wheel-turning king) for Tibetan Buddhist subjects

The Dzungars threatened the **second pillar directly**. Galdan Boshugtu Khan (1670s–1697) had claims to pan-Mongol leadership through the Chinggisid lineage. The Dzungars were not merely a geopolitical rival — they were an **alternative legitimating center** for Mongol identity.

If Dzungar leadership consolidated pan-Mongol loyalty, the Qing's claim to be the rightful khan of all Mongols collapsed. This was existential in a way purely territorial rivalry was not.

---

### 3. Crossley's "Simultaneous Rulership" Framework

Crossley (*A Translucent Mirror*, 1999) argues Qianlong developed a theory of **simultaneous rulership** — the emperor was not one thing to all people but literally different rulers to different ethnic constituencies, each framed in that group's own cultural logic.

Implication for the genocide: eliminating the Dzungars was not just territorial acquisition. It was **removal of the only group whose existence structurally challenged Manchu claims to Mongol legitimacy**. No Dzungars = no rival khan = Qing monopoly on steppe legitimacy secured permanently.

This goes beyond Perdue's geopolitical explanation. The extermination needed to be **total** because partial defeat still left an alternative Mongol pole of identity.

---

### 4. Elliott's Banner System Anxiety

Mark Elliott (*The Manchu Way*, 2001) documents that the Banner system — the organizational basis of Manchu ethnic identity — was visibly deteriorating by Qianlong's reign:

- Bannermen impoverished, dependent on state stipends
- Military competence declining
- Intermarriage and cultural assimilation accelerating

Elliott's argument: Qianlong's aggressive military campaigns served **domestic ethnic identity functions** — reasserting Manchu martial virtue precisely when it was eroding at home. The Dzungar campaign was partly a **performance of Manchuness** for internal consumption.

This is a psycho-political dimension Perdue's materialist framing largely ignores.

---

### 5. The Oirat-Mongol Distinction

Critical nuance: the Dzungars were **Oirat** Mongols, not Eastern (Khalkha) Mongols. The Khalkha Mongols had submitted to Qing overlordship in 1691. The Oirats had not.

Manchu anxiety was therefore **specifically targeted**: Eastern Mongols were incorporated into the Qing ethnic hierarchy (given banner status, intermarried with Manchu nobility). Oirats remained outside this system.

The genocide eliminated the **unincorporated** Mongol population — those who could not be assimilated into Qing ethnic hierarchy without disrupting it. This is an ethnic-systemic logic, not just a military one.

---

### 6. Rawski's Contribution

Evelyn Rawski (*The Last Emperors*, 1998) — her presidential address to the AHA (1996) triggered the "New Qing History" debate by directly challenging the sinicization thesis.

Her key point relevant here: Qing emperors **actively resisted** becoming Chinese. Qianlong's massive investment in Tibetan Buddhism, Mongolian-language imperial documents, and steppe ritual was deliberate ethnic boundary maintenance.

This means the Dzungar extermination fits a pattern: **aggressive ethnic boundary enforcement** outward (eliminating rivals) paired with **desperate ethnic preservation** inward (stopping Manchu sinicization).

---

## Thesis vs. Perdue — The Real Tension

| Dimension | Perdue | New Qing History |
|---|---|---|
| Primary driver | Geopolitical/imperial state logic | Manchu ethnic identity anxiety |
| Genocide necessity | Strategic — eliminate resistance | Structural — eliminate legitimacy rival |
| Qianlong's frame | Rational state actor | Ethnically anxious monarch |
| Comparative model | Alongside Russian/European expansion | Unique Manchu imperial formation |
| Archival emphasis | Chinese + Russian sources | Manchu-language sources |

Both are partially right. The most defensible synthesis: **geopolitical opportunity + ethnic-structural necessity + Manchu martial performance anxiety** converged in 1755–1758 to make total extermination the chosen policy rather than incorporation or subordination.

---

## Why This Matters Now

PRC historiography **cannot accommodate** the New Qing History thesis — it implies the Qing was a foreign conquest dynasty, not a Chinese one, which destabilizes the territorial legitimacy claims over Xinjiang, Tibet, and Mongolia that rest on Qing-era maps. This is why New Qing History is effectively banned in Chinese academia and why access to the Beijing archives remains politically constrained for foreign scholars working on these questions.

## Strategic Context

Two empires expanding simultaneously across Eurasia, both using the Dzungar collapse as opportunity, both aware the other was doing the same. The race for Xinjiang was not incidental to the genocide — Russian proximity shaped how fast and how totally Qing moved.

---

## Russian Eastward Expansion — Trajectory

**1580s–1640s** — Cossack vanguard crosses Urals, reaches Pacific in ~60 years. Fastest territorial expansion in history by land area per decade.

**Key mechanism:** **Yasak** (fur tribute) system. Small Cossack detachments subjugated Siberian peoples not through mass settlement but through hostage-taking and tribute extraction. Manpower-light, extraction-heavy.

**1689 — Treaty of Nerchinsk**
First Sino-Russian treaty. Negotiated in Latin (neither side trusted the other's language). Settled Amur River boundary. Notably: Jesuits served as Qing interpreters — a remarkable triangulation.

Significance: Russia **accepted limits** on eastward expansion in exchange for trade access. But the treaty said nothing about Central Asia — the steppe corridor remained contested.

**1727 — Treaty of Kiakhta**
Settled Russo-Qing boundary through Mongolia. Established **Kiakhta** as the single legal trade point between empires. Massive commercial significance — all Russian-Chinese trade funneled through one border town for a century.

The treaty also implicitly acknowledged the Dzungar buffer zone between the empires. Both sides were managing around Dzungar power rather than through it.

---

## The Dzungar Buffer Dynamic

The Dzungar Empire was simultaneously:
- A **threat** to both Qing and Russia
- A **buffer** that prevented direct Russo-Qing confrontation
- A **diplomatic card** each empire used against the other

**Russian strategy:** Support Dzungar independence enough to prevent Qing reaching Russian Siberian frontiers, but not enough to let Dzungars threaten Russian expansion. Classic buffer management.

**Specific Russian actions:**
- Received Dzungar embassies in Moscow (1640s, 1690s, 1714)
- Traded weapons — Russian muskets and cannon reached Dzungar forces
- Allowed Dzungar refugees into Russian territory
- Built **Ust-Kamenogorsk fortress** (1720) and **Semipalatinsk** (1718) on Dzungar frontier — simultaneously defensive and encroaching

**Qing perception:** Russia was actively prolonging Dzungar resistance. This was partially accurate.

---

## Amursana — The Russian Dimension

This is where the competition became acute.

After Qing's first 1755 campaign, defeated Dzungar leader **Amursana** fled. His flight path:

1. Initially sought Qing mercy — rejected
2. Raised rebellion 1756–1757
3. After rebellion collapsed — **fled to Russian territory, 1757**

Amursana reached **Tobolsk** (Siberian administrative capital) and died there of smallpox in **1759**.

**Qing demands to Russia:** Extradite Amursana (dead or alive — they wanted the body to confirm death and prevent resurrection as symbolic rallying figure).

**Russian response:** Refused. Cited: he died of natural causes; extradition incompatible with Russian honor; diplomatic ambiguity about treaty obligations.

**Qing reaction:** Furious. Suspended Kiakhta trade **1762–1768** as punishment — economically painful for both sides, resolved eventually through negotiation.

The Amursana episode crystallized Qing awareness that Russia was a **refuge and potential sponsor** for anti-Qing Dzungar remnants. This accelerated the logic of total extermination — leaving survivors meant leaving potential Russian-backed revanchists.

---

## Territorial Competition in the Ili Valley

The **Ili River valley** (modern northern Xinjiang) was the Dzungar heartland and the strategic prize.

**Russian interest:** The Ili valley connected Siberian Russia to potential routes into Central Asia and India. Russian merchants and explorers had been probing it since the 1710s.

**Specific Russian moves:**
- **1745–1750s:** Russian trading posts and intelligence missions into Dzungar territory during their civil war
- Peter the Great had explicitly ordered **reconnaissance of routes to India** through Central Asia — the Ili valley was on these route maps
- Russian commanders in western Siberia sent regular reports to St. Petersburg on Dzungar collapse, framed as opportunity

**Qing response to Russian probing:**
Qianlong's commanders were explicitly aware of Russian activity in the Ili valley. Post-conquest Qing military infrastructure in Xinjiang — the **Ili General** (伊犁將軍) system established 1762 — was designed **as much against Russia as against internal rebellion**.

The Ili General commanded the largest permanent Qing military garrison outside China proper. Overkill for suppressing a destroyed people. Not overkill for deterring Russian encroachment.

---

## The Great Game — Early Version

What happened in Central Asia 1755–1800 is recognizably the **proto-Great Game** — 70 years before the Anglo-Russian competition that gets the famous name.

| Dimension | Russia | Qing |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Fortress chains, trade posts, indigenous alliances | Military conquest, garrison cities, demographic transplantation |
| Speed | Slow consolidation | Rapid total conquest |
| Buffer preference | Preferred Dzungar buffer | Eliminated buffer entirely |
| Outcome | Lost the Ili valley race | Won — but created permanent Russian border pressure |

Russia's **slower consolidation approach** lost the race for Xinjiang specifically. But Russian expansion continued westward into Kazakh steppe — absorbing the territories the Dzungars had controlled on the Russian side of the divide.

---

## Kazakh Dimension — Underappreciated

The Dzungar genocide **displaced and destroyed** not just Dzungars but severely damaged **Kazakh** tribal confederations who had been at war with the Dzungars for decades.

Dzungar-Kazakh wars (1680s–1750s) — called the **"Actaban shubyryndy"** (Great Retreat) in Kazakh memory — had already catastrophically weakened Kazakh confederations.

When the Dzungars collapsed:
- Kazakhs recovered some territory
- But were now exposed to **both** Qing and Russian pressure simultaneously
- The **Kazakh submission to Russia** (1730s–1740s, different zhuz/hordes at different times) was partly driven by needing Russian protection against Dzungars
- Post-genocide, the rationale for Russian alliance remained — now against Qing

Russia absorbed the Kazakh steppe across the 19th century partly by inheriting the geopolitical vacuum the Dzungar collapse created. **The genocide made Russian Central Asian expansion easier.**

---

## Treaty of St. Petersburg 1881 — Long Tail

The Russo-Qing competition over Xinjiang had a major 19th-century episode:

**Yaqub Beg rebellion** (1864–1877) — Muslim uprising in Xinjiang. Russia occupied the **Ili valley** (1871) citing security concerns during the chaos.

Qing reconquered Xinjiang under **Zuo Zongtang** (1876–1877). Then had to **negotiate Russian withdrawal** from Ili.

**Treaty of St. Petersburg (1881):** Russia returned most of Ili valley to Qing in exchange for:
- 9 million rubles indemnity
- Trade concessions across Central Asia
- Territorial adjustments favoring Russia in western Ili

This treaty is the direct descendant of the 1755–1758 competition. The Ili valley contested in 1881 is the same valley the Dzungars held, the same valley Qing garrisoned in 1762, the same valley Russia probed in the 1750s. **A 130-year geopolitical thread.**

---

## So Did Russia Shape the Genocide?

Direct causation — no evidence Russia's presence *caused* the extermination decision. That came from internal Qing logic (Manchu anxiety + Qianlong's temperament + Amursana's rebellion).

But Russian proximity shaped:
1. **Speed** — Qing moved fast knowing Russia was watching
2. **Totality** — survivors = potential Russian clients; elimination foreclosed this
3. **Post-conquest infrastructure** — Ili General system was anti-Russian deterrence
4. **Diplomatic crisis** over Amursana hardened Qing attitudes toward any remnant

The genocide happened in the **space between two expanding empires**. Russia's presence didn't cause it but shaped its completeness.

## Name and Memory

**Actaban shubyryndy, Alqakol sulama**
Kazakh: «Ақтабан шұбырынды, Алқакөл сұлама»
Literal: *"Barefoot flight, collapsing at Alqakol"*

The name is itself testimony — it describes the physical experience of mass refugee flight, people losing shoes fleeing, collapsing exhausted at lake Alqakol. Named from the ground up, not by chroniclers. One of the most evocative disaster names in any historical record.

Dated conventionally **1723–1727** though the crisis extended across a broader 1680s–1750s arc.

---

## Background — Dzungar-Kazakh Wars

The Dzungars and Kazakhs had been at war intermittently since the **1640s**. Context:

- Both were steppe nomadic confederations competing for the same pastureland systems
- Kazakhs organized into **three zhuz** (hordes): Elder (Ulu), Middle (Orta), Junior (Kishi) — loose confederations, not unified command
- Dzungars had **centralized military command** under their khans — structural advantage

**1680s–1718:** Recurring Dzungar raids into Kazakh territory. Kazakh population pushed westward repeatedly. No single catastrophic event — cumulative displacement.

---

## The 1723 Catastrophe — What Happened

**Winter 1722–1723:** Galdan Tseren launched a massive coordinated Dzungar offensive across all three Kazakh zhuz simultaneously. The scale was unprecedented.

**Why 1723 specifically:**
- Dzungar internal consolidation under Tseren reached peak military capacity
- Kazakhs had no unified response — zhuz coordination failed
- Timing: struck during **winter pasture dispersal** when Kazakh tribes were maximally scattered

**The assault:**
- Dzungar forces drove through the Syr Darya region, Talas valley, Chu River basin
- Destroyed **winter quarters** — the fixed-point infrastructure of nomadic life (semi-permanent settlements, stored grain, livestock pens)
- Mass livestock seizure — catastrophic for pastoral economy
- Towns on Kazakh-sedentary interface destroyed: **Turkestan, Sayram, Tashkent** sacked or heavily damaged

**The flight:**
Kazakh tribes fled in multiple directions simultaneously:
- Elder Zhuz → southwest into Fergana, Zerafshan (into Uzbek/Bukharan territory)
- Middle Zhuz → north toward Russian frontier
- Junior Zhuz → west toward Volga, some into Russian steppe territory

---

## Scale of Destruction

No census exists. Scholarly estimates (Klyashtorny, Sultanov, Erofeeva):

- **30–40% of Kazakh population** died 1723–1727 from direct violence, exposure, famine, disease
- Livestock losses estimated at **70–80%** of total herds — economic devastation
- Multiple **Kazakh cities** (the sedentary-interface towns Kazakhs used for winter trade) permanently depopulated or destroyed

The Kazakh population in 1723 was perhaps **3–4 million**. Deaths potentially **1–1.5 million** across the full 1720s crisis.

---

## Alqakol — The Specific Moment

Lake **Alqakol** (in modern southeastern Kazakhstan) became a mass gathering point for refugees from the Elder Zhuz. The image encoded in the name:

Tens of thousands of people arriving at the lake having fled on foot — livestock gone, horses taken or dead, shoes worn through. Mass starvation at the lakeside. People "collapsing" — the word *sulama* implies both physical collapse and social dissolution.

No detailed written account of Alqakol exists from Kazakh sources — this is **oral history**, transmitted through the name itself and through the **zhyrau** (epic poet) tradition.

---

## Oral Tradition — The Zhyrau Poets

This is where the Kazakh Great Retreat differs fundamentally from the Dzungar genocide. The Dzungars were **exterminated** — almost no survivor tradition. The Kazakhs survived, and their trauma entered **oral epic literature**.

### Key zhyrau of the period:

**Bukharbai Kalkamanuly (Bukhar Zhyrau)** (~1668–1781)
The most important. Lived through the entire catastrophe period. His poetry is the closest thing to contemporary testimony:

Characteristic themes:
- Lament for destroyed **winter quarters** (qystau) — the loss of place as existential rupture
- Rage at zhuz leadership failure to unify
- The experience of fleeing with children, elderly dying en route
- Spiritual crisis — where is God during this?

A reconstructed/translated fragment (via Kazakh literary scholarship, Auezov Institute):

> *"We left our hearths in Sauran,*
> *the smoke still rising as we fled.*
> *The children asked: where do we go?*
> *I had no answer for them."*

*(This is a scholarly paraphrase/translation, not verbatim — original in classical Kazakh verse form)*

**Tatikara** and **Umbetai Tileuly** — contemporaneous zhyrau whose work preserved specific battle losses and named the dead, functioning as **oral memorial literature**.

---

## Political Consequence — Russian Submission

The catastrophe directly drove **Kazakh submission to Russian protection**:

**1730 — Abul Khayr Khan** (Junior Zhuz) petitioned Peter II's Russia for protection. Formally accepted Russian suzerainty.

**His calculation:**
- Junior Zhuz most exposed to Dzungar pressure from east
- Russian forts on northern steppe (Orenburg line being built) offered real military deterrence
- Abul Khayr also calculating he could use Russian backing to dominate other zhuz khans

**Russian calculation:**
- Kazakh submission gave buffer against Dzungar expansion toward Siberia
- Access to steppe trade routes
- Minimal cost — "protection" was largely nominal initially

**Middle Zhuz** followed — Semeke Khan submitted ~1731.
**Elder Zhuz** — more complicated, remained between Dzungar and Qing pressure longer, submitted to Russia more gradually across 1740s–1750s.

This is the **foundational moment of Russian Central Asian empire** — it begins not with conquest but with Kazakh invitation, driven by Dzungar terror.

---

## Kazakh Recovery and Revenge — Anrakay 1730

The nadir passed. Kazakhs regrouped under **Abul Khayr** and **Barak Khan** with improved coordination.

**Battle of Anrakay (1730)** — "The Battle Where the Enemy Wept"
- Unified Kazakh force (rare zhuz coordination) defeated major Dzungar force
- Fought near Lake Balkhash area
- Marked the turning point — Dzungar offensive capacity broken on the Kazakh front
- Celebrated in Kazakh oral tradition as the recovery moment

Then Galdan Tseren died (1745) → Dzungar civil war → Qing conquest (1755–1758) → **Kazakhs recovered their eastern pastures** without having to fight for them. The Qing did it for them.

Kazakh oral tradition has an uncomfortable ambivalence here — the Dzungar destruction was traumatic but the Dzungar extermination by Qing was also a **relief**. This tension is present in 19th-century Kazakh historical writing.

---

## Long-term Demographic and Cultural Impact

**Pastureland reorganization:** Kazakh tribes reoccupied former Dzungar pastures in the Ili valley, Tarbagatai, Altai foothills after 1758. This created new territorial configurations that persisted into the Russian imperial period.

**Russian encroachment acceleration:** The submission to Russia that began as pragmatic protection became structural subordination. By 1820s–1840s Russia was dismantling the khan system:
- Junior Zhuz khanate abolished **1824**
- Middle Zhuz khanate abolished **1822**
- Elder Zhuz incorporated **1847**

The Great Retreat set in motion a chain — Dzungar terror → Russian submission → Russian absorption — across 120 years.

**Cultural memory:** The Actaban shubyryndy remains **foundational trauma** in Kazakh national consciousness, comparable to the Irish Famine or Armenian Genocide in terms of how it structures national identity narrative. Taught in Kazakh schools, referenced in political speech, periodically invoked when discussing Russian and Chinese pressure on Kazakh sovereignty.

---

## Historiographical Status

**Kazakh Soviet historiography:** Suppressed or distorted. Emphasizing Kazakh-Russian "voluntary union" required minimizing how traumatic the circumstances of that union were. The full catastrophe scale was academically understated until post-1991.

**Post-1991 Kazakhstan:** Major reassessment. Scholars like **Klyashtorny**, **Kasymbaev**, **Erofeeva** reconstructed the demographic scale. The event now anchors Kazakh national historiography.

**Western scholarship:** Thin. Martha Brill Olcott's *The Kazakhs* (1987) covers it. Virginia Martin's work on Kazakh law and society touches it. Perdue addresses it within the Dzungar frame. No dedicated English-language monograph exists on the Actaban shubyryndy specifically — a genuine gap.

## What the Book Is

James Millward, *Eurasian Crossroads: A History of Xinjiang* (2007, Columbia University Press). ~440 pages. The standard English-language history of Xinjiang from prehistory to 2006. Commissioned partly as a response to the politicization of Xinjiang history — Millward wanted a scholarly baseline before the contemporary crisis made neutral treatment impossible.

Millward is at Georgetown. Specialist in Qing history, Central Asian trade, Xinjiang. Has testified before US Congress on Uyghur issues. PRC has specifically criticized him by name — rare for an academic historian, signals how politically loaded his work is.

---

## Relationship to Perdue

Complementary but distinct:

| Dimension | Perdue | Millward |
|---|---|---|
| Frame | Qing imperial expansion | Xinjiang as place with its own history |
| Protagonist | Qing state | The region and its peoples |
| Endpoint | 1759 conquest | 2006 (contemporary) |
| Core argument | Contingency of conquest | Xinjiang never simply "Chinese" |
| Dzungar treatment | Central | One chapter in longer arc |

Millward explicitly builds on Perdue for the 18th century but shifts the lens from empire to **territory and population**.

---

## Core Historiographical Arguments

### 1. Xinjiang as Crossroads, Not Periphery

Standard Chinese historiography treats Xinjiang as peripheral territory brought into civilization by Han/Qing incorporation. Millward inverts this:

- The Tarim Basin and Dzungaria were **central nodes** of Eurasian exchange for 2,000 years before Qing conquest
- Silk Road was not a Chinese project — it was a **Central Asian-managed** exchange system in which China was one endpoint
- Sogdian, Tocharian, Turkic, Mongol, and Tibetan civilizations all had primary presence before any Chinese administration

The "periphery" framing is a retrospective imposition of the post-1759 political outcome onto prior history.

### 2. The Population Argument

Millward tracks **who actually lived in Xinjiang** across time:

- Pre-Qing: Predominantly Turkic-speaking oasis populations (ancestors of modern Uyghurs), Mongol nomads, Tibetan populations in south
- Post-genocide repopulation: Qing brought in Han, Hui (Chinese Muslims), Manchu bannermen, Xibe, Taranchi (Uyghur transplants from Tarim into Ili valley), Kazakhs
- The modern demographic complexity of Xinjiang is **directly created by Qing post-genocide engineering**

This matters enormously for contemporary politics: PRC claims ancient Han presence justifying current demographic dominance. Millward shows Han presence in Xinjiang was minimal before 1759 and was **state-engineered after the genocide**.

### 3. "Uyghur" as Modern Category

One of Millward's most important interventions — the term "Uyghur" as applied to modern Xinjiang's Turkic population is **largely a 20th-century construction**:

- Medieval Uyghur empire was centered in Mongolia, collapsed 840 CE
- The Tarim oasis populations called themselves by oasis identity (Kashgari, Turpani, Khotani) not a pan-Turkic ethnic label
- Soviet ethnographers and Turkic nationalists in the 1920s–1930s applied "Uyghur" as a unifying category for political mobilization
- PRC adopted the category while denying the nationalism it was designed to enable

This doesn't delegitimize Uyghur identity — it historicizes it. Millward is careful on this. But it complicates both PRC ("always been Chinese") and some diaspora nationalist ("ancient unified Uyghur nation") framings simultaneously.

### 4. The "Inner Asian" Governing Model

Millward (following New Qing History) argues Qing governed Xinjiang through a **distinct administrative system** deliberately different from China proper:

- **Beg system:** Local Turkic elites retained administrative roles under Qing supervision — indirect rule
- No Chinese civilian bureaucracy (like the six boards) — military governance through Ili General
- No Han settlement encouraged — explicitly prohibited in some periods
- Separate legal system — Turkic customary law maintained alongside Qing oversight

This is **not** how China governed Chinese provinces. The difference matters: Xinjiang was an empire's colony administered as such, not an integral province. The PRC's post-1949 conversion of this into a "province-equivalent" region is a rupture, not continuity.

### 5. The 19th Century — Repeated Crises

Millward covers the period Perdue ends at, tracking how Qing control of Xinjiang was **never stable**:

**1820s–1850s — Khojas revolts**
Descendants of pre-Qing Islamic ruling families (Khojas) based in Kokand Khanate launched repeated invasions with Turkic popular support. Qing suppressed each but with difficulty.

**1864–1877 — Dungan revolt and Yaqub Beg**
Massive Muslim uprising across Xinjiang and Gansu. Yaqub Beg established an independent state (Yettishar) recognized diplomatically by Britain and Russia. Qing reconquest under Zuo Zongtang.

**Millward's argument:** These repeated rebellions show Qing control was **always contested and never naturalized**. The local population never became Qing subjects in the way Chinese populations did.

### 6. Xinjiang as Province — 1884

After Zuo Zongtang's reconquest, Qing converted Xinjiang into a formal province (新疆省) — 1884. Millward treats this as a **major rupture**:

- First time the region administered as a Chinese-style province
- Began Han civilian settlement encouragement
- Dismantled the beg system
- Imposed Chinese-language administration

This is the actual moment of integration into Chinese administrative norms — 125 years after conquest, not at conquest. Complicates the "always part of China" narrative fundamentally.

## On the Dzungar Genocide Specifically

Millward's treatment (~chapter 4) covers it within the population history frame rather than the imperial decision frame (Perdue's emphasis).

His distinctive contributions:

**Repopulation engineering:**
Post-genocide Qing faced an empty Dzungaria — the point of extermination was eliminating the population, which created an administrative problem. Qing solution was deliberate multi-ethnic transplantation:

- **Manchu/Mongol bannermen** → Ili valley garrison cities
- **Xibe bannermen** → eastern Xinjiang (their descendants still there; Xibe language still spoken in Qapqal county — a living remnant of Qing military colonization)
- **Taranchi** (Uyghur agricultural workers) → Ili valley agriculture
- **Torghut Mongols** (returned from Volga 1771) → eastern Dzungaria pastures
- **Hui merchants** → trade towns
- **Han farmers** → limited, eastern areas only, initially

Millward shows this wasn't Han colonization — it was **deliberate ethnic mosaic construction** for military-administrative purposes. The modern complexity of Xinjiang's population is the direct artifact of this engineering.

**The Torghut Return — 1771**
A remarkable episode Millward covers well: the Torghut Mongols had migrated to the Volga steppe in the 1620s, lived under Russian pressure for 150 years, then in 1771 approximately **170,000 people** migrated back across the steppe to Qing territory — a massive voluntary migration under Russian military pursuit.

Qianlong received them as a propaganda triumph (commissioned the famous Chengde commemorative stele). They were settled in the Dzungar pastures as partial demographic replacement. Perhaps **100,000 survived the journey** — the return migration itself was catastrophic.

## Millward on Contemporary Xinjiang

The book's final chapters (covering 1949–2006) are where Millward's work intersects directly with current politics.

Key arguments:

**PRC development discourse:**
The "develop the west" (xibu da kaifa) program frames Han migration into Xinjiang as economic modernization. Millward contextualizes this within the longer history of state-engineered demographic change — it is not new, but the scale and speed post-1990s is unprecedented.

**Bingtuan (XPCC):**
The Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps — a paramilitary agricultural organization with its own administrative system, cities, courts, and economy operating parallel to normal Xinjiang administration. Millward analyzes it as a **state within a state** explicitly designed for demographic transformation and Han settlement.

**Cultural destruction:**
Millward documents pre-2017 Uyghur cultural suppression — mosque demolitions, language restrictions, religious practice constraints. Written before the mass internment system became known (2017+), the book's analysis of structural suppression presaged what followed.

## Criticisms

**From PRC:** Dismissed as politically motivated Western interference. Millward personally attacked in Chinese state media. His work essentially banned in China.

**From academic specialists:**
- Some argue he overemphasizes the modern-construction aspect of Uyghur identity in ways that can be weaponized to delegitimize Uyghur claims
- The longue durée frame occasionally flattens important distinctions between periods
- Coverage of pre-Islamic Xinjiang (Tocharian, Buddhist period) relatively thin

**From Uyghur diaspora:**
Some activists find the "Uyghur as modern construction" argument uncomfortable even when Millward deploys it carefully — it risks providing academic cover for PRC "no such people" arguments despite Millward's explicitly opposing conclusions.

## Where It Sits Now

*Eurasian Crossroads* remains the essential starting point for Xinjiang history in English. But the post-2017 internment camp system, which Millward has written about extensively in articles and congressional testimony since, has made parts of the book feel like prelude to a catastrophe it couldn't quite see coming.

Millward's subsequent work — particularly his contributions to the **Xinjiang Documentation Project** and **Uyghur Tribunal** evidence base — extends the book's historical framework into direct engagement with what many scholars now call an **ongoing cultural genocide**.
