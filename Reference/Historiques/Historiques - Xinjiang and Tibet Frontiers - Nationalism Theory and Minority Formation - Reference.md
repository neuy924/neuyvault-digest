# Nationalizing Frontiers and Minority Formation: China, Xinjiang, and Tibet through Anderson, Gellner, Smith, Brubaker, Hobsbawm, Breuilly, and Hroch

## Abstract (English)

This discussion develops a synthetic theoretical framework for analyzing China, Xinjiang, and Tibet by integrating eight linked problematics across seven major theorists of nationalism: imagined community, modernization and standardization, ethno-symbolic depth, classification and groupness, invented tradition, nationalism as politics aimed at state power, and staged minority mobilization. Its central claim is that no single theory adequately explains the Chinese frontier problem. China is best understood as a nationalizing state that seeks to render frontier regions legible, governable, and integrated within a unified political order while preserving the formal language of multiethnic inclusion. In this setting, Xinjiang and Tibet are not merely administrative peripheries but historically dense borderlands in which classification, security, education, memory, religion, and territorial attachment intersect. Brubaker is most useful for analyzing China as a nationalizing state and for resisting the reification of minorities as fixed groups; Gellner best explains the pressures toward linguistic, educational, and administrative standardization; Anderson clarifies how national community is imagined across distance through language, narrative, and mediated simultaneity; Smith explains the durability of Tibetan and Uyghur identity through myth, memory, religion, and sacred territory; Hobsbawm illuminates the political manufacture of historical continuity through invented tradition; Breuilly sharpens analysis of nationalism as organized politics directed toward or against state power; and Hroch helps sequence minority national formation from cultural revival to activist agitation and, under some conditions, broader mobilization. The discussion argues that Xinjiang is best read as a securitized and standardized frontier, Tibet as a frontier where religion, memory, and sacred geography remain central to political identity, and China itself as a state project combining classification, modernization, and historical narration in order to stabilize territorial unity. This produces a layered framework in which frontier governance is understood not as a single process of “assimilation,” but as the interaction of state-building, symbolic production, political contestation, and uneven minority formation. Brubaker’s original triadic model remains useful but requires adjustment in the Chinese case, because Tibet and Xinjiang do not map neatly onto the classic minority–nationalizing state–external homeland structure derived from post-communist Europe. Brubaker’s work originated in the post-1989 breakup of multinational communist states, while Hobsbawm is associated with “invented tradition,” Breuilly with nationalism as politics, and Hroch with the staged mobilization of smaller nations.

## Preamble

Brubaker’s concepts did **not** emerge in a vacuum. They were forged in two overlapping contexts.

First, his early work grew out of **West European debates on citizenship, nationhood, and immigration**, especially the contrast between France and Germany. In a 1990 article summarizing that line of work, he argued that vis-à-vis immigrants, France defined the citizenry more expansively as a territorial community, while Germany defined it more restrictively as a community of descent. ([Sage Journals][1])

Second, his best-known nationalism concepts were shaped by the **late 1980s and 1990s collapse of communist multinational states**, above all the USSR, Yugoslavia, and Czechoslovakia. *Nationalism Reframed* explicitly opens from the fact that Europe was supposed to be moving beyond the nation-state, but instead the Soviet Union, Yugoslavia, and Czechoslovakia disintegrated into nationally defined successor states. ([Center for Nationalism Studies][2])

So the milieu was:

* **late Cold War / post-Cold War Europe**
* **citizenship and immigration debates in France and Germany**
* **the breakup of multinational communist federations**
* **the surge of “ethnic conflict” talk in the 1990s**
* **dissatisfaction with treating nations and ethnicities as fixed, self-evident groups**. ([Sage Journals][1])

## 1) The historical context behind the concepts

### A. Before *Nationalism Reframed*: citizenship, nationhood, and immigration

Brubaker’s earlier book, *Citizenship and Nationhood in France and Germany* (1992), came out of a different but related problem: why two neighboring West European states handled **citizenship, nationhood, and immigrant incorporation** so differently. His core contrast was that France leaned toward a more territorial-political understanding of membership, while Germany historically leaned toward descent. ([Sage Journals][1])

That book belongs to the intellectual world of:

* comparative historical sociology,
* postwar Western Europe,
* migration and naturalization policy,
* and critiques of the simple “civic France vs ethnic Germany” cliché.

So the seedbed of Brubaker’s later work was already there: he was interested in how **states define membership**, how nationhood is **institutionally reproduced**, and how supposedly obvious national categories are historically made rather than naturally given. ([Sage Journals][1])

### B. The decisive shift: post-1989 Europe

The most famous Brubaker concepts came out of the **post-1989 nationalist reordering of Europe**. The collapse of communism did not produce the peaceful fading of nationalism many had expected. Instead, it produced new states, new citizenship laws, disputes over Russians outside Russia, Hungarians outside Hungary, Serbs outside Serbia, and wars in the former Yugoslavia. *Nationalism Reframed* was written directly into that setting. ([Center for Nationalism Studies][2])

This is the real birthplace of:

* **nationalizing state**
* **national minority**
* **external national homeland**
* **triadic nexus**
* and the emphasis on incomplete or contested **“stateness.”** A later discussion of Brubaker quotes his warning that former Soviet republics had achieved juridical independence, but their sociological “stateness” remained to be established. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

### C. Why *Ethnicity without Groups* followed

By the early 2000s, Brubaker had become dissatisfied not only with nationalism theory but with the broader language of **ethnic groups** and **identity**. In *Ethnicity without Groups*, he argued against “groupism,” the tendency to treat ethnic groups, nations, and races as substantial entities. The Harvard description of the book says exactly that: he challenges pervasive “groupism” and shifts attention from fixed groups to how ethnicity is constructed. ([Google Books][4])

That move makes sense historically. The 1990s were full of claims about “ancient hatreds” and ethnic groups as if they were timeless actors. Brubaker’s intervention was a reaction against that analytic laziness.

## 2) The original examples in the books

## A. *Citizenship and Nationhood in France and Germany* (1992)

### What problem was he trying to explain?

Why did **France** and **Germany**—two major neighboring states with intertwined histories—develop such different traditions of **citizenship and nationhood**, especially in relation to immigrants and descendants of immigrants? ([Sage Journals][1])

### What were the original examples?

The original examples were not exotic frontier cases. They were the **French and German citizenship traditions themselves**.

Brubaker used:

* **France** as the classic case of a more territorial and state-centered model of membership;
* **Germany** as the classic case of a more descent-centered model. ([Sage Journals][1])

The point was not simply jus soli versus jus sanguinis as legal doctrine in the abstract. It was that citizenship law reflected deeper historically sedimented ideas of **nationhood**. France, in his account, treated the nation more as a political-territorial community; Germany more as a community of descent. ([Sage Journals][1])

### Why this book matters for the later concepts

This book supplied the template for his later work:

* nationality is not just sentiment,
* states codify belonging,
* legal and administrative categories matter,
* and nationhood is historically produced, not merely felt.

## B. *Nationalism Reframed: Nationhood and the National Question in the New Europe* (1996)

### What problem was he trying to explain?

Why did nationalism re-emerge so powerfully in post-communist Europe, and how should we conceptualize political struggles in places that had just emerged from multinational empires and communist federations? *Nationalism Reframed* focuses on Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union and on the breakup of the USSR, Yugoslavia, and Czechoslovakia into nationally defined successor states. ([Center for Nationalism Studies][2])

### What were the original examples?

This is where the famous examples appear.

#### 1. **Post-Soviet successor states**

The nationalizing-state concept was built around newly independent states such as **Estonia, Latvia, Ukraine, and Kazakhstan**. Brubaker later revisited the concept using exactly these four cases, focusing on demography, language, polity, and economy. ([Taylor & Francis Online][5])

The original point was that these were not settled, neutral nation-states. They were states that were formally for all citizens but were also engaged in projects of promoting a **core nation** after the Soviet collapse.

#### 2. **Russians outside Russia**

A second core example was **ethnic Russians in the “near abroad”**—that is, Russians living in the former Soviet republics after 1991. These populations exemplified the relation between a **national minority**, a **nationalizing state**, and an **external homeland** claiming some concern for them. Later work discussing Brubaker’s framework uses Russians in neighboring post-Soviet states as a paradigmatic case. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][6])

#### 3. **Hungarians outside Hungary**

Another classic example was **Hungarian minorities in neighboring states** such as Romania and Slovakia, with **Hungary** as the external homeland. A later treatment of Brubaker’s triadic nexus states this directly: the nexus was evident in the relationship between Hungary, neighboring states like Slovakia and Romania, and Hungarian communities living there. ([src-h.slav.hokudai.ac.jp][7])

#### 4. **Former Yugoslavia**

The book also used the **breakup of Yugoslavia** as a sustained illustration of the triadic relational nexus. A searchable copy of the book notes that the breakup of Yugoslavia is used to illustrate the dynamics of the triadic nexus. ([Masaryk University][8])

### What concepts came out of these examples?

From those cases came the main Brubaker vocabulary:

* **nationalizing states**
* **national minorities**
* **external national homelands**
* **triadic nexus**
* weak or incomplete **stateness** in newly independent states. ([src-h.slav.hokudai.ac.jp][7])

## C. *Ethnicity without Groups* (2004)

### What problem was he trying to explain?

Why do scholars keep talking as if ethnic groups, nations, and races are naturally bounded entities? Brubaker’s answer was that scholarship had fallen into **groupism**. The Harvard description says the book challenges that pervasive and commonsense tendency and shifts the focus from groups as entities to group-making processes, categorization, and identifications. ([Google Books][4])

### What were the original examples?

This book is not a single-case monograph. It is a collection of essays, and the examples are more varied.

From reviews and summaries, the original examples include:

* **historical and contemporary case studies** used to illustrate “ethnicity without groups,” rather than one master case, ([Academia][9])
* a **rich analysis of the 1848 commemorations** in **Hungary, Romania, and Slovakia**, showing how nationalism is performed and framed rather than merely possessed, ([Carnegie Council][10])
* and the broader **Transylvanian / Cluj-Kolozsvár** Hungarian-Romanian setting, which then became central in the later collaborative book *Nationalist Politics and Everyday Ethnicity in a Transylvanian Town*. Reviews of that later project describe it as showing how ethnicity is reproduced from above as a basic category of social and political life and lived in everyday ways, which is very much the empirical spirit of *Ethnicity without Groups*. ([ResearchGate][11])

### What concepts came out of these examples?

This is where Brubaker sharpened:

* **groupism**
* **groupness**
* **categories vs groups**
* **group-making projects**
* and the turn from “identity” as a thing to **identification, categorization, and practice**. ([Google Books][4])

## 3) The original intellectual milieu in one line

Brubaker’s concepts were born at the intersection of:

* **comparative historical sociology**
* **late-20th-century European citizenship debates**
* **the post-1989 breakup of multinational communist states**
* **the 1990s boom in writing about ethnicity and nationalist conflict**
* and a methodological revolt against treating nations or ethnicities as fixed groups. ([Sage Journals][1])

## Interpretation

The development is sequential.

**Book 1** asks: how do states define membership?
**Book 2** asks: what happens when multinational states collapse and new nationalizing states confront minorities and external homelands?
**Book 3** asks: why are we still talking as if these categories are natural groups rather than political and cognitive effects?

That is the clearest way to understand the evolution of Brubaker’s conceptual toolkit. ([Sage Journals][1])

## Red team review

One caution: Brubaker’s best-known concepts are often quoted more than they are read. The result is that **“nationalizing state”** and **“triadic nexus”** sometimes get detached from their very specific post-communist European origins. Likewise, **“groupism”** is often repeated as a slogan even when analysts still smuggle groups back in through the back door. That is a real weakness in how his work is used.

## Preamble

These four are often grouped together, but they are **not doing the same job**.

* **Anderson** asks how nations become imaginable as communities.
* **Gellner** asks why modern social structure makes nationalism functional and likely.
* **Smith** asks why nations have such depth and emotional durability, and why modernist theories underplay myths, memories, and premodern ethnic materials.
* **Brubaker** asks analysts to stop treating nations and ethnicities as fully formed things, and instead study the **categories, institutions, and political processes** that make them appear real. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][1])

## Topic map

The cleanest comparison is across five questions:
**What is a nation? When does it arise? What drives it? What evidence matters? What is the main analytical mistake?** 

## 1) What is a nation?

### Benedict Anderson

For Anderson, the nation is an **imagined political community**, imagined as limited and sovereign. “Imagined” does not mean fake; it means members will never know most fellow members personally, yet still imagine a shared communion. He ties this strongly to print media, declining religious universalisms, and the breakdown of dynastic legitimacy. ([Verso][2])

### Ernest Gellner

For Gellner, nationalism is fundamentally tied to **modernity**, especially the social requirements of industrial society. His classic move is functional: modern society needs cultural standardization, literacy, mobility, and interchangeable persons, so nationalism helps align culture and state. One Cambridge overview notes that criticism of Gellner often centers on his strong linkage between industrialization and nationalism. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

### Anthony D. Smith

Smith defines the nation more thickly as a **named human population** sharing historic territory, myths and memories, mass public culture, common legal rights and duties, and a common economy. He rejects the idea that nations are either timeless essences or purely modern fabrications; instead he argues that modern nations often draw on older **ethnies**, myths, symbols, memories, and sacred attachments. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

### Rogers Brubaker

Brubaker is less interested in giving one grand essence of “the nation” than in analyzing how nationhood is institutionalized and politically activated. In *Nationalism Reframed*, his focal units are **nationalizing states**, **national minorities**, and **external homelands**; in *Ethnicity without Groups*, he shifts further toward categories, practices, and variable “groupness,” warning against treating nations as solid, naturally bounded entities. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

## 2) When do nations arise?

This is where the deepest split lies.

**Gellner** and **Anderson** are both broadly **modernists**, but not in the same register. Gellner grounds nationalism in the structural demands of industrial modernity; Anderson grounds it in cultural-historical changes such as print capitalism, vernacularization, and the decline of dynastic and sacred orders. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

**Smith** is the main critic of this compression. He does not deny that nationalism is modern, but he insists that nations cannot be understood without the premodern symbolic materials from which they are built. A Cambridge discussion of Smith presents him as the key representative of **ethno-symbolism**, rejecting the claim that nationhood is exclusively modern. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][6])

**Brubaker** is partly orthogonal to this debate. He is less invested in declaring a single birth-date for nations than in showing how categories of nationhood are produced, institutionalized, and mobilized in particular settings—especially late-20th-century post-communist Europe. 

## 3) What drives nationalism?

### Anderson: media and imagination

Anderson’s main engine is the capacity of print languages, newspapers, novels, and new temporal consciousness to let large populations imagine themselves as a shared community. His emphasis is cultural and communicative. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][1])

### Gellner: social structure and standardization

Gellner’s main engine is the requirement of industrial society for standardized high culture, mass education, and mobile labor. Nationalism is not just an idea; it is fitted to a particular kind of social order. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

### Smith: myths, memories, symbols, sacred history

Smith’s main engine is the enduring force of symbolic inheritance—myths of origin, collective memory, territorial attachment, shared values, and historically sedimented ethnocultural repertoires. He thinks modernist accounts systematically understate why nationalism grips people so deeply. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

### Brubaker: states, classifications, claims, and political fields

Brubaker’s main engine is relational and institutional. Nationhood is reproduced through censuses, citizenship laws, territorial arrangements, minority claims, homeland claims, and state projects. He is interested in how nationalism works as a field of interaction, not only as a cultural narrative or structural necessity. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

## 4) What kind of evidence does each privilege?

**Anderson** privileges cultural forms and historical shifts in communication: novels, newspapers, print-languages, and the decline of older legitimacy structures. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][1])

**Gellner** privileges macro-sociological transformation: industrialization, education systems, labor mobility, and cultural standardization. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

**Smith** privileges longue durée historical materials: myths, symbols, rituals, memories, historical territories, religious idioms, and ethnocultural continuity. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

**Brubaker** privileges institutional and analytic caution: citizenship regimes, state categories, minority/homeland claims, and the difference between **categories** and actually mobilized **groups**. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

## 5) What does each get right—and what does each miss?

### Anderson

**Strength:** He explains why nations feel real without needing face-to-face community, and why media and language matter so much.
**Weakness:** He can underplay coercion, state administration, and the uneven institutional machinery that turns imagination into power. ([Verso][2])

### Gellner

**Strength:** He explains why nationalism is tightly connected to modern schooling, standardization, and state-building.
**Weakness:** His industrial-modernist linkage can be too strong; even sympathetic critics note the fit between industrialization and nationalism is not always tight. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

### Smith

**Strength:** He explains the affective depth and historical durability of nations better than flat modernism does.
**Weakness:** He risks smuggling too much continuity from premodern ethnies into modern nations, making nationhood look less discontinuous than it often is. A Cambridge discussion of Smith explicitly places him between modernism and stronger perennial claims. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][7])

### Brubaker

**Strength:** He is the sharpest on **method**. He shows that analysts often reify nations and ethnicities instead of studying the state categories and political projects that produce them.
**Weakness:** He can seem thinner on the emotional and symbolic content of nationalism than Anderson or Smith, and less equipped than Gellner to explain why modern mass society repeatedly generates national forms. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

## 7) Best synthesis

The best way to use them together is not to pick one winner.

Use **Anderson** for how nations become imaginable.
Use **Gellner** for why modern states and economies need standardized national culture.
Use **Smith** for why nationalism reaches backward into myth, memory, and sacred history.
Use **Brubaker** for how states, categories, and political struggles make nations operationally real—and for avoiding the mistake of treating them as already-coherent entities. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][1])

## Red Team Review

The standard textbook simplification—Anderson and Gellner as “modernists,” Smith as “ethno-symbolist,” Brubaker as “constructivist critic”—is directionally right but too neat. Anderson and Gellner are not interchangeable modernists; Smith is not a simple primordialist; and Brubaker is not just adding another origin theory but attacking the analytical habit of turning categories into groups. ([Verso][2])

## Known unknowns

## Preamble

Applied directly to **China/Xinjiang/Tibet**, the four theorists do **not** perform equally well.

* **Brubaker** is strongest on the **state project**, classification, and the problem of reifying “Uyghurs” or “Tibetans” as fully unified actors.
* **Anderson** is strongest on how national belonging becomes **imaginable**, especially through language, narrative, and mediated simultaneity.
* **Gellner** is strongest on why a modern state pushes hard for **linguistic standardization, schooling, mobility, and cultural homogenization**.
* **Anthony D. Smith** is strongest on why Tibetan and Uyghur identities can carry deep affective force through **myth, memory, religion, sacred territory, and historical continuity**. ([OUP Academic][1])

The correct move is not to pick one winner. It is to assign each theorist to the part of the problem they explain best.

## Topic map

There are three distinct objects here:
**China** as a state-building project; **Xinjiang** as a securitized frontier of classification and incorporation; **Tibet** as a frontier where religion, territory, and historical memory are unusually central. Oxford’s overview of nationalism and minorities in China describes post-1949 minority policy as combining the Qing legacy with a socialist multiethnic state project, and notes that in the PRC ethnic diversity was restructured according to a majority–minority dichotomy. ([OUP Academic][1])

## 1) Benedict Anderson applied to China/Xinjiang/Tibet

Anderson is useful where the question is: **how is a national community imagined across distance?** In the Chinese case, this helps explain the construction of a broad multiethnic Chinese nation in which frontier regions are narrated as integral parts of one national historical space. Townsend’s classic discussion of Chinese nationalism already stressed that the shift from culturalism to nationalism in China was real but more complex than a simple European template, which fits Anderson’s concern with changing forms of collective imagination. ([JSTOR][2])

For **Xinjiang**, Anderson helps with the cultural mechanics of incorporation: Mandarin education, shared national narratives, textbooks, and developmental storytelling can be read as attempts to make Uyghur students imagine themselves inside a wider Chinese national community. Recent work on Xinjiang Class schools explicitly describes them as a central nation-building initiative shaping identity transformation, while another study examines how ethnic identity, national identity, and perceived mobility interact in such settings. ([Taylor & Francis Online][3])

For **Tibet**, Anderson helps less than Smith. He can explain how exile communities, media, ritual circulation, and texts help sustain a translocal Tibetan imagined community, but he is weaker on the specifically religious and sacred dimensions that structure Tibetan nationalism. Kolås’s study argues that Tibetan Buddhism, rather than a secular nationalist ideology alone, supplies vital idioms for Tibetan political identity within Tibet and in exile. That pushes beyond Anderson’s strongest terrain. ([OUP Academic][4])

**Judgment:** Anderson is good on **imagination, media, language, and narrative scale**, but weaker on coercive administration and on the non-secular symbolic depth of Tibetan politics. ([OUP Academic][4])

## 2) Ernest Gellner applied to China/Xinjiang/Tibet

Gellner is most useful where the question is: **why does a modern state demand standardization?** This fits China very well. The PRC’s frontier governance has repeatedly involved schooling, linguistic hierarchy, bureaucratic integration, labor mobility, and developmental modernization. Oxford’s overview describes minority policy as part of a long nation-building process that internalized earlier center–periphery relations within the modern state. That is recognizably Gellnerian terrain. ([OUP Academic][1])

In **Xinjiang**, Gellner is especially strong. Research on Xinjiang schooling and language policy shows how Uyghur-language classrooms are organized within a larger Chinese sociolinguistic hierarchy, and recent work on Xinjiang Class schools shows identity being shaped through education, mobility, and state-led modernization. These are classic Gellner mechanisms: mass education, standard language, and social mobility tied to state-building. ([ETH Zurich Files][5])

In **Tibet**, Gellner still helps with schooling and bureaucratic incorporation, but he explains less about why Tibetan identity is so deeply fused with religion and sacred geography. He captures the state’s homogenizing push better than he captures the lived symbolic world that resists or refracts that push. Kolås’s article makes that limitation obvious: Tibetan nationalism is not well reduced to modernization alone because religious idioms remain central to political identity. ([OUP Academic][4])

**Judgment:** Gellner is strongest on the **state side** of the China/Xinjiang/Tibet equation: why a modernizing state standardizes language, education, and administrative culture. He is weaker on why Tibetan or Uyghur nationhood has deep historical-emotional resonance. ([ETH Zurich Files][5])

## 3) Anthony D. Smith applied to China/Xinjiang/Tibet

Smith is strongest where the question is: **why do these identities feel historically thick, morally charged, and symbolically durable?** He is therefore especially useful for **Tibet**, where religion, sacred history, memory, and territory are inseparable. Kolås shows that Tibetan political identity relies heavily on Buddhist symbolism both inside Tibet and in exile. That is much closer to Smith’s ethno-symbolic sensibility than to a purely modernist account. ([OUP Academic][4])

Smith also has real value for **Uyghur** identity, though less cleanly than for Tibet. Uyghur identity is sustained not only by present-day policy conflict but by historical memory, language, Islam, and place-based belonging. Reports on village renaming and cultural erasure claims in Xinjiang point to conflict over precisely those symbolic materials—names, religious references, local memory, and historical markers—that Smith would treat as crucial to collective durability. ([AP News][6])

For **China as a state project**, however, Smith is less complete. He can explain why frontier identities persist, but he is weaker on the administrative and classificatory machinery through which the PRC governs minorities. He explains symbolic depth better than institutional process. ([OUP Academic][1])

**Judgment:** Smith is best for the **minority-side depth** of Tibet and, to a somewhat lesser extent, Xinjiang: sacred territory, memory, language, religion, and historical continuity. ([OUP Academic][4])

## 4) Rogers Brubaker applied to China/Xinjiang/Tibet

Brubaker is the sharpest direct analytical tool here, with one important caveat. He works best through **nationalizing state**, **groupism**, **groupness**, and **classification**, but less cleanly through the full **external homeland** corner of his classic triad. His original triadic model was explicitly framed around post-Soviet and post-communist Europe. ([irihs.ihs.ac.at][7])

Applied to **China**, Brubaker’s “nationalizing state” fits strongly. China is formally a multiethnic state, but the center pursues an integrationist project that defines the acceptable terms of belonging. Oxford’s overview says the PRC restructured ethnic diversity into a majority–minority dichotomy; that is exactly the kind of state-produced field Brubaker helps us analyze. ([OUP Academic][1])

Applied to **Xinjiang**, Brubaker is strongest of all four. He tells you not to treat “Uyghurs” as a fixed collective substance, but to examine how classification, education, securitization, renaming, language policy, and labor or school institutions produce and reshape groupness. The Xinjiang Class studies and the literature on language hierarchy fit this very well. ([Taylor & Francis Online][3])

Applied to **Tibet**, Brubaker still helps, especially against lazy reification. But he does less than Smith to explain why Tibetan identity is so densely religious and civilizational. Tibet also fits Brubaker’s full triad poorly, because there is no straightforward sovereign “external homeland state”; exile networks and diaspora politics matter, but they are not the same thing. ([OUP Academic][4])

**Judgment:** Brubaker is best for **classification, state projects, group-making, and methodological discipline**. He is less strong on sacred-symbolic depth and less cleanly portable in the “external homeland” dimension. ([irihs.ihs.ac.at][7])

## Preamble

Adding **Eric Hobsbawm, John Breuilly, and Miroslav Hroch** improves the discussion substantially. The earlier set was strong on imagination, modernization, symbols, and classification. What it lacked was a sharper account of **invented historical tradition** (Hobsbawm), **nationalism as politics aimed at state power** (Breuilly), and **how small nations are mobilized in stages by patriotic elites before mass uptake** (Hroch). These three are not redundant. They fill different gaps in Anderson, Gellner, Smith, and Brubaker. ([OUP Academic][1])

## 1) Eric Hobsbawm — brief bio

Eric Hobsbawm was a **British historian**, one of the most influential Marxist historians of the 20th century, and a major analyst of nationalism, modernity, and political tradition. In the nationalism field he is especially associated with *Nations and Nationalism since 1780* and, with Terence Ranger, *The Invention of Tradition*. Britannica directly associates him with the idea that “traditions” presented as ancient are often modern constructions, including the well-known example of Highland Scottish symbols such as kilts and bagpipes being reworked into national tradition. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][2])

### Hobsbawm’s theory

Hobsbawm is broadly **modernist**, but with a distinctive emphasis. He treats nations and nationalism as **historically recent**, closely tied to the modern state, mass politics, literacy, and the political use of the past. His best-known move is to show that many “ancient” national traditions are not ancient at all, but **invented traditions**: recent practices presented as old in order to create continuity, legitimacy, and identity. Britannica’s summary of neotraditionalism uses Hobsbawm’s Scottish example precisely this way. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][2])

In practice, Hobsbawm asks:
how do states and elites **fabricate historical continuity**, ritual, ceremony, and symbol to stabilize modern nationhood? His contribution is especially useful where nationalism leans heavily on flags, school rituals, commemorations, heroic lineages, and “timeless” customs that are actually politically assembled. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][2])

### Why Hobsbawm matters for China/Xinjiang/Tibet

For **China**, Hobsbawm helps analyze how the state narrates seamless historical continuity between empire, nation, frontier, and civilization. For **Xinjiang** and **Tibet**, he is useful for studying official heritage work, ritualized unity discourse, museum histories, school memory, and state narratives that present contingent modern arrangements as natural civilizational continuity. He is less useful than Smith for sacred-symbolic depth from the minority side, and less useful than Brubaker for classification and group-making. But he is very good on **the political manufacture of continuity**. This fills a real gap in the earlier framework. ([Encyclopedia Britannica][2])

## 2) John Breuilly — brief bio

John Breuilly was a **historian of nationalism**, especially associated with *Nationalism and the State* and later editorial and synthetic work such as *The Oxford Handbook of the History of Nationalism*. Cambridge and Oxford references consistently treat him as a central theorist of nationalism whose approach focuses on politics, statehood, and the organization of support. A recent Cambridge chapter summarizes his classic position: nationalism involves the organization of mass support for political purposes in relation to state power. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

### Breuilly’s theory

Breuilly’s core argument is that nationalism should be understood first as **politics**, not primarily as sentiment, myth, or identity. Nationalist movements matter because they are projects aimed at **obtaining or controlling state power**. He is therefore suspicious of explanations that begin and end with culture or consciousness. The central question for him is: who is mobilizing, against what state, for what political objective, and under what institutional conditions? Cambridge materials discussing his work present him as a theorist of nationalism in relation to state management and political organization. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

This makes Breuilly especially strong on:

* nationalist movements against empires,
* nationalism as opposition to existing states,
* the political uses of nationalist rhetoric,
* and the relationship between elites, institutions, and mass mobilization. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

### Why Breuilly matters for China/Xinjiang/Tibet

Breuilly is useful when the question is not merely “how are Tibetans or Uyghurs imagined?” but **what political claims are being made, by whom, and toward what kind of state transformation?** He is therefore particularly helpful for distinguishing between:

* cultural identity,
* ethnic classification,
* dissidence,
* separatism,
* autonomy claims,
* and state securitization of those claims.

He is stronger than Anderson or Smith on the **political-strategic** side of minority nationalism. He is weaker than Brubaker on category-making, and weaker than Gellner on modernization as a structural force. But for ex-empires, borderlands, and minority movements, Breuilly is often indispensable because he restores the question of **power and state contestation**. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][4])

## 3) Miroslav Hroch — brief bio

Miroslav Hroch is a **Czech historian** best known for comparative work on the formation of **smaller European nations**, especially in *Social Preconditions of National Revival in Europe*. Cambridge lists the book as a comparative analysis of the social composition of patriotic groups among smaller European nations, and later *Nationalities Papers* marked his continuing influence through a dedicated special issue on his model. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

### Hroch’s theory

Hroch’s signature contribution is a staged model of national movements among “small nations.” His well-known **A–B–C scheme** describes how national movements often develop:

* **Phase A**: scholarly or intellectual interest in the language, history, and culture of a population;
* **Phase B**: patriotic agitation by activists trying to turn cultural work into political mobilization;
* **Phase C**: mass national movement, where broader layers of the population are mobilized.

The Cambridge references and later discussions of Hroch explicitly identify his work with comparative analysis of patriotic groups and with the long influence of the A–B–C model. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

Hroch is especially strong because he links **social composition** and **mobilization sequence**. He asks who the early patriots are, what class and occupational bases they come from, and how or whether a broader population is drawn into national politics. He is therefore more processual than Smith, less structural than Gellner, and more movement-oriented than Anderson. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

### Why Hroch matters for China/Xinjiang/Tibet

Hroch is most useful not for the Chinese state as such, but for analyzing **minority national formation** or attempted national formation. For **Tibet**, one could ask how intellectual, religious, and diasporic work corresponds to Hroch’s phases, and whether Tibetan nationalism has moved unevenly between elite-cultural and mass-political forms. For **Xinjiang**, Hroch helps ask whether Uyghur identity is operating mainly as a culturally reproduced category, an activist project, or a mass national movement, and how state repression interrupts or reshapes those sequences. He is less directly applicable than Brubaker because his original model was built around smaller European nations, but he is valuable for **sequencing and social composition of mobilization**. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][5])

## Quick read of the table

The table points to a clear division of labor.

**For China as a state project**, the strongest pairing is **Brubaker + Gellner**. Brubaker explains the **nationalizing-state** logic and the classification of populations into administratively legible majority/minority positions; Gellner explains why a modern state presses toward linguistic, educational, and administrative standardization. Recent reporting on China’s new ethnic unity law, framed around building a stronger unified Chinese national consciousness and strengthening Mandarin-centered integration, fits that combination especially well. ([AP News][5])

**For Xinjiang**, the strongest trio is **Brubaker + Gellner + Breuilly**. Brubaker handles classification, groupness, and securitization; Gellner handles modernization, schooling, mobility, and standardization; Breuilly helps distinguish cultural difference from political projects aimed at state power. Scholarship on China often centers minority governance discussions on Xinjiang, and Mao-era theory tied minority nationality tightly to territorial sovereignty rather than open constituent sovereignty. ([OUP Academic][2])

**For Tibet**, the strongest trio is **Smith + Brubaker + Anderson**. Smith best captures religion, sacred territory, myth, and historical memory; Brubaker guards against treating “Tibetans” as a permanently unified political block; Anderson helps with translocal imagination through exile, texts, and mediated community. ([OUP Academic][2])

## Best composite framework

If forced into one integrated model for **China / Xinjiang / Tibet**, the most defensible synthesis is:

* **Brubaker** for **state classification and majority–minority governance**
* **Gellner** for **modernizing standardization**
* **Smith** for **religion, memory, and sacred attachment**
* **Anderson** for **imagined scale and mediated community**
* **Hobsbawm** for **invented continuity and ritualized historical legitimacy**
* **Breuilly** for **political claims on or against the state**
* **Hroch** for **stages of minority national mobilization**. ([nationalism.ceu.edu][1])

## Red Team Review

The weakness in any master table like this is obvious: it can imply each theorist cleanly “owns” one dimension of the case. That is false. Gellner can still say something about Tibet; Smith can still say something about the Chinese state; Brubaker can still illuminate Tibet; Breuilly can still matter for Xinjiang. The table is useful only as a map of **relative explanatory advantage**, not as a set of sealed compartments.
