## Key Theorists — Full Table

### NQH (Western Revisionist School)

| Scholar | Dates / Institution | Key Work(s) | Central Argument | Core Themes | Relation to NQH Debate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pamela Kyle Crossley** | b. 1955 / Dartmouth | *A Translucent Mirror* (1999); *Orphan Warriors* (1990) | Qing emperors theorized "simultaneity" — universal sovereign identity co-equally Manchu, Confucian, Buddhist, Inner Asian. Not strategic performance but cosmological claim. | Simultaneity; imperial ideology; sovereign identity; Manchu construction; multiethnic legitimation | Foundational NQH theorist. Most ideologically ambitious; most contested by Chinese critics. Directly attacks sinicization at the level of imperial self-conception. |
| **Mark Elliott** | b. 1966 / Harvard | *The Manchu Way* (2001); *Emperor Qianlong* (2009) | Banner system was primary ethnic boundary mechanism — spatial segregation, legal separation, economic isolation maintained Manchu distinctiveness. "Ethnic sovereignty" as operative legitimating principle. | Banner system; ethnic boundaries; institutional history; spatial segregation; identity decay | NQH co-founder. More empirically grounded than Crossley. Banner decay evidence creates tension with simultaneity ideology — ideology constructed as material basis eroded. |
| **Evelyn Rawski** | b. 1939 / Pittsburgh | *Reenvisioning the Qing* (1996 AHA); *The Last Emperors* (1998) | Qing success derived from deliberate refusal to fully sinicize. Multiethnic empire governed through parallel frameworks: Mongol marriage alliance, Tibetan Buddhist patronage, Lifanyuan. | Anti-sinicization; Mongol alliance; Tibetan Buddhism; Lifanyuan; archival methodology | Crystallized NQH as a school with direct challenge to Ping-ti Ho. Most institutionally prominent NQH statement. |
| **James Millward** | b. 1961 / Georgetown | *Beyond the Pass* (1998); *Eurasian Crossroads* (2007) | Qing governed Xinjiang through hybrid non-Chinese frameworks — beg system, military colonies, Andijani merchants — with shallow cultural penetration. 1884 provincialization, not 1759 conquest, marks genuine integration. | Frontier history; beg system; fiscal-military empire; Xinjiang; Muslim oasis society | NQH's frontier voice. Most attentive to non-Han subject perspectives and imperial violence. Most politically exposed on contemporary Xinjiang. |

### Chinese Respondents

| Scholar | Dates / Institution | Key Work(s) | Central Argument | Core Themes | Relation to NQH Debate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ge Zhaoguang** | b. 1950 / Fudan | *Here in "China" I Dwell* (2011); *What Is China?* (2014); *History of Chinese Thought* (1998–2001) | Chinese civilizational continuity is historically real — grounded in written language, scholar-gentry, Confucian frameworks. NQH mistakes court ideology for empire; ordinary Han experience is evidence too. | Civilizational continuity; written language; scholar-gentry; anti-NQH methodology; Zhongguo identity | Most sophisticated Chinese response. Reverses anachronism charge against NQH. Defends historical coherence of "China" without nationalist essentialism. |
| **Zhong Han** | contemp. / Inner Mongolia Univ. | Articles critiquing NQH source readings (2000s–2010s) | NQH misreads and decontextualizes Manchu documents. Crossley's simultaneity imposes postmodern identity theory onto pragmatic court administration. | Source criticism; archival rebuttal; anti-simultaneity; empirical methodology | Most technically detailed Chinese critic. Source-by-source rebuttal of NQH archival claims. |
| **Feng Erkang** | b. 1937 / Nankai | Studies in Qing Social History | NQH overstates Manchu distinctiveness; misses social evidence of Han-Manchu integration in daily administrative and social life. | Social history; Han-Manchu integration; administrative Confucianism | Senior Chinese critic alongside Ge and Zhong Han. Emphasizes social history evidence NQH court methodology misses. |
| **Fei Xiaotong** | 1910–2005 / Peking Univ. | *Zhonghua Minzu Is a Unified Entity* (1988) | "Zhonghua minzu" constitutes a "pluralistic unity" (duoyuan yiti) — 56 ethnic groups forming a unified nation. Yuan and Qing are Chinese dynasties as members of the multiethnic Chinese nation. | Zhonghua minzu; pluralistic unity; multiethnic nationalism; ethnic classification | Provides theoretical foundation for PRC incorporation of Yuan and Qing as Chinese dynasties. Projects modern category onto pre-modern periods. |
| **Ping-ti Ho** | 1917–2012 / Chicago | *The Significance of the Ch'ing Period* (1967) | Qing success derived from sinicization — Manchu wholesale adoption of Han institutions, Confucian ideology, administrative structures. Manchu identity was thinning fiction. | Sinicization; Han institutional absorption; Confucian governance; civilizational gravity | Primary target of NQH revisionism. His 1967 article is the thesis Rawski's 1996 address directly dismantles. |

### Yuan / Mongol Studies

| Scholar | Dates / Institution | Key Work(s) | Central Argument | Core Themes | Relation to NQH Debate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Thomas Allsen** | 1940–2019 / College of New Jersey | *Mongol Imperialism* (1987); *Culture and Conquest in Mongol Eurasia* (2001) | Yuan was eastern node of Eurasian imperial system — the Pax Mongolica connecting China to Persia, Central Asia, Russia. Inner Asian framing insufficient; requires Eurasian scale. | Eurasian world system; Pax Mongolica; cross-cultural exchange; non-China-centric | Most important Yuan parallel to NQH. His Eurasian framing exceeds NQH's Inner Asian frame — suggests NQH's decentering of China may not go far enough. |
| **Morris Rossabi** | b. 1941 / CUNY-Columbia | *Khubilai Khan: His Life and Times* (1988) | Kublai was genuinely bifurcated — inhabiting Chinese and Mongol cultural worlds simultaneously without fully belonging to either. Parallel to Crossley's Qianlong but more pragmatic, less ideologically theorized. | Kublai Khan; bifurcated identity; Chinese-Mongol synthesis; pragmatic rulership | Yuan parallel to NQH simultaneity argument. Kublai's cultural bifurcation anticipated Crossley's framework without NQH theoretical apparatus. |
| **David Robinson** | contemp. / Colgate | *Martial Spectacles of the Ming Court* (2013); *Empire's Twilight* (2009) | Yuan court culture was unstable hybrid performance — neither Chinese nor essentialist Mongol but specific to the Yuan court's contested environment. Synthesis continuously negotiated, never resolved. | Court culture; cultural performance; hybrid identity; martial spectacle; instability | Yuan parallel to Crossley's methodology. More attentive to instability of synthesis than Crossley's more stable simultaneity. |
| **Elizabeth Endicott-West** | contemp. | *Mongolian Rule in China* (1989) | Yuan local administration was pragmatic hybrid — adapted Chinese administrative forms while maintaining Mongol personnel hierarchies. Neither sinicization nor pure alien imposition. | Local administration; pragmatic hybridism; administrative adaptation | Yuan parallel to Elliott's institutional approach. Suggests NQH multiethnic governance sophistication is a general conquest dynasty feature. |
| **John Dardess** | b. 1937 / Kansas | *Conquerors and Confucians* (1973) | Confucianization of Yuan governance was genuine — Yuan emperors engaged substantively with Confucian political frameworks as governing ideology. | Confucianization; Yuan political thought; Chinese literati advisors | Creates same tension with Inner Asian-focused Yuan scholars that Ho creates against NQH — structural parallel to the Qing assimilation debate. |
| **Frederick Hok-lam Chan** | 1937–2021 / Washington | *Legitimation in Imperial China* (1984) | Kublai and Yuan emperors achieved genuine institutional synthesis of Chinese and Mongol frameworks — substantive political integration, not strategic performance. | Yuan legitimation; institutional synthesis; dual governance | Moderate synthesis position for Yuan — parallel to how Elliott moderates between Ho and Crossley for the Qing. |

### Classical / Traditional Chinese Historiography

| Scholar | Dates | Key Work(s) | Central Argument | Core Themes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sima Qian** | 145–86 BCE | *Shiji* (Records of the Grand Historian) | History organized as sequential dynastic biographies encoding moral cosmology. Political legitimacy as primary unit of historical time. | Dynastic cycle; moral historiography; Mandate of Heaven; benji annals |
| **Ouyang Xiu** | 1007–1072 | *New History of the Tang*; *New History of the Five Dynasties* | Zhengtong requires virtue and effective governance — not automatic on territorial control. Central architect of orthodox succession debate. | Zhengtong; orthodox succession; dynastic legitimacy; moral governance |
| **Zhu Xi** | 1130–1200 | *Tongjian Gangmu* | Zhengtong requires moral legitimacy, not just military control. Jin dynasty lacked it despite holding north China; Southern Song retained orthodox succession. | Zhengtong; moral legitimacy; Song-Jin succession; Neo-Confucian historiography |
| **Hu Sansheng** | 1230–1302 | Commentary on *Zizhi Tongjian* | Resistance historiography under Mongol rule — preserving Song loyalist memory and implicitly contesting Mongol legitimacy through classical commentary. | Resistance historiography; Song loyalism; commentary tradition; implicit legitimacy critique |
| **Liang Qichao** | 1873–1929 | *New Historiography* (1902) | Dynastic periodization incompatible with modern historical science — serves dynastic politics. Called for historiography centered on the Chinese nation. | Anti-dynastic periodization; modern historiography; nationalist history; methodological reform |
| **Guo Moruo** | 1892–1978 | *Studies on Ancient Chinese Society* | Marxist periodization: feudalism began with Western Zhou; Shang was slave society. Chinese history follows universal five-stage developmental schema. | Marxist periodization; slave society; feudalism origins; five-stage schema |
| **Fan Wenlan** | 1893–1969 | *General History of China* | Feudalism began at Qin-Han unification, not Western Zhou (contra Guo Moruo). Periodization debate encoded ideological stakes about Chinese historical development. | Marxist periodization; feudalism; Qin-Han transition; historical materialism |
