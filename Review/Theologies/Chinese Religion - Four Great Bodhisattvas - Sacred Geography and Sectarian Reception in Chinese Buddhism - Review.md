## 1. Introduction: Bodhisattvas, Localization, and Chinese Buddhist Religious Worlds

Chinese Buddhism did not simply inherit Indian Mahāyāna bodhisattvas as a fixed pantheon. It reworked them into a set of religious worlds: scriptural, liturgical, visual, geographic, and sectarian. In that transformation, bodhisattvas became more than doctrinal exemplars on the path to Buddhahood. They became objects of pilgrimage, loci of miracle literature, anchors of temple cult, organizers of sacred mountains, and mediating figures through whom Chinese Buddhists connected cosmology, devotion, and daily religious need. Mahāyāna as a movement made bodhisattvas central to the religious imagination of East Asia, and by the medieval period it had become the dominant influence on the Buddhist cultures of Central and East Asia. Within that broader Mahāyāna framework, Chinese Buddhism developed especially powerful bodhisattva-centered forms of devotion and interpretation.

A review of Chinese Buddhist bodhisattvas therefore has to do more than summarize names and attributes. It must explain how certain figures came to matter so much in China, why some became attached to specific mountains, why others became associated with particular rituals or sectarian systems, and how texts, places, and images reinforced one another over time. The standard grouping of the Four Great Bodhisattvas — 觀音, 文殊, 普賢, and 地藏 — is the clearest expression of this process. The grouping is not merely mnemonic. It condenses a larger religious logic in which compassion, wisdom, practice, and salvific vow are differentiated, institutionalized, and localized. At the same time, Chinese Buddhist life cannot be reduced to these four alone. Figures such as 彌勒, 大勢至, and 藥師 complicate the picture by showing that Chinese reception was not governed by one closed symbolic system, but by multiple overlapping devotional economies.

The central claim of this article is that Chinese Buddhism localized bodhisattvas through four mutually reinforcing processes: textual canonization, sacred geography, visual-literary stabilization, and sectarian reception. Canonical Mahāyāna texts established the doctrinal and salvific profiles of bodhisattvas; Chinese sacred geography mapped those profiles onto mountains and pilgrimage networks; iconography and miracle literature made them visible and narratable; and traditions such as 天台, 華嚴, 禪, and 淨土 selectively amplified different figures according to their own scriptural and practical priorities. The result was not a simple transplantation of Indian Mahāyāna into China, but a specifically Chinese religious ecology in which bodhisattvas became embedded in local landscapes, institutional routines, and patterns of popular devotion.

This framing also requires caution. "Chinese Buddhism" was never a single, homogeneous system. Sectarian identities were porous, temple practice was often mixed, and pilgrimage, ritual, and lay devotion routinely crossed doctrinal boundaries. A bodhisattva might be interpreted one way in a scholastic context, another way in temple liturgy, and still another in local miracle tales. Accordingly, the purpose of this review is not to impose a rigid taxonomy, but to identify dominant patterns of reception and the mechanisms by which they were stabilized. The article treats bodhisattvas not as isolated theological entities, but as nodes in broader networks of text, place, ritual, and historical memory.

### 1.1 Scope and Aims

This review focuses primarily on the bodhisattvas most central to the Chinese Buddhist religious imagination: 觀音菩薩, 文殊菩薩, 普賢菩薩, and 地藏菩薩. These four form the best-known Chinese grouping and are closely associated with the Four Sacred Mountains — 五臺山, 普陀山, 峨眉山, and 九華山 — which became major pilgrimage centers in Chinese Buddhism. Because this grouping is both canonical in later Chinese religious culture and analytically useful, it serves as the article's primary axis. Yet the article also extends comparison to 彌勒菩薩, 大勢至菩薩, and 藥師佛, since these figures reveal important limits in the explanatory power of the fourfold system. 彌勒 shows broad cross-sectarian portability; 大勢至 demonstrates a stronger fit within 淨土 devotion; and 藥師 shows how a highly important ritual figure may cut across sectarian boundaries without becoming the defining emblem of one school.

The article has four main aims. First, it clarifies the doctrinal and symbolic roles of the major bodhisattvas in Chinese Buddhism. Second, it reconstructs the historical formation of mountain–bodhisattva pairings and shows that these were produced unevenly rather than given from the beginning. Third, it distinguishes the textual basis of bodhisattva cults from their later Chinese localization in pilgrimage, art, and ritual. Fourth, it compares the sectarian reception of these figures in 天台, 華嚴, 禪, and 淨土, arguing that sectarian reception is best understood in terms of scriptural center of gravity rather than exclusive ownership. In this sense, the review is designed not as a catalogue of devotional figures but as an analysis of how Chinese Buddhism converted Mahāyāna bodhisattvas into enduring cultural institutions.

A secondary aim is comparative. By placing Buddhist sacred mountains alongside earlier and parallel Chinese mountain cults, especially those tied to Daoist and imperial sacred geography, the article seeks to show that Buddhist localization occurred within an already sacralized landscape. This wider context matters because it prevents the standard Four Sacred Mountains model from being read as self-explanatory. In many cases Buddhism was not creating sacred mountains ex nihilo; it was overlaying, redirecting, and reinterpreting older patterns of mountain sanctity.

### 1.2 Method and Framing

Methodologically, this article adopts a synthetic review approach that combines four lenses: textual, historical, iconographic, and reception-historical. The textual lens identifies how bodhisattvas are presented in major Mahāyāna scriptures and related traditions. The historical lens asks when and how those figures became attached to concrete Chinese sites, institutions, and devotional habits. The iconographic lens considers how bodhisattvas and sacred mountains became visually recognizable in paintings, temple sculpture, and pilgrimage imagery. The reception-historical lens examines how different Chinese Buddhist traditions appropriated the same figures differently according to their own doctrinal priorities. This multi-lens method is necessary because no single source type explains the Chinese Buddhist bodhisattva fully. Scripture alone does not explain mountain cult; pilgrimage alone does not explain doctrinal authority; sectarian labels alone do not explain popular devotion.

The article also distinguishes between canonical basis and Chinese cultic elaboration. This distinction is essential. A sutra may establish a bodhisattva's general salvific role, but it usually does not, by itself, determine later Chinese pilgrimage geography or visual form. For example, the importance of mountain pilgrimage in Buddhism is a widespread phenomenon, but in China specific mountains — 峨眉山, 五臺山, 普陀山, and 九華山 — became especially important as the homes of particular bodhisattvas through processes of localization, temple accumulation, and retrospective narrative consolidation. Treating those later associations as if they were already explicit in the earliest canonical sources would flatten the historical process that made Chinese Buddhism distinctive.

A further methodological principle concerns the use of the term "sect." In the Chinese Buddhist context, traditions such as 天台, 華嚴, 禪, and 淨土 should not be imagined as mutually sealed confessional blocs. They are better understood as textual-doctrinal centers of gravity within a highly porous religious field. A temple might maintain 禪 liturgy, perform 淨土 recitation, and enshrine 觀音 or 地藏 without perceiving any contradiction. Likewise, Chinese monks were ordained within shared vinaya structures while still participating in distinctively Mahāyāna vows and devotional systems. This article therefore treats sectarian reception comparatively and probabilistically: not in terms of exclusive possession, but in terms of stronger or weaker affinity between a figure and a tradition's dominant textual and ritual priorities.

Finally, the article adopts a localization model rather than a diffusion-only model. The question is not simply how Indian bodhisattvas "spread" to China. It is how they were re-sited, re-imagined, and institutionalized within Chinese religious space. Sacred mountains, mountain monasteries, pilgrimage routes, local miracle narratives, and the prestige of named landscapes all played decisive roles in this transformation. In this sense, the study of bodhisattvas in Chinese Buddhism is simultaneously a study of doctrine, landscape, and cultural translation.

With this framework in place, the next section turns to the Four Great Bodhisattvas themselves. Rather than introducing them as a flat list, it will analyze them as a differentiated Chinese Mahāyāna system in which 觀音, 文殊, 普賢, and 地藏 came to organize distinct but overlapping domains of compassion, wisdom, practice, and vow.

## 2. The Four Great Bodhisattvas as a Chinese Mahāyāna System

The Four Great Bodhisattvas — 觀音菩薩, 文殊菩薩, 普賢菩薩, and 地藏菩薩 — are among the most recognizable figures in Chinese Buddhism. Yet their importance is not exhausted by familiarity. In the Chinese Buddhist religious imagination, these four do not function merely as a loose collection of admired beings. They form a practical symbolic system through which core Mahāyāna ideals were differentiated, ritualized, and made available to distinct religious needs. Read together, they distribute across four major axes of the path: compassion, wisdom, practice, and vow under conditions of suffering and death. That fourfold configuration is not a single canonical schema stated once and for all in one source; rather, it is the mature result of Chinese reception, in which scriptural profiles, pilgrimage networks, temple cults, and sectarian readings gradually converged into a stable devotional set.

This matters because Chinese Buddhism is often misdescribed in one of two inadequate ways. One approach flattens the bodhisattvas into a series of "virtues": Guanyin equals compassion, Mañjuśrī equals wisdom, Samantabhadra equals practice, Kṣitigarbha equals vows. That is not false, but it is too thin. Another approach treats these figures as popular devotional additions to a more properly philosophical Buddhism. That is also inadequate. The four bodhisattvas are not peripheral to Chinese Mahāyāna; they are one of the main ways in which Chinese Buddhism organized its understanding of salvation, cultivation, and religious efficacy. They mediate between doctrine and daily life. Through them, highly abstract Mahāyāna concepts become ritually legible and socially actionable.

A useful way to read the four together is to see them as a Chinese Mahāyāna grammar of religious response. 觀音 addresses immediate suffering through merciful intervention; 文殊 addresses ignorance through wisdom and discernment; 普賢 translates aspiration into disciplined conduct and vow; 地藏 extends salvific care into the darkest zones of karmic suffering, death, and the underworld. The significance of this arrangement is not only symbolic but also institutional. Different temples, mountain cults, sectarian traditions, and lay practices could privilege one figure over another, but the quartet as a whole offered a balanced and durable map of religious life. In effect, Chinese Buddhism came to express major dimensions of the bodhisattva path not through one single universal figure, but through a differentiated constellation.

| Bodhisattva | Primary symbolic center | Dominant religious function | Typical sphere of reception in Chinese Buddhism |
|---|---|---|---|
| 觀音菩薩 | Compassion, mercy, responsiveness | Rescue from suffering, protection, hearing cries of the world | Lay devotion, miracle cult, protection, pilgrimage |
| 文殊菩薩 | Wisdom, prajñā, discernment | Cutting through ignorance, doctrinal authority, awakening insight | Scholasticism, visionary cult, sacred mountain symbolism |
| 普賢菩薩 | Practice, vows, ethical enactment | Repentance, merit dedication, performance of the bodhisattva path | Liturgical practice, vow traditions, moral cultivation |
| 地藏菩薩 | Redemptive vow, filiality, endurance | Rescue of beings in hell, care for the dead, karmic remediation | Funerary religion, memorial rites, afterlife devotion |

### 2.1 觀音: Compassion and Responsive Salvation

Among the four, 觀音菩薩 is the most immediately accessible and, in much of Chinese religious life, the most widely beloved. The central symbolic feature of Guanyin is not merely compassion in the abstract, but responsive compassion — compassion that hears, answers, and intervenes. This distinction is critical. Guanyin is not simply admired as morally compassionate; Guanyin is approached as a figure who can actually respond to crisis, danger, fear, and grief. That is why Guanyin devotion became so pervasive across social classes, regions, and institutional settings. The figure bridges elite Mahāyāna soteriology and ordinary lay religiosity with unusual ease.

In Chinese Buddhism, Guanyin's prominence rests in large part on the way her salvific role can be translated into a wide range of human circumstances: illness, childbirth, danger at sea, family distress, fear of punishment, vulnerability in travel, and emotional suffering. This breadth of application made Guanyin one of the most versatile devotional figures in the Chinese Buddhist world. Theological abstraction and practical urgency converge here. Guanyin's compassion is not merely contemplative or exemplary; it is interventionist. In this respect, Guanyin occupies the pastoral center of the fourfold bodhisattva system.

The flexibility of Guanyin's manifestations is equally important. Chinese reception strongly emphasized the bodhisattva's capacity to appear in whatever form is required by the needs of sentient beings. This feature enabled extraordinary iconographic and devotional expansion. Guanyin could be regal or simple, male-coded or feminized, solitary or embedded in narrative rescue scenes. The proliferation of forms did not weaken the figure's identity; it strengthened it by making Guanyin adaptable to different devotional ecologies. In China, this plasticity became one of the foundations of Guanyin's mass appeal.

At the level of religious function, Guanyin's cult often mediates between Buddhist aspiration and worldly concern. This is sometimes dismissed as "popular religion," but that dismissal is analytically lazy. What Guanyin demonstrates is the extent to which Chinese Buddhism was able to articulate a bodhisattva ideal not only for monks, exegetes, or advanced practitioners, but for ordinary persons confronting contingency and suffering. Guanyin is thus not an embarrassment to doctrinal Buddhism; she is one of the clearest demonstrations of how Mahāyāna universalized itself in Chinese conditions.

### 2.2 文殊: Wisdom, Discernment, and Doctrinal Authority

If Guanyin is the pastoral center of the four, 文殊菩薩 is the intellectual and visionary center. In Chinese Buddhism, 文殊 represents prajñā — not simply book learning, but wisdom that cuts through confusion and sees reality correctly. This is why 文殊 is associated not only with scriptural study and doctrinal interpretation, but also with insight, eloquence, and the awakening of bodhicitta. His importance lies in the fact that suffering, in Mahāyāna thought, is not merely endured; it is generated and sustained by ignorance. 文殊 therefore addresses a more radical level of the religious problem than Guanyin. Where Guanyin hears the cries of beings already in distress, 文殊 targets the delusive cognition that underlies bondage itself.

This distinction helps explain why 文殊 occupies a special place in scholastic and visionary forms of Chinese Buddhism. He is not simply wise in a generic moral sense. He is the bodhisattva whose function is to clarify, discriminate, and illuminate. Hence his repeated association with preaching, philosophical subtlety, and the capacity to articulate the Dharma. If Guanyin is maximally approachable, 文殊 is more often linked to elevation, austerity, and doctrinal refinement. Yet that does not make him marginal. On the contrary, he anchors the intellectual legitimacy of the bodhisattva system.

The symbolism of 文殊 also serves a structural function within the fourfold scheme. Chinese Buddhism did not allow compassion alone to stand as sufficient. Mercy without wisdom risks sentimentality; intervention without discernment risks spiritual superficiality. 文殊 therefore counterbalances Guanyin by insisting that salvation must involve correct seeing. This is one reason why Chinese Buddhists repeatedly paired wisdom and compassion, whether explicitly or implicitly. In the fourfold system, 文殊 secures the cognitive dimension of liberation.

At the level of reception, 文殊 often appears less in the intimate register of lay petition and more in the high register of sacred prestige, mountain cult, and doctrinal authority. This is not an absolute distinction, but it is a useful one. He functions as the bodhisattva most readily aligned with religious elites, scriptural cultures, and cosmological vision. Yet that too is part of his broader Chinese role: not every major bodhisattva had to be emotionally immediate. Some had to uphold the intellectual architecture of Mahāyāna religion itself.

### 2.3 普賢: Practice, Vows, and Enacted Cultivation

If 文殊 represents wisdom, 普賢菩薩 represents what may be called the performative turn of Chinese Mahāyāna. He is the bodhisattva of enacted cultivation: vows, repentance, ethical conduct, dedication of merit, and the disciplined performance of the path. This is a crucial role, because Chinese Buddhism did not understand enlightenment as either pure doctrinal understanding or pure devotional reliance. It required conduct, repetition, ritual, and intentional self-formation. 普賢 gives that dimension a personified focus.

The importance of 普賢 lies precisely in the fact that he refuses to let the path remain abstract. It is possible to admire compassion and to praise wisdom without changing how one lives. 普賢 stands against that possibility. In his religious profile, vows are not rhetorical flourishes but technologies of transformation. Repentance is not merely emotional regret but ritual-moral recalibration. Dedication of merit is not symbolic excess but the extension of practice beyond the self. For this reason, 普賢 is especially important in liturgical, ethical, and vow-centered forms of Chinese Buddhism.

Within the fourfold symbolic system, 普賢 fills a structural gap. If Guanyin responds to suffering and 文殊 clarifies its roots, something still remains to be done: the path must be walked. 普賢 is therefore the bodhisattva of implementation. This gives him a somewhat different texture from the others. He is less frequently the focus of immediate popular rescue narratives than Guanyin and less likely to dominate doctrinal prestige in the manner of 文殊. Yet his role is indispensable. Without 普賢, the system would have compassion and wisdom but no durable account of religious discipline.

The Chinese reception of 普賢 also suggests something broader about Mahāyāna localization. Chinese Buddhism valued ritualized action, cumulative cultivation, repentance ceremonies, and formal vows not as secondary additions but as core religious forms. 普賢 therefore became a figure through whom collective liturgical life and personal ethical aspiration could be harmonized. In this sense, he is one of the clearest examples of how a bodhisattva could become embedded not only in doctrinal literature but in the repetitive forms of temple life itself.

### 2.4 地藏: Redemptive Vow, Death, and Postmortem Care

Among the four, 地藏菩薩 is the most distinctive in social function and emotional atmosphere. Whereas Guanyin is associated with immediate merciful rescue, 文殊 with wisdom, and 普賢 with cultivated practice, 地藏 is concentrated around the themes of great vow under extreme conditions, filiality, afterlife suffering, and rescue of beings in hell. This gives him a particular gravitas within Chinese Buddhism. 地藏 is the bodhisattva of those situations in which ordinary religious consolation may seem least adequate: death, mourning, karmic fear, and the imagined terrors of the underworld.

This is why 地藏 became so central to memorial rites and popular afterlife religion. His cult addresses concerns that are both doctrinal and deeply social. The dead must be cared for. Parents and ancestors must not be abandoned. Karma has consequences beyond visible life. Suffering may continue after death, and religious action on behalf of the dead remains meaningful. 地藏 gathers these concerns into a single salvific figure. In doing so, he creates one of the most powerful bridges between Mahāyāna doctrine and Chinese kinship-based moral worlds.

The emphasis on vow is decisive here. All bodhisattvas have vows, but 地藏's reception in East Asia makes vow itself the core of his charisma. He is the figure who does not withdraw from the darkest realms. If Guanyin often represents immediate compassion for the living, 地藏 represents long endurance on behalf of those whose situation appears spiritually hopeless. This produces a different devotional tone: less immediacy, more perseverance; less rescue from accident, more accompaniment through karmic extremity.

In the fourfold system, 地藏 extends Mahāyāna beyond visible life and into the postmortem imagination. He ensures that Chinese Buddhism does not end its concern with the human at the boundary of death. This makes his role especially important in a Chinese religious context shaped by ancestor concern, memorial obligation, and moral continuity between generations. If Guanyin is the most affectively accessible of the four, 地藏 may be the most existentially grave.

### 2.5 The Four as a System: Complementarity Rather than Equivalence

Having treated the four individually, the larger point can now be stated more precisely: the Four Great Bodhisattvas form a complementary religious system, not a set of equivalent interchangeable devotions. They divide the work of Mahāyāna salvation into differentiated domains. Each addresses a distinct aspect of the human religious condition, and each became associated with particular institutional and emotional registers within Chinese Buddhism. Guanyin dominates crisis devotion and compassionate rescue; 文殊 secures the domain of insight; 普賢 structures cultivation; 地藏 governs the redemptive imagination of death and karmic suffering.

This complementarity also explains the durability of the fourfold scheme. It is broad enough to cover a wide range of religious needs, but differentiated enough to avoid redundancy. A devotee, temple, or sect could foreground one figure without denying the legitimacy of the others. Chinese Buddhism thereby achieved both specialization and integration. The system could sustain sacred mountains, liturgies, visual programs, and sectarian emphases without dissolving into chaos or collapsing into a single universal bodhisattva.

| Religious problem | Bodhisattva most strongly associated | Dominant mode of response |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate suffering, danger, fear | 觀音菩薩 | Merciful intervention, protection, responsive salvation |
| Ignorance, confusion, lack of insight | 文殊菩薩 | Wisdom, discrimination, doctrinal illumination |
| Failure to enact the path | 普賢菩薩 | Vow, repentance, practice, ethical performance |
| Death, hell, karmic extremity, filial concern | 地藏菩薩 | Redemptive endurance, postmortem rescue, memorial care |

The Four Great Bodhisattvas are one of the most sophisticated products of Chinese Buddhist reception history. They condense a broad religious universe into a manageable symbolic order without reducing that universe to a single principle. Their importance lies not only in their individual cults but in their capacity to together represent a Chinese Mahāyāna understanding of what salvation, cultivation, and compassion require.

If this section has treated the Four Great Bodhisattvas as a symbolic and functional system, the next section turns to the spatial dimension of that system. The question becomes how these figures were localized into the Four Sacred Mountains — 五臺山, 普陀山, 峨眉山, and 九華山 — and how sacred geography transformed bodhisattva devotion into pilgrimage, landscape, and institutional permanence.

## 3. Sacred Geography and the Four Sacred Mountains

If the previous section treated the Four Great Bodhisattvas as a differentiated religious system, this section examines the spatial form that system took in Chinese Buddhism. The critical move was localization. Bodhisattvas did not remain only as scriptural or iconographic figures. They were mapped onto mountains, temple networks, pilgrimage routes, and regional devotional economies. The mature result was the standard association of 文殊 with 五臺山, 觀音 with 普陀山, 普賢 with 峨眉山, and 地藏 with 九華山. This fourfold sacred geography is one of the clearest examples of how Chinese Buddhism transformed Mahāyāna ideals into lived landscapes.

The phrase "sacred mountain" can be misleading if treated too passively. These mountains were not merely scenic locations where preexisting devotional content happened to be placed. They functioned as ritual environments that amplified, stabilized, and reshaped the meanings of the bodhisattvas attached to them. In other words, the mountain did not simply host the cult; it became part of the cult's theological grammar. A bodhisattva associated with wisdom, compassion, practice, or postmortem redemption acquired those meanings in more vivid and socially durable form once the cult was anchored to a specific landscape. Sacred geography thus gave religious abstraction a topographic body.

This process also changed the scale of Chinese Buddhism. A bodhisattva attached to a mountain is no longer only an object of temple devotion or scriptural recollection. The bodhisattva becomes the center of pilgrimage circulation, monastic patronage, literary production, visual representation, and regional prestige. Mountains organize movement. They accumulate temples, legends, donations, and imperial attention. They invite narrative consolidation: miracles happen there, masters travel there, visions are reported there, inscriptions are carved there. Through such accumulation, the mountain becomes not merely associated with holiness but actively productive of it.

| Sacred mountain | Associated bodhisattva | Dominant symbolic field | Characteristic religious function |
|---|---|---|---|
| 五臺山 | 文殊菩薩 | Wisdom, vision, doctrinal prestige | Pilgrimage of insight, monastic authority, visionary revelation |
| 普陀山 | 觀音菩薩 | Compassion, refuge, rescue | Protection, mercy, accessibility, maritime salvation |
| 峨眉山 | 普賢菩薩 | Practice, ascent, vow | Embodied cultivation, discipline, ritual enactment |
| 九華山 | 地藏菩薩 | Death, filiality, karmic redemption | Memorial rites, afterlife care, postmortem rescue |

### 3.1 五臺山 and 文殊

Among the four mountain–bodhisattva pairings, 五臺山 and 文殊菩薩 form the most intellectually charged combination. Even in mature popular memory, 五臺山 is not merely a place where one prays to 文殊. It is the mountain of wisdom, sacred vision, doctrinal legitimacy, and transregional Buddhist prestige. The topography matters here. 五臺山 is not imagined primarily as lush refuge or intimate sanctuary, but as a landscape of altitude, austerity, and revelatory distance. Such a setting naturally reinforces the religious persona of 文殊 as the bodhisattva of prajñā, discrimination, and awakened insight.

The symbolic fit is unusually tight. 文殊 addresses the problem of ignorance; 五臺山 spatializes that response as ascent, clarity, and remoteness from ordinary confusion. The mountain becomes the external analogue of the cognitive transformation 文殊 represents. To approach 五臺山 is not merely to travel; it is to enter a zone coded as spiritually elevated and epistemically clarified. This is why the mountain accrued such extraordinary authority in Chinese Buddhist imagination. It was not simply one pilgrimage destination among others, but the landscape most suited to the production of visionary prestige.

This prestige was not confined to internal Chinese religious life. 五臺山 became a nodal site in broader East Asian and Inner Asian Buddhist networks. That wider importance reinforced the mountain's symbolic density. A pilgrimage site associated with wisdom becomes more compelling when it is also recognized as a center of high sanctity across linguistic and regional boundaries. Thus 五臺山 gathered doctrinal, monastic, political, and visionary authority into a single sacred landscape.

In literary and visual culture, this pairing also encouraged an image of the mountain as more than a site of physical temples. 五臺山 often appears as a sacred environment of apparition, encounter, and multiplicity. The mountain's terraces, monasteries, and sacred routes do not just house the cult; they suggest a cosmos in which wisdom is layered, stratified, and spatially manifest. In that respect, 五臺山 is the clearest case in which sacred geography and doctrinal symbolism achieve mutual reinforcement.

### 3.2 普陀山 and 觀音

If 五臺山 is the elevated landscape of wisdom, 普陀山 is the most affectively accessible of the four mountains. Associated with 觀音菩薩, it became a geography of refuge, compassion, rescue, and availability. Its maritime and island setting is not incidental. Water, peril, mist, crossing, and uncertainty all deepen the symbolic logic of Guanyin devotion. A bodhisattva whose defining trait is responsive mercy finds especially potent localization in a site marked by instability, exposure, and passage. The island becomes a place where one turns toward compassion in conditions of vulnerability.

This is a fundamentally different sacred atmosphere from that of 五臺山. 普陀山 does not primarily project scholastic prestige or high doctrinal austerity. Instead, it signifies nearness to human distress. That is one reason why it became so central to lay devotion. The mountain-island setting makes concrete the idea that Guanyin responds when beings are endangered — by sea, by circumstance, by fear, by the precariousness of embodied life. Geography here amplifies devotional psychology. The approach to 普陀山 dramatizes the act of seeking rescue.

The pairing also reveals how Chinese Buddhism localized transregional Buddhist imagination. Guanyin's broader Mahāyāna role as hearer of the cries of the world was not sufficient by itself to generate a specific Chinese pilgrimage complex. What 普陀山 provided was a fixed sacred stage on which Guanyin's compassionate efficacy could be repeatedly enacted, narrated, and institutionalized. Through monasteries, caves, shrines, and maritime legends, the island landscape made compassion visible and locatable. One could not only recite Guanyin's name; one could journey to Guanyin's mountain.

The result is that 普陀山 became the most immediately "pastoral" of the Four Sacred Mountains. Whereas 五臺山 tends upward toward wisdom, 普陀山 tends outward toward suffering beings. It is a site where Chinese Buddhism encoded one of its most important claims: that enlightenment is not merely contemplative transcendence, but merciful responsiveness to the world's instability. In sacred-geographic terms, 普陀山 is compassion made inhabitable.

### 3.3 峨眉山 and 普賢

峨眉山, associated with 普賢菩薩, occupies a somewhat different place in the fourfold sacred system. It is less dominated by the pastoral immediacy of 普陀山 or the visionary height of 五臺山. Instead, 峨眉山 is best read as the mountain of embodied cultivation, ascent, and performative vow. This makes it especially appropriate to 普賢, whose Chinese religious profile emphasizes practice, repentance, dedication of merit, and the enactment of the bodhisattva path.

The symbolic fit here is subtle but powerful. 普賢 is the figure who transforms aspiration into disciplined conduct. A mountain associated with him therefore naturally signifies effort, repetition, and cultivated movement. Pilgrimage to 峨眉山 is not only symbolic affiliation with a revered bodhisattva; it also dramatizes the logic of practice itself. The ascent becomes a form of religious pedagogy. One climbs, persists, performs, and endures. The landscape mirrors the very structure of vow-based cultivation.

This pairing is important because it prevents the Four Sacred Mountains from being reduced to emotional rescue or visionary revelation alone. Chinese Buddhism required a sacred geography of practice, not merely of mercy and insight. 峨眉山 fills that role. It anchors the bodily and liturgical dimensions of Mahāyāna life. Where 普賢 is the bodhisattva who insists that the path must be enacted, 峨眉山 becomes the landscape in which that enactment is imagined most fully.

The cultural resonance of 峨眉山 also extends beyond narrowly doctrinal framing. It entered Chinese imagination as a site where scenic grandeur and religious effort coincide. This matters because Chinese sacred mountains were never purely abstract cult sites. Their power was partly aesthetic, partly liturgical, partly narrative. In the case of 峨眉山, aesthetic sublimity reinforces the sense of ascent, arduousness, and transformative striving. That aesthetic dimension does not distract from the cultic one; it deepens the experience of pilgrimage as an enacted approach toward higher discipline.

### 3.4 九華山 and 地藏

九華山, sacred to 地藏菩薩, is the most existentially grave of the Four Sacred Mountains. If 普陀山 signifies compassionate rescue for the living and 五臺山 encodes wisdom's altitude, 九華山 is shaped above all by themes of death, mourning, filial obligation, karmic burden, and postmortem salvation. It is therefore the mountain where Chinese Buddhist sacred geography most clearly intersects with the moral and emotional structures of family memory and funerary religion.

This gives 九華山 a distinctive tone within the fourfold system. It is not simply a place to seek doctrinal elevation or ritual merit. It is a place where the living confront their obligations toward the dead and where Buddhist soteriology enters the terrain of grief, anxiety, and afterlife imagination. Through 地藏, the mountain becomes a site of hope directed toward those whose suffering lies beyond ordinary social reach. The sacred landscape mediates between visible life and invisible karmic consequence.

The association with 地藏 is especially significant in the Chinese context because it aligns Mahāyāna bodhisattva devotion with enduring concerns around ancestor care, memorial rites, and the continuity of moral responsibility across generations. This does not mean 九華山 is reducible to "popular" afterlife religion. Rather, it shows how Chinese Buddhism extended its doctrinal commitments into one of the most socially central domains of religious practice.

In symbolic terms, 九華山 completes the Four Sacred Mountains by extending the spatial map of Mahāyāna into the darkest and most liminal sphere. Without 九華山, the fourfold system would lack a sacred geography adequate to the religious problems of mourning, karmic fear, and postmortem care. It is the mountain that guarantees that Chinese Buddhist sacred space encompasses not only wisdom, mercy, and cultivation, but also the redemptive labor of accompanying beings through death and beyond.

### 3.5 The Four Mountains as a System: Pilgrimage, Differentiation, and Permanence

**Table 5. Distinctive spatial logic of the Four Sacred Mountains**

| Mountain | Spatial character | Religious mood | What the landscape does for the cult |
|---|---|---|---|
| 五臺山 | Elevated, terraced, austere | Visionary, scholastic, revelatory | Converts wisdom into a geography of clarity and prestige |
| 普陀山 | Island, maritime, liminal | Merciful, protective, accessible | Converts compassion into refuge and rescue |
| 峨眉山 | Ascending, arduous, scenic | Disciplined, vow-centered, performative | Converts practice into embodied pilgrimage |
| 九華山 | Interior, solemn, memorial | Grave, filial, redemptive | Converts vow into afterlife care and karmic remediation |

Considered together, the Four Sacred Mountains form more than a set of parallel pilgrimage sites. They amount to a spatial mandala of Chinese Mahāyāna values. Each mountain specializes in a distinct religious register; each attracts a different affective orientation; each stabilizes one bodhisattva's cult by embedding it in landscape. The result is a distributed sacred system in which no single site has to bear the full burden of Chinese Buddhist devotion.

This differentiation had important institutional consequences. A bodhisattva cult attached to a mountain gains durability because the mountain itself organizes repetition. Pilgrimages recur. Temples accumulate. Local economies develop. Literary and visual traditions form around the site. Imperial patronage, regional networks, and monastic lineages all find stable points of attachment. Sacred geography, in this sense, is a technology of permanence.

The fourfold system also shows how Chinese Buddhism balanced specialization and integration. A devotee could approach one mountain for one kind of religious need, yet still recognize all four as participating in a single sacred order. The system could support different sectarian emphases, local practices, and devotional moods while still sustaining an overarching symbolic coherence.

At the same time, one should resist overly schematic readings. The mountains were never only symbols. They were contested, layered, historically produced, and entangled with local cults, imperial politics, and non-Buddhist sacred geographies. The mature fourfold system gives an appearance of elegant equilibrium, but that equilibrium was achieved historically and unevenly. The next section turns from the symbolic logic of the Four Sacred Mountains to the question of how these mountain–bodhisattva pairings emerged.

## 4. Historical Formation: How Mountain–Bodhisattva Pairings Emerged

The mature system of the Four Sacred Mountains — 五臺山–文殊, 普陀山–觀音, 峨眉山–普賢, and 九華山–地藏 — is one of the most stable features of later Chinese Buddhist culture. But stability in retrospect should not be mistaken for symmetry in origin. These pairings did not emerge through a single coordinated founding moment, nor were they all established by the same mechanism. Some developed through Buddhist overlay onto earlier sacred landscapes; some through the localization of transregional Buddhist myth; some through gradual temple accumulation and retrospective narrative consolidation; and some seem to have acquired their fully explicit bodhisattva-place identity only relatively late. The historical task, then, is to replace a tidy canonical diagram with a process model: layering, localization, institutionalization, and late stabilization.

This section argues that the mountain–bodhisattva system was constructed through three broad stages. First, mountains in China were already sacred before the mature Buddhist fourfold system emerged; they carried local numinous significance, imperial prestige, and, in some cases, Daoist sanctity. Second, Buddhism inserted itself into these landscapes through monasteries, visions, pilgrimage, and textual-geographic interpretation. Third, over time, particular sites became so strongly associated with particular bodhisattvas that later memory compressed long historical development into seemingly primordial religious identity.

The asymmetry is especially important. 五臺山–文殊 has the clearest early doctrinal and cultic consolidation. 普陀山–觀音 is a strong case of Buddhist sacred geography produced by localizing a transregional sacred abode into a Chinese island environment. 峨眉山 appears to have been a major Buddhist site extremely early, but the exact early path by which it became specifically 普賢's mountain is less cleanly documented. 九華山–地藏 is canonical in later tradition, yet scholarship suggests that the full construction of Jiuhua as the earthly abode of Dizang was especially sharpened in the 明清 period, despite earlier Buddhist and legendary roots.

**Table 6. Modes of formation of the four mountain–bodhisattva pairings**

| Pairing | Earliest secure pattern | Main formation mechanism | Degree of early clarity |
|---|---|---|---|
| 五臺山–文殊 | Strong by Northern Wei; intensified in Tang | Buddhist overlay onto earlier sacred/Daoist mountain plus doctrinal-geographic identification | High |
| 普陀山–觀音 | Founded in 916; pilgrimage center by Song | Localization of Avalokiteśvara's sacred abode into Chinese island geography | High for cult formation, later than 五臺山 |
| 峨眉山–普賢 | Buddhist importance from 1st century CE; later stable 普賢 association | Very early Buddhist implantation, later stronger bodhisattva-specific branding | Moderate on precise earliest 普賢 linkage |
| 九華山–地藏 | Mature sacred identity fully standard later; earlier cult roots present | Buddhist site formation plus saint/manifestation traditions, with later consolidation | Moderate; later hardening especially important |

### 4.1 Earlier Mountain Cults and Pre-Buddhist Sacrality

Buddhist localization in China did not begin on an empty map. Long before the mature Four Sacred Mountains system, mountains in China already carried strong religious, political, and cosmological significance. In the broad Chinese religious field, mountains were loci of numinous power, sacrifice, state ritual, and communication between human and transcendent orders. The most famous examples are the Five Sacred Peaks, which were integrated into directional and imperial cosmology; but the larger point is that mountain sacrality predated Buddhist occupation of the sites later central to Chinese Buddhism.

The clearest specific example in the present context is 五臺山. Britannica notes that prior to its Buddhist association, the mountain "appears to have been designated a holy mountain of Daoism during the later Han dynasty." This is a load-bearing fact because it shows the mechanics of religious layering directly: a mountain may first be sacred in one framework and later be re-inscribed within another. Buddhist sacralization here was not annihilation of prior holiness but redirection of it. The mountain's prior prestige made it an attractive site for later Buddhist appropriation, while Buddhist identification with 文殊 eventually redefined the mountain's dominant meaning.

This layering model should be generalized cautiously but firmly. The earlier sacred status of mountains helps explain why Buddhist localization was so effective. Buddhism entered Chinese space not as a tradition indifferent to geography, but as one capable of recognizing and reworking landscapes already charged with cultural meaning. Mountains offered altitude, remoteness, scenic sublimity, and inherited numinosity; Buddhism supplied monastic institutions, scriptural hermeneutics, visions, and bodhisattva-centered cults. The later sacred mountain system is the result of that interaction.

### 4.2 Buddhist Implantation and Localization

If preexisting mountain sacrality supplied the substrate, Buddhist implantation supplied the institutional and narrative machinery. This process took several forms: temple founding, monastic settlement, pilgrimage circulation, saintly biographies, miracle tales, textual interpretation, and retrospective claims that a bodhisattva had manifested at or inhabited a specific site. What matters is that mountains became Buddhist not merely because monks visited them, but because enduring networks of ritual, architecture, patronage, and story accumulated there.

峨眉山 is the strongest case for very early Buddhist implantation. UNESCO states that the first Buddhist temple in China was built on the summit of Mount Emei in the 1st century CE, and that the site developed over time into one of Buddhism's holiest places, eventually containing more than thirty temples. This evidence is important because it distinguishes early Buddhist occupation from later sectarian or bodhisattva-specific codification. Emei was unmistakably Buddhist very early, but that fact alone does not fully settle when or how the site became specifically 普賢's mountain in the mature sense.

普陀山 shows a different mode of localization. Britannica records that the site was believed to have been founded in 916, that its early cult was connected with Avalokiteśvara / 觀音, and that it was already a place of pilgrimage by the Song. This is not simply a case of taking over an already famous inland sacred peak. It is a case of creating a powerful Chinese sacred center by identifying an island environment with the broader Buddhist sacred world of Avalokiteśvara. In other words, Putuo's power comes not mainly from reassigning an older canonical Chinese mountain, but from localizing a transregional bodhisattva geography into the maritime east.

五臺山, by contrast, shows the combination of overlay and doctrinal-geographic identification. Britannica states that the mountain came into Buddhist prominence in the 5th century under the Northern Wei, when, as 清涼山, it became identified as the dwelling place of 文殊; the cult intensified in the Tang, and the mountain became closely associated with 華嚴 patriarchs. This is the clearest case in which Buddhist institutions, doctrinal prestige, and an already sacred landscape converged rapidly into a durable mountain–bodhisattva pairing.

### 4.3 Uneven Consolidation across Sites

The four pairings did not reach mature canonical status at the same speed. 五臺山–文殊 is the most clear-cut and earliest securely articulated example among the four. The site had pre-Buddhist or Daoist sacred credentials, but its Buddhist identity became highly legible by the Northern Wei and expanded strongly in the Tang. That combination of early textual-geographic identification and later cultic growth makes Wutai the strongest anchor for the entire fourfold system.

普陀山–觀音 is later, but once formed, it is exceptionally successful. The island's foundation date in 916, early connection to Guanyin, and Song-era pilgrimage status show that Putuo became canonical through rapid medieval development rather than ancient inheritance. This matters because it demonstrates that canonical sacred geography in Chinese Buddhism could be made, not merely inherited.

峨眉山–普賢 is more complicated. Emei's Buddhist importance is indisputable and extremely early, but short-form reference sources are less precise about the earliest stage of the specific 普賢 identification than they are for Wutai and Putuo. Britannica states that Samantabhadra is the patron deity of Emei, and UNESCO documents the mountain's long Buddhist history and the later importance of temples such as 萬年寺, which contains a major 普賢 bronze image. Still, the historical sequence appears to be: early Buddhist sacred mountain first, stronger and more explicit 普賢 codification later.

九華山–地藏 is perhaps the clearest example of late hardening of sacred identity. Britannica confirms the mature fact that Jiuhua in Anhui is sacred to 地藏 and a favorite pilgrimage site, while scholarship summarized by Brill argues that the full construction of Jiuhua as the divine abode of Dizang "primarily occurred in the Ming–Qing period." This does not mean the cult was invented only then; it means rather that earlier devotion, hagiography, and site development were consolidated into a more definitive sacred geography in late imperial China.

**Table 7. Historical rhythm of formation**

| Site | Earlier sacred/Buddhist substrate | Key consolidation phase | Historiographic takeaway |
|---|---|---|---|
| 五臺山 | Earlier Daoist-sacred status; later Buddhist overlay | Northern Wei foundation of 文殊 identity; Tang intensification | Strongest example of early Buddhist re-inscription of an older sacred mountain |
| 普陀山 | Island setting within regional maritime networks | Foundation in 916; pilgrimage prominence by Song | Strong case of successful medieval localization rather than primordial sanctity |
| 峨眉山 | Very early Buddhist implantation from 1st c. CE | Later stable patronal identity of 普賢 | Early Buddhist site, later clearer bodhisattva specialization |
| 九華山 | Earlier Buddhist/devotional roots | Especially strong place–deity consolidation in 明清 | Canonical identity may be late even when cult roots are older |

### 4.4 Overlay, Not Replacement

A recurring mistake in discussions of Chinese sacred mountains is to imagine a simple linear sequence: non-Buddhist mountain first, Buddhist mountain second, older layer erased. The evidence suggests something more complex. Buddhist sacred geography in China usually worked by overlay, not pure replacement. Earlier numinous status, local cultic memory, and even Daoist sanctity could remain legible beneath Buddhist re-inscription, even when Buddhist dominance later became overwhelming. This is explicit at 五臺山, but the broader pattern is likely more general: Buddhist sacralization often succeeded because it occupied places already endowed with prestige, mystery, and ritual gravity.

This also explains why later tradition tends to naturalize what was historically produced. Once temples, pilgrimage routes, legends, and images have accumulated for centuries, the association of mountain and bodhisattva comes to appear self-evident. The mountain seems always already to belong to the bodhisattva. Yet historically that belonging had to be assembled through repeated acts of naming, building, patronage, narration, and travel. The historian's task is therefore demystifying in the neutral sense: not denying sacred significance, but showing the processes that made that significance durable.

The fourfold system is thus best understood as a retrospective equilibrium. It is a highly successful religious arrangement, but one assembled from different temporal rhythms and different mechanisms of sacralization. Some sites became Buddhist early, some became bodhisattva-specific early, some localized transregional mythic space, and some achieved their final cultic profile only after long gestation. Once seen this way, the Four Sacred Mountains cease to be a static chart and become a historical process of Chinese Buddhist world-making.

With the historical formation of the mountain–bodhisattva pairings clarified, the next section turns to the textual foundations of these cults. The key question there is different: not how the sites were sacralized, but how canonical and para-canonical texts established the doctrinal and salvific profiles of 觀音, 文殊, 普賢, and 地藏, profiles that Chinese Buddhism later localized into sacred geography.

## 5. Textual Foundations of the Major Bodhisattva Cults

If the previous section examined how mountain–bodhisattva pairings were historically formed, this section asks a different question: what do the texts actually establish? That distinction is critical. In Chinese Buddhism, canonical and para-canonical scriptures usually define a bodhisattva's doctrinal role, salvific function, and symbolic profile; they do not, for the most part, directly produce the later Chinese mountain associations in their mature form. Those mountain associations emerged through localization, pilgrimage, temple accumulation, and retrospective narrative consolidation. The texts, however, remain foundational because they supplied the basic religious grammar that later Chinese devotion amplified.

The textual basis of the major Chinese bodhisattva cults is uneven. 觀音 has the clearest single devotional scriptural anchor in the Lotus Sutra, especially the 〈觀世音菩薩普門品〉. 普賢 is strongly grounded in the Avataṃsaka / 華嚴 world, especially the 行願 material that made him the paradigmatic bodhisattva of vows, repentance, and dedication of merit. 地藏 is anchored most decisively by the 《地藏菩薩本願經》, whose East Asian reception shaped his profile as savior of beings in hell and central figure in after-death liturgies. 文殊 is the least reducible to one single popular chapter; his authority is distributed across broader Mahāyāna textual worlds, especially 般若 and 華嚴, where he appears as the bodhisattva of wisdom, eloquence, and awakened insight.

**Table 8. Textual foundations of the major bodhisattva cults**

| Bodhisattva | Main textual basis in Chinese Buddhism | What the text primarily establishes | Later Chinese cultic expansion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 觀音菩薩 / 觀世音菩薩 | 《妙法蓮華經》〈觀世音菩薩普門品〉 | Responsive compassion, rescue from danger, protean manifestation | Mass lay devotion, miracle cult, 普陀山, feminized and multiple iconographic forms |
| 文殊菩薩 / 文殊師利 | Distributed across 般若 and 《華嚴經》 textual worlds, plus Mañjuśrī literature | Wisdom, doctrinal eloquence, bodhicitta, insight | Scholastic prestige, visionary cult, 五臺山 association |
| 普賢菩薩 | 《華嚴經》, especially 〈普賢行願品〉 | Vows, repentance, dedication of merit, enacted practice | Liturgical centrality, moral cultivation, 峨眉山 cult |
| 地藏菩薩 | 《地藏菩薩本願經》 and related 地藏 texts | Hell rescue, filiality, karmic remediation, postmortem care | Memorial rites, after-death liturgies, 九華山 cult |

### 5.1 觀音 and the Lotus Sutra World

Among the four, 觀音菩薩 has the clearest and most influential single textual anchor in East Asian Buddhism: the Lotus Sutra chapter commonly known as the Universal Gate chapter, 〈觀世音菩薩普門品〉. This chapter presents Avalokiteśvara as the bodhisattva who responds when beings call his name and rescues them from shipwreck, fire, attack, imprisonment, demons, and many other dangers. This is the decisive textual reason Guanyin became, in Chinese Buddhism, the paradigmatic bodhisattva of responsive salvation rather than merely abstract compassion.

Several implications follow from this. First, Guanyin's textual basis is unusually portable. A single chapter can be recited, excerpted, ritualized, illustrated, preached, and applied to everyday anxieties. Second, the chapter's emphasis on 救苦救難-type functions made it exceptionally compatible with lay religiosity. Third, its recurring insistence that Avalokiteśvara can manifest in whatever form is needed supplied a scriptural rationale for later Chinese iconographic plurality. This does not mean that all later forms of Guanyin are directly prescribed in the sutra; rather, the sutra creates a theological permission structure for flexibility of form and accessibility of intervention.

Britannica notes that worship of Avalokiteśvara as Guanyin was introduced into China as early as the 1st century CE and had entered all Buddhist temples by the 6th century, while the Lotus material gave that worship a highly legible scriptural center. The Universal Gate chapter did not by itself create Guanyin's entire Chinese career, but it provided the most powerful canonical engine for his transformation into the bodhisattva of immediate, universal, and practical compassion. The Lotus Sutra establishes Guanyin's salvific profile, but it does not directly create 普陀山 as the Chinese sacred mountain of Guanyin. Text precedes localization, but the two are not identical.

### 5.2 文殊 across 般若 and 《華嚴經》

The textual basis of 文殊菩薩 is both strong and diffuse. Unlike 觀音, 文殊 is not defined in Chinese Buddhism by one overwhelmingly dominant popular chapter. Instead, he appears across broader Mahāyāna textual worlds, especially those of 般若 and 《華嚴經》. This distributed textual basis is not a weakness. It is precisely what allowed 文殊 to become the personification of a broad Mahāyāna concern with prajñā, doctrinal articulation, discernment, and the awakening of bodhicitta.

In Chinese Buddhism, 文殊 is therefore less the center of a single recited devotional unit and more the concentrated symbol of a wider scriptural universe in which wisdom is the indispensable corrective to delusion. He speaks, questions, clarifies, and catalyzes insight. That is why his later reception is so closely tied to scholastic prestige and sacred topographies of revelation.

The distributed nature of 文殊's textual profile also helps explain his later sectarian reception. In 華嚴, he is structurally central because that tradition requires a figure who personifies insight into the interpenetrating cosmos. In 禪, he is equally useful as the symbolic embodiment of cutting wisdom, even without a single devotional chapter comparable to Guanyin's. In both cases, the textual foundation is real but not concentrated into one popularly dominant unit. That difference in textual architecture corresponds to a difference in religious style: 文殊's authority tends to be more interpretive and doctrinally elevated, less immediately pastoral.

### 5.3 普賢 and the 《華嚴經》 / 行願 Tradition

If 文殊 is the wisdom pole of the Huayan universe, 普賢菩薩 is its pole of practice and vow. The most decisive textual basis here is the Avataṃsaka / 華嚴經 tradition, especially the 〈普賢行願品〉, often circulated independently in East Asia. This text lays out the famous Ten Great Vows of Samantabhadra, including paying homage to all buddhas, praising the tathāgatas, making extensive offerings, repenting karmic obstacles, rejoicing in others' merit, requesting the turning of the Dharma wheel, asking the buddhas to remain in the world, constantly studying with the buddhas, constantly according with sentient beings, and universally dedicating all virtue.

This textual profile is the key to 普賢's later Chinese identity. He is not merely "good conduct" in a generic sense. He is the bodhisattva who converts ideal aspiration into structured religious action. The Ten Great Vows are not ornamental; they provide a liturgical and ethical template for the actual performance of Mahāyāna life. That is why 普賢 becomes so important in repentance, merit transfer, vow-making, and disciplined cultivation. His scriptural base is inherently performative.

Because the 〈普賢行願品〉 became important not only in Huayan but also in broader East Asian liturgical circulation, 普賢's textual basis helped him cross sectarian boundaries more easily than a narrowly scholastic figure might. His cult is specific, but not sect-sealed. The very form of the text — vows that can be adopted, recited, and ritualized — encouraged transmission into temple life beyond one doctrinal school.

### 5.4 地藏 and the Vow-and-Afterlife Scriptures

For 地藏菩薩, the primary textual anchor in East Asian Buddhism is the 《地藏菩薩本願經》. This sutra presents precisely the themes that later define Dizang's East Asian cult: vast vows, karmic consequence, the relief of beings in hell, and the moral power of devotion and transfer on behalf of the dead. Britannica similarly emphasizes that Kṣitigarbha, who had limited importance in India and Tibet, became in East Asia the lord of hell and central figure in after-death liturgies. This East Asian reorientation is crucial. 地藏's Chinese prominence is profoundly scriptural, but the relevant scriptural world is one whose most powerful social effects are funerary, memorial, and karmic.

The doctrinal significance of the Original Vows text lies in its combination of cosmic severity and pastoral utility. It takes karmic consequence seriously, including postmortem suffering, yet it also insists that the compassion and vows of 地藏 remain active in those very realms. This produces a textual basis for practices surrounding memorial transfer, filial devotion, rituals for the deceased, and religious concern for invisible suffering. In Chinese Buddhism, that made 地藏 not merely another bodhisattva among many, but one of the most socially indispensable figures in the management of grief, death, and moral continuity across generations.

### 5.5 Text and Localization

**Table 9. What the texts do, and what later Chinese Buddhism adds**

| Bodhisattva | What the key text establishes | What later Chinese Buddhism adds |
|---|---|---|
| 觀音 | Compassion that hears cries, rescues from concrete dangers, manifests in many forms | Mass recitation, miracle cult, feminized/iconographic plurality, 普陀山 pilgrimage |
| 文殊 | Wisdom, discernment, eloquence, awakening of bodhicitta | Scholastic prestige, visionary mountain cult, 五臺山 centrality |
| 普賢 | Vows, repentance, dedication of merit, enacted practice | Liturgical centrality, moral cultivation, 峨眉山 as practice landscape |

The preceding subsections make one broader point clear: texts establish profiles; Chinese Buddhism localizes those profiles. This principle is analytically necessary because it prevents two common errors. The first is to treat later mountain associations as if they were simply present from the start in the sutras. The second is to treat the cults as if they arose independently of textual foundations. Neither is correct. The texts define what sort of bodhisattva one is dealing with — rescuer, wisdom figure, vow-practitioner, postmortem savior — while localization through pilgrimage, temples, images, and miracle tales determines where and how that figure will be religiously encountered in China.

Any serious study of Chinese Buddhist bodhisattvas must resist choosing between textual study and lived religion. The cultic history is unintelligible without the scriptures, but the scriptures alone do not explain why some bodhisattvas became attached to particular mountains, social functions, or sectarian patterns. Chinese Buddhism is best understood at the intersection of canon, ritual, geography, and reception.

## 6. Sectarian Reception in Chinese Buddhism

The previous section argued that the major bodhisattva cults in Chinese Buddhism rest on uneven but identifiable textual foundations. The next question is how those figures were received, amplified, and repositioned within major Chinese Buddhist traditions. This is not a trivial addendum. In Chinese Buddhism, bodhisattvas were not simply inherited from scripture and then left unchanged. They were selectively emphasized according to the doctrinal center of gravity, ritual style, and practical needs of different traditions. A sectarian comparison therefore reveals not only which figures were honored, but which became structurally central, which remained secondary, and which were repurposed for lived religious practice.

A crucial methodological warning is required at the outset. Chinese Buddhist "sects" should not be imagined as mutually exclusive confessional blocs. As Britannica notes, Mahāyāna in China did not stand apart from monastic Buddhism as a separate institutional species; Chinese monks were ordained within shared vinaya systems while taking Mahāyāna vows and participating in a broad religious field. In practice, a temple could combine 禪 liturgy, 淨土 recitation, 觀音 devotion, and memorial rites for the dead without perceiving any contradiction. The comparison in this section therefore concerns patterns of emphasis, not strict monopolies.

**Table 10. Sectarian fit of the four great bodhisattvas**

| Bodhisattva | 天台 | 華嚴 | 禪 | 淨土 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 觀音菩薩 | Very strong; naturally amplified by the Lotus Sutra world | Important but not structurally central in the way 文殊/普賢 are | Strong in lived devotion and visual culture, despite Chan's anti-form rhetoric | Very strong as attendant of 阿彌陀佛 and major Pure Land devotional figure |
| 文殊菩薩 | Important as wisdom within Lotus-centered synthesis | Foundational; one of the key Huayan bodhisattvas | Highly compatible symbol of cutting wisdom | Secondary in doctrinal Pure Land structure |
| 普賢菩薩 | Integrated through vow and practice | Foundational; paired with 文殊 in Huayan | Absorbed as enacted discipline more than as an independent cultic center | Secondary but harmonizable through vow and merit-transfer language |
| 地藏菩薩 | Accepted and ritually useful, but not sect-defining | Peripheral to Huayan's main cosmic structure | Important at the monastery–popular religion interface | Often important in funerary and memorial practice, but not core to Amitābha-centered doctrine |

### 6.1 天台

Among the major Chinese traditions, 天台 provides the most obvious sectarian environment for the elevation of 觀音菩薩. Britannica's entry on Tiantai is explicit that the 《法華經》 was considered the supreme doctrine. That fact alone explains much of the sectarian pattern. If the Lotus Sutra is the organizing center, then the bodhisattva whose cult is most powerfully anchored in the Lotus — through the 〈觀世音菩薩普門品〉 — will inevitably enjoy privileged resonance. In Tiantai, 觀音 is therefore not simply one devotional option among many; he is the bodhisattva most organically aligned with the school's principal scriptural world.

This does not mean Tiantai reduces itself to Guanyin devotion. On the contrary, Tiantai is famous for its synthetic and classificatory breadth. But precisely because the school places all Buddhist teaching into a comprehensive doctrinal hierarchy, it can integrate multiple bodhisattvas while still granting special force to those most naturally supported by the Lotus. Thus 文殊 and 普賢 are fully intelligible within Tiantai, yet they do not occupy the same emblematic place there as they do in 華嚴. 地藏 is likewise available to Tiantai ritual and belief, especially where repentance, merit transfer, and afterlife concern enter practice, but he is not central to Tiantai's doctrinal self-definition.

Tiantai demonstrates that sectarian reception in Chinese Buddhism often follows scriptural affinity rather than exclusive sectarian ownership. Guanyin is not "owned" by Tiantai, but Tiantai's scriptural center gives Guanyin unusual prominence.

### 6.2 華嚴

If Tiantai most naturally amplifies 觀音, 華嚴 most clearly centers 文殊 and 普賢. This is a structural result of the Avataṃsaka / 華嚴經 cosmos itself. Britannica's entry on Samantabhadra notes his intimate association with 毘盧遮那佛 and his triadic pairing with 文殊, while Britannica's entry on Manjusri emphasizes his role as the bodhisattva of wisdom. In Huayan thought, this pairing is not accidental. The tradition requires both the insight that sees the interpenetrating cosmos correctly and the vow-practice that enacts that vision. 文殊 and 普賢 therefore stand as the two great poles of the Huayan religious imagination: wisdom and cultivated action.

This is why Huayan provides the strongest sectarian "home" for 文殊 and 普賢 within Chinese Buddhism. Together they prevent Huayan from collapsing into either sterile metaphysics or empty ritualism. Wisdom without practice would remain contemplative abstraction; practice without wisdom would lack cosmic comprehension.

In this context, 觀音 and 地藏 remain entirely legitimate but comparatively secondary. Guanyin can be integrated into the broader Mahāyāna universe that Huayan encompasses, yet she is not architectonic for Huayan self-understanding in the way 文殊 and 普賢 are. 地藏 is even less central to Huayan doctrinal identity, because his strongest religious domains — afterlife anxiety, funerary devotion, and karmic redemptive care — lie somewhat outside Huayan's primary cosmological emphasis.

### 6.3 禪

The case of 禪 is more complicated because Chan rhetoric often presents itself as suspicious of textual dependence, iconographic attachment, and elaborate devotional mediation. Yet this rhetoric has repeatedly been overstated by modern interpreters. In actual Chinese Buddhist history, Chan did not eliminate bodhisattvas from its world. Rather, it re-signified them. Britannica's article on Chan painting is particularly revealing: it notes that Chan art, despite its suggestive and anti-literal tendencies, sometimes includes figures such as the 白衣觀音.

Within Chan, 觀音 is especially adaptable because the bodhisattva's compassion can be assimilated to qualities of stillness, immediacy, and unmediated presence. Guanyin in Chan visual culture does not necessarily operate through the full petitionary and miracle-centered register familiar from broader lay devotion; instead, the figure may become an icon of contemplative compassion, inward quiet, or attentive non-discursiveness.

文殊 is likewise highly compatible with Chan, albeit for different reasons. As the bodhisattva of cutting wisdom, 文殊 readily maps onto Chan's emphasis on insight that severs conceptual proliferation. By contrast, 普賢 is less iconically dominant in Chan but fits naturally with the monastic ethos of sustained practice. 地藏 enters Chan somewhat differently — seldom central to Chan self-definition, yet important wherever Chan institutions meet the broader religious life of death, memorial rites, and karmic care.

### 6.4 淨土

Among the four traditions considered here, 淨土 has the most sharply defined center: 阿彌陀佛 and rebirth in 極樂世界. This determines the pattern of bodhisattva reception. In Pure Land, bodhisattvas matter most where they belong to the salvific field of Amitābha. That gives special prominence to 觀音, who, as Britannica notes, forms with 阿彌陀佛 and 大勢至 a ruling triad for Pure Land Buddhists. In other words, Guanyin is not merely compatible with Pure Land; he is one of its principal supporting presences.

This also means that the sectarian reception of 觀音 in Pure Land differs from his reception in Tiantai. In Tiantai, Guanyin is privileged because of the Lotus world; in Pure Land, Guanyin is privileged because of Amitābha's salvific entourage and the practical economy of rebirth devotion. By contrast, 文殊 and 普賢 are respected but secondary in doctrinal Pure Land structure. 地藏 occupies a particularly interesting border position. He is not central to Pure Land doctrine proper, yet in actual Chinese religious life Pure Land aspiration frequently intersects with funerary liturgy, care for the dying, and transfer of merit to the dead.

### 6.5 Sectarian Comparison as a Map of Emphasis

**Table 11. Dominant sectarian affinities**

| Tradition | Primary scriptural/ritual center | Bodhisattva(s) with strongest affinity | Nature of the affinity |
|---|---|---|---|
| 天台 | Lotus Sutra synthesis | 觀音 | Strong scriptural affinity through the Lotus world |
| 華嚴 | Avataṃsaka / 華嚴經 cosmos | 文殊, 普賢 | Structural pairing of wisdom and vow-practice |
| 禪 | Direct insight, monastic practice, visual-symbolic culture | 觀音, 文殊 | Symbolic and practical absorption rather than exclusive doctrinal centrality |
| 淨土 | 阿彌陀佛 devotion and rebirth in 極樂世界 | 觀音 (and especially 大勢至 outside the fourfold core) | Integration into Amitābha's salvific triad |

The comparison across 天台, 華嚴, 禪, and 淨土 reveals a broader pattern. Chinese Buddhist sectarian life does not sort bodhisattvas into exclusive silos. Instead, it produces clusters of elective affinity. Across all of these, 地藏 often remains less sect-defining but persistently important wherever death, memorial care, and karmic anxiety are in view.

This pattern also clarifies why the same bodhisattva can feel markedly different depending on sectarian context. 觀音 in Tiantai is Lotus-grounded; in Chan, contemplatively stylized; in Pure Land, integrated into Amitābha's triad. 文殊 in Huayan is cosmologically central; in Chan, a symbol of incisive insight. 普賢 in Huayan is architectonic; elsewhere, often more quietly liturgical. Sectarian reception, then, is less about doctrinal ownership than about strategic intensification.

## 7. Comparative Extensions: 彌勒、大勢至、藥師

The preceding sections focused on the four bodhisattvas most central to the standard Chinese Buddhist symbolic system: 觀音、文殊、普賢、地藏. Yet that fourfold arrangement does not exhaust the Chinese Mahāyāna religious field. A fuller account requires at least three comparative extensions: 彌勒菩薩, 大勢至菩薩, and 藥師佛. These three are analytically useful because each tests the limits of the fourfold scheme in a different way. 彌勒 represents broad trans-sectarian portability and future-oriented Buddhology; 大勢至 represents unusually strong integration into one specific salvific system, namely 淨土; and 藥師 represents ritual-functional importance that cuts across traditions without normally becoming the defining emblem of any one school.

**Table 12. Comparative profile of 彌勒、大勢至、藥師**

| Figure | Religious type | Core profile | Strongest sectarian fit | Comparative significance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 彌勒菩薩 | Future Buddha, currently bodhisattva | Future advent, 兜率天, cross-sectarian cult portability | Broadly distributed rather than narrowly sectarian | Tests the limits of the fourfold system by showing pan-Buddhist durability |
| 大勢至菩薩 | Bodhisattva | Attendant of 阿彌陀佛, strongest in Pure Land devotion | 淨土 | Shows a figure whose reception is unusually concentrated in one salvific economy |
| 藥師佛 | Buddha, not bodhisattva | Healing, ritual efficacy, this-worldly protection and cure | Broad ritual presence; no single school fully monopolizes him | Shows how ritual-functional importance can exceed sectarian centrality |

### 7.1 彌勒 as a Cross-Sectarian Future Buddha

Among these three figures, 彌勒 is the most broadly portable across Buddhist traditions. Britannica describes Maitreya as the future Buddha, presently a bodhisattva residing in Tuṣita / 兜率天, who will descend to renew the Dharma when the teachings of Śākyamuni have decayed. It also notes that Maitreya was the earliest bodhisattva around whom a major cult developed and that he was accepted by all schools of Buddhism. That breadth matters. It means that 彌勒 enters Chinese Buddhism not as the property of a single sectarian tradition but as a figure already carrying pan-Buddhist authority.

This makes 彌勒 especially important as a comparative control. Unlike 觀音, whose Chinese prominence is strongly tied to the Lotus Sutra and later to 普陀山, or 大勢至, whose strongest reception is tightly linked to the Amitābha triad, 彌勒 is not defined primarily by a single Chinese localization or one dominant sectarian liturgical niche. His cult instead centers on futurity, deferred Buddhahood, eschatological renewal, and aspiration toward rebirth in 兜率天. Britannica explicitly notes that the early cult of Maitreya appears to have prepared the way for the later Pure Land traditions involving Amitābha, which gradually superseded it. That historical displacement is analytically important.

### 7.2 大勢至 as a Pure Land-Centered Bodhisattva

If 彌勒 is the most trans-sectarian of the three, 大勢至菩薩 is the most sectarianly concentrated. Britannica is unusually clear here: Mahāsthāmaprāpta is "most popular among the Pure Land sects," and he is often depicted together with 阿彌陀佛 and 觀音. This is as close as one gets to a straightforward case of a figure whose Chinese significance is overwhelmingly structured by one sectarian devotional system.

This has two major implications. First, 大勢至 is a useful contrast to 觀音. Both belong strongly to the Amitābha field in East Asia, but Guanyin also has a much wider independent life in Chinese religion through the Lotus world, miracle devotion, and sacred geography. Mahāsthāmaprāpta does not expand nearly so widely beyond Pure Land contexts. Second, this concentration clarifies the mechanics of sectarian reception. Not every bodhisattva is simply received more or less intensely across all traditions; some are structurally dependent on one religious ecology for their strongest meaning.

### 7.3 藥師 as a Ritual-Functional Buddha

藥師 differs from the other figures in this section in a basic but important way: he is not a bodhisattva but a Buddha, 藥師琉璃光如來 / Bhaishajyaguru. Britannica describes Bhaishajyaguru as the healing Buddha, widely worshipped in Tibet, China, and Japan, and notes that while some ailments were believed curable by touching his image or calling his name, more serious illnesses called for complex ritual acts prescribed in the cult's principal scripture.

This profile marks 藥師 as a strongly ritual-functional figure. He belongs to a world of healing, protection, bodily vulnerability, and this-worldly benefit, even when those benefits remain framed within a larger Buddhist soteriological horizon. That makes him highly important in institutional and lay practice alike. Yet it also explains why he does not become the defining emblem of 天台, 華嚴, 禪, or 淨土. His cult is powerful, but its power lies in use-value, not in monopolizing doctrinal identity.

### 7.4 Comparative Implications

**Table 13. Three different models of reception**

| Figure | Primary engine of reception | Secondary supports | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|---|
| 彌勒 | Pan-Buddhist futurity and Tuṣita aspiration | Broad doctrinal portability, early devotional cult | Importance can persist without narrow sectarian concentration |
| 大勢至 | Amitābha-centered Pure Land integration | Iconography of the Pure Land triad | Some figures derive force from highly specific sectarian embedding |
| 藥師 | Healing and ritual efficacy | Wide temple and lay usefulness | Ritual-functional value can outweigh sectarian centrality |

Taken together, 彌勒、大勢至、藥師 expand the article's argument in three directions. 彌勒 shows that some figures remain important through pan-Buddhist scope and futurity rather than through strong localization. 大勢至 shows that some figures achieve their strongest life inside a single, clearly bounded salvific system. 藥師 shows that ritual and practical efficacy can produce enduring importance even without sectarian centrality.

This broader comparison also sharpens the significance of the Four Great Bodhisattvas. The fourfold system is powerful not because it includes every important Buddhist figure, but because it solved a specific representational problem: how to map major Mahāyāna values into a coherent Chinese symbolic, geographic, and devotional order.

## 8. Buddhist Sacred Geography and Daoist Mountain Cults

The Four Sacred Mountains of Chinese Buddhism cannot be understood in isolation from the wider Chinese history of sacred mountains. Chinese Buddhist mountain cults emerged within a landscape already structured by older patterns of mountain veneration, imperial ritual, cosmological ordering, and Daoist sanctification. Any serious comparison must therefore move beyond a simple Buddhist internal history and ask how Buddhist sacred geography positioned itself within a preexisting sacred topography.

The central contrast is clear, though not absolute. Buddhist sacred mountains in China are primarily organized around bodhisattva presence, salvific specialization, and pilgrimage to charismatic figures. Daoist and imperial mountain systems are more strongly organized around cosmic order, territorial symbolism, numinous efficacy, and the ritual articulation of sovereignty. Yet these two systems were never hermetically sealed. Chinese pilgrimage historically centered on places considered 靈 — sites where sacred power becomes manifest — and that broader Chinese category of numinous place is exactly what allowed Buddhist and Daoist mountain systems to overlap, compete, and mutually reshape one another.

**Table 14. Buddhist and Daoist mountain systems in comparative perspective**

| Dimension | Buddhist sacred mountains | Daoist / imperial sacred mountains |
|---|---|---|
| Primary organizing principle | Bodhisattva-centered sacred geography | Cosmological-directional and territorial order |
| Main religious focus | Pilgrimage to a salvific figure; compassion, wisdom, vow, practice | Numinous efficacy, cosmic alignment, immortality, state sacrifice |
| Typical sacred map | 四大佛教名山: 峨眉山、五臺山、普陀山、九華山 | 五嶽 and wider Daoist sacred mountain networks |
| Institutional form | Monasteries, pilgrimage circuits, miracle narratives, devotional temples | State ritual sites, Daoist temples, grotto-heavens, mountain sanctuaries |
| Symbolic logic | Which salvific mode is localized here? | Where does this mountain stand in the cosmic order? |

### 8.1 The Five Sacred Peaks and Cosmological Order

The most important background to Buddhist sacred geography is the older Chinese system of the Five Sacred Peaks / 五嶽. Britannica's entry on 泰山 notes that it was one of China's five holy mountains and usually ranked first among them. Britannica's Taoism material further explains that the five holy mountains were correlated with the five directions and the Five Phases / 五行, embedding them in a larger cosmological system rather than treating them simply as isolated holy places.

This directional-cosmological structure matters because it differs fundamentally from the Buddhist fourfold mountain system. The Buddhist question is usually: which bodhisattva manifests here, and what kind of salvation does this site mediate? The older Chinese and Daoist-facing question is more often: where is this mountain located within the ordered cosmos, and what authority or efficacy follows from that position? The Five Peaks model encodes territory and cosmology; the Four Buddhist Mountains encode differentiated salvific functions.

UNESCO's entry on 泰山 states that the mountain was the object of an imperial cult for nearly two thousand years. Mountains in this system were not simply pilgrimage destinations for private devotion; they were part of the ritual management of sovereignty and world order. Daoist sacred geography extended this cosmological pattern further through systems of 洞天 and additional holy mountains and sanctuaries.

### 8.2 Overlap, Competition, and Layering

If one stopped at the contrast above, the picture would still be too neat. Chinese mountains were rarely monopolized by one tradition. The strongest example in the present discussion is 五臺山. Britannica reports that 五臺山 appears to have been a Daoist holy mountain during the Later Han before becoming, by the Northern Wei, a major Buddhist site associated with 文殊. That pattern is not incidental. It shows the mechanics of religious layering directly: a mountain can first be sacred under one logic and later be re-inscribed under another without losing its status as a privileged site of encounter with the sacred. The Buddhist cult succeeds partly because it enters a place already recognized as numinous.

Competition should also be kept in view. Buddhist sacred mountains did not merely coexist passively with Daoist or imperial geographies. They offered an alternative model of sacred organization. A mountain might still be numinous, but the meaning of that numinosity could be redirected from state sacrifice or Daoist cosmology toward bodhisattva devotion and pilgrimage. The success of the Buddhist system lay partly in its ability to make this shift seem natural: wisdom belongs at 五臺山, compassion at 普陀山, practice at 峨眉山, redemptive care for the dead at 九華山.

### 8.3 Localization as Religious Strategy

Seen in this wider frame, Buddhist sacred geography in China was not merely decorative or secondary. It was one of the most effective tools of localization available to Chinese Buddhism. A tradition whose central figures had South Asian origins could become deeply Chinese by attaching those figures to named Chinese mountains, monastery complexes, regional pilgrimage circuits, and literary landscapes.

This is why the comparison with Daoist mountain cults is so useful. Daoist and imperial traditions had already shown that mountains could function as privileged points of contact between human order and larger cosmic power. Buddhism did not simply copy that model, but it clearly benefited from entering a culture where mountains already carried extraordinary symbolic capital. The Buddhist innovation was to organize sacred mountains not around directional cosmology or state sovereignty, but around distinct modes of salvation embodied by bodhisattvas. The mountain ceased to be merely a cosmic pillar and became also a soteriological environment.

### 8.4 Comparative Conclusion

**Table 15. What the Buddhist–Daoist comparison clarifies**

| Analytical question | What the comparison shows |
|---|---|
| Why mountains? | Mountains were already among the strongest sacred forms in Chinese religion, so Buddhism localized itself through an already prestigious spatial medium. |
| Why four Buddhist mountains? | Buddhism created a differentiated salvific map, assigning distinct bodhisattvas and religious functions to specific sites. |
| Was this replacement? | Usually no; the pattern is better described as layering, reinterpretation, and competition within shared sacred landscapes. |
| What is specifically Buddhist here? | The localization of bodhisattva presence and pilgrimage around compassion, wisdom, practice, and vow rather than directional cosmology alone. |

The most useful conclusion is not that Buddhist and Daoist mountain cults were simply opposites. They are better understood as different sacred grammars operating within the same Chinese landscape. That shared dependence on mountains made overlap inevitable. Chinese sacred geography is therefore best read as a history of layering rather than replacement. Buddhism localized itself successfully not because it found an empty land, but because it could inhabit, redirect, and intensify a preexisting culture of sacred mountains.

## 9. Iconography, Literature, and the Visualization of Sacred Mountains

If the preceding sections examined bodhisattvas as doctrinal figures, sacred mountains, and sectarian objects of reception, this section turns to a different but equally decisive problem: how were these worlds made visible and narratable? Chinese Buddhist sacred geography did not endure by text and pilgrimage alone. It also depended on representation. Mountains had to be pictured, described, storied, and ritualized in forms that allowed devotees to recognize them as distinctive sacred environments.

The central argument of this section is that Chinese Buddhist sacred mountains became stable religious worlds through the mutual reinforcement of iconography and literature. Iconography gave devotees recognizable bodily and environmental signs: 文殊 with sword, scripture, and lion; 觀音 in white-robed or water-moon forms linked to island and maritime imagery; 普賢 with elephant and vow-centered dignity; 地藏 in monk form with staff and wish-granting jewel. Literature — especially miracle tales, pilgrimage records, gazetteers, and descriptive writing — then embedded these figures in named landscapes and routinized expectations about what a pilgrim might encounter there.

**Table 16. Visual-literary stabilization of the Four Sacred Mountains**

| Mountain | Associated bodhisattva | Dominant visual markers | Dominant literary atmosphere |
|---|---|---|---|
| 五臺山 | 文殊菩薩 | Lion, sword, scripture, terraced peaks, monastery complexes | Vision, revelation, doctrinal prestige, apparition |
| 普陀山 | 觀音菩薩 | Water-Moon form, white-robed Guanyin, island, cave, dragon, sea | Refuge, rescue, perilous passage, compassionate accessibility |
| 峨眉山 | 普賢菩薩 | Elephant mount, summit temples, ascent imagery, sacred scenic grandeur | Cultivation, vow, pilgrimage-as-practice |
| 九華山 | 地藏菩薩 | Monk's robe, shaved head, staff, wish-granting jewel, solemn temple space | Mourning, filiality, hell rescue, memorial care |

### 9.1 Iconographic Markers of the Bodhisattvas

The first condition of durable cult representation is recognizability. Chinese Buddhist bodhisattvas became visually stable through a cluster of repeated attributes, postures, and companion elements. 文殊 is among the clearest cases. Metropolitan Museum collection entries consistently identify Mañjuśrī through the sword that cuts ignorance, the scripture or lotus bearing a scripture, and the lion as mount. This iconography is not incidental decoration. It condenses 文殊's doctrinal role into immediately legible form: wisdom is militant against delusion, scriptural yet transcendent, and sovereign enough to ride the lion.

地藏 is equally recognizable, but by almost opposite means. Rather than princely ornament or dramatic weaponry, Kṣitigarbha is repeatedly shown as a monk — with shaved head, robe, staff, and wish-granting jewel. The Met's descriptions stress that this monk-form is standard and that the staff and jewel are his customary attributes. 地藏's authority is not displayed as royal splendor but as renunciant endurance and compassionate proximity to suffering.

觀音 presents a more variable case, and that variability is itself important. The Met's entry on 水月觀音 explains that one especially prominent form after the tenth century shows Avalokiteśvara seated in a relaxed pose representing the bodhisattva in his pure land or personal paradise, originally associated with Potalaka and later identified in China with 普陀山 by the Ming. Guanyin's iconography is therefore both stable and expansive: stable in compassion and salvific accessibility, expansive in form, posture, and atmosphere.

For 普賢, the iconic marker is the elephant mount, often white and associated with dignity, vow, and patient force. In Chinese visual programs, especially those inflected by the 華嚴 world, the distinction between lion-riding wisdom and elephant-riding practice becomes one of the clearest ways to stage the complementarity of the two bodhisattvas.

### 9.2 Mountain Landscapes in Visual Culture

The iconography of the bodhisattvas is only half the story. Chinese Buddhist sacred mountains also developed as landscape codes. This is especially clear in representations of 普陀山. The Met's description of Bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara in Water Moon Form explicitly connects the water-moon manifestation to the bodhisattva's island pure land and notes that by the Ming this paradise had been identified with 普陀山. The bodily pose, the watery setting, and the island association mutually reinforce one another.

Related descriptions of Guanyin imagery emphasize a still richer Putuo repertoire: island, cave, sea, clouds, dragons, and approach by supplicants. 普陀山 becomes visible not just as "a place where Guanyin is worshipped," but as a maritime sacred environment of refuge and liminality. In visual terms, the island itself functions as an attribute of the bodhisattva.

五臺山 offers a different landscape code. UNESCO's description highlights the mountain's monastic density and singles out Shuxiang Temple, whose enormous sculptural program includes more than 500 statues distributed across mountain-and-water scenes representing Buddhist narratives. Wutai visual culture is not only about a single iconic 文殊 image. It is about the rendering of an entire sacred world — terraced peaks, monastery complexes, and monumental visual programs that make the mountain itself a theater of revelation.

For 峨眉山 and 九華山, the visual codes are less succinctly captured in brief museum summaries, but the general pattern remains visible. Emei's cultic world is associated with ascent, summit temples, and the embodied movement of pilgrimage, while Jiuhua's environment is shaped more by solemn temple presence, monastic gravity, and the iconography of 地藏 in robe, staff, and jewel. The difference in affect is important: Putuo is maritime and liminal; Wutai is elevated and visionary; Emei is arduous and vow-centered; Jiuhua is interior, memorial, and grave.

### 9.3 Literary Representation: Miracle Tales, Gazetteers, Pilgrimage Writing

Visuality alone does not stabilize a sacred mountain. It must be accompanied by language that teaches readers and pilgrims how to interpret what they see. Chinese Buddhist sacred mountains acquired this interpretive frame through miracle tales, local and monastic records, gazetteers, pilgrimage accounts, and descriptive literature. Pilgrims historically traveled to sites thought to be 靈, where saints or bodhisattvas might appear, where healing or divine aid could be obtained, and where sacred presence was concentrated. Such expectations are inherently literary as well as ritual.

This literary function is especially clear for 五臺山, whose prestige depended not only on institutional development but on being repeatedly described as a place of encounter with 文殊. Apparitional narratives, doctrinal prestige, and mountain descriptions create a textual atmosphere in which the site becomes a natural destination for those seeking wisdom and revelation. The same principle applies to 普陀山, where maritime peril and compassionate rescue are narratively foregrounded, and to 九華山, whose literary aura is shaped by death, filiality, karmic burden, and the salvific reach of 地藏.

Literature also regularizes memory. A pilgrim who has never seen 普陀山 may still know it as Guanyin's island, just as one who has never climbed 五臺山 may already imagine terraces, monasteries, and 文殊's presence. Gazetteers and miracle records therefore do more than document cults; they help create the conditions under which the cults reproduce themselves.

### 9.4 Representation as Cultic Infrastructure

**Table 17. What representation does for sacred geography**

| Medium | Function in cult formation | Example effect |
|---|---|---|
| Iconography | Makes bodhisattvas instantly recognizable through attributes and posture | 文殊's sword and lion; 地藏's monk form, staff, and jewel |
| Landscape imagery | Gives each mountain a distinct scenic and affective identity | Putuo as island/cave/sea world; Wutai as terraced monastic revelation-space |
| Miracle tales / pilgrimage narratives | Teaches devotees what kinds of encounters to expect at the site | Apparition, rescue, healing, memorial efficacy |
| Gazetteers / descriptive writing | Stabilizes names, routes, temple clusters, and site identity | Converts local cult into durable pilgrimage knowledge |

The larger point is that iconography and literature together form a kind of cultic infrastructure. They allow sacred mountains to circulate beyond the local. A painting of 水月觀音, a sculpted 文殊 on a lion, a monk-form 地藏 with staff and jewel, or a textual account of mountain apparitions can travel where the mountain itself cannot. Through such portable forms, sacred geography becomes reproducible in monasteries, homes, ritual manuals, and the imaginations of distant devotees. This is one reason the Four Sacred Mountains could become national and transregional religious centers rather than remaining only local holy sites.

Representation also stabilizes difference. Without distinct visual and literary codes, the four mountain cults might blur into one another. Instead, the codes sharpen their specialization. Images and texts make these differences durable and transmissible. Sacred geography thus becomes not merely a matter of terrain, but of repeated acts of depiction and narration.

## 10. Conclusion: From Bodhisattva to Religious World

The argument of this article has been that major bodhisattvas in Chinese Buddhism are best understood not as isolated devotional figures but as organizers of religious worlds. In the Chinese case, bodhisattvas became simultaneously textual, spatial, ritual, visual, and sectarian presences. Canonical Mahāyāna sources supplied their basic salvific and doctrinal profiles; sacred mountains localized those profiles in Chinese terrain; visual and literary traditions stabilized them in imagination; and traditions such as 天台、華嚴、禪、淨土 selectively amplified them according to their own doctrinal and practical priorities. The result was not a simple reception of Indian Mahāyāna, but a specifically Chinese reconstitution of the bodhisattva ideal as a durable cultural system.

One of the main conclusions is therefore methodological. Chinese Buddhism cannot be adequately described if one studies only doctrine, only sects, or only popular devotion. The Four Great Bodhisattvas — 觀音、文殊、普賢、地藏 — show why. Their importance lies not merely in what the sutras say about them, nor merely in their later temple cults, but in the interaction between scriptural role and Chinese localization. Guanyin becomes fully intelligible only when the Lotus world is read together with miracle devotion and 普陀山; 文殊 only when wisdom texts are read together with 五臺山; 普賢 only when 行願 is read together with 峨眉山 and liturgical performance; 地藏 only when vow-and-afterlife scripture is read together with memorial religion and 九華山.

A second conclusion concerns system formation. The Four Great Bodhisattvas are not simply four popular deities retrospectively grouped together. In mature Chinese Buddhism they function as a differentiated symbolic order: 觀音 organizes compassion and immediate rescue, 文殊 wisdom and discernment, 普賢 vow and enacted cultivation, 地藏 redemptive endurance in relation to death and karmic extremity. This fourfold arrangement proved so durable because it solved a representational problem: it distributed core Mahāyāna values across figures that were both distinct and mutually compatible.

A third conclusion concerns sacred geography. The Four Sacred Mountains — 五臺山、普陀山、峨眉山、九華山 — were not decorative additions to already finished cults. They were among the main mechanisms by which bodhisattva devotion acquired permanence. Pilgrimage routes, monastic patronage, miracle narratives, artistic programs, and named landscapes gave bodhisattvas territorial depth. Mountains transformed scriptural figures into destinations, and destinations into institutional ecosystems.

A fourth conclusion is historiographic. The apparent symmetry of the mature system should not mislead us into imagining a single moment of origin. The mountain–bodhisattva pairings formed unevenly. More broadly, Buddhist mountain sacralization in China was usually a matter of layering rather than replacement. It entered a landscape already shaped by older notions of sacred mountains, numinous place, and ritual prestige. Chinese pilgrimage traditions broadly centered on places experienced as spiritually potent, where extraordinary presence might become manifest; Buddhist sacred mountains succeeded in part because they could inhabit that preexisting logic while redirecting it toward bodhisattva-centered salvation.

The sectarian comparison likewise reinforces the article's main claim. Sectarian life in Chinese Buddhism did not divide bodhisattvas into exclusive confessional boxes. Instead, it produced recurrent patterns of emphasis. 天台, with its Lotus-centered orientation, gave especially strong place to 觀音. 華嚴, with its Avataṃsaka cosmos, gave structural priority to 文殊 and 普賢. 禪 often re-signified bodhisattvas symbolically and aesthetically rather than systematizing them doctrinally. 淨土 centered 阿彌陀佛 and therefore elevated those figures most closely tied to Amitābha's salvific field, especially 觀音 and, beyond the fourfold core, 大勢至.

The comparative extension to 彌勒、大勢至、藥師 sharpened this point further. Not all sacred figures in Chinese Buddhism become important by the same route. Some, such as 彌勒, endure through broad pan-Buddhist scope and future-oriented expectation. Some, such as 大勢至, derive their strongest force from tight integration into a specific salvific system. Others, such as 藥師, remain highly important through ritual efficacy and pastoral usefulness without becoming the defining emblem of a single major school.

A final conclusion concerns representation. Sacred mountains and bodhisattvas became durable not only because they were preached or visited, but because they were made visible and narratable. Images, temple programs, pilgrimage descriptions, and miracle literature taught devotees what to expect, what to see, and how to remember. In this sense, Chinese Buddhist sacred geography depended on an imaginative infrastructure as much as on physical terrain.

The strongest general formulation, then, is this: in Chinese Buddhism, a bodhisattva is never only a doctrinal figure. A bodhisattva can become a chapter in a scripture, a sectarian emphasis, a painted icon, a ritual partner, a mountain abode, a miracle-bearing landscape, and a focal point of grief, hope, wisdom, or vow. The study of Chinese Buddhism must therefore proceed at the intersection of text, place, image, institution, and practice. Once that is recognized, the Four Great Bodhisattvas appear not as a decorative appendix to Mahāyāna thought, but as one of the principal ways in which Mahāyāna was made Chinese.

### 10.1 Limits and Future Directions

This article has been synthetic rather than exhaustive. Several important areas remain underdeveloped. The first is regional variation: even within China, the reception of a bodhisattva could shift significantly by locality, period, institutional setting, and lay or monastic usage. The second is chronology: the mature fourfold mountain system often obscures the uneven and historically layered formation of the pairings. The third is medium specificity: painting, sculpture, pilgrimage writing, liturgy, and miracle tales do not all shape bodhisattva reception in the same way.

A related limit concerns sectarian language itself. Labels such as 天台、華嚴、禪、淨土 are indispensable, but they can suggest a tidier division of religious life than historical practice actually exhibits. Chinese temples and lay networks routinely crossed these lines. Future research would benefit from closer work on mixed ritual environments, where, for example, a temple might combine Chan institutional identity, Pure Land recitation, Guanyin devotion, and 地藏 memorial rites.

Finally, further work should place Chinese bodhisattva cults more explicitly into transregional comparison. The relation between Chinese sacred mountains and Tibetan, Japanese, or broader East Asian adaptations of bodhisattva geography remains especially promising. The Oxford material on Dunhuang art notes that prominent bodhisattvas came to be associated with realms identified with sacred mountains in China, allowing devotees to visit and worship these beings in specific places. That line of inquiry opens outward from Chinese localization to larger questions of Buddhist spatial imagination across Asia.
