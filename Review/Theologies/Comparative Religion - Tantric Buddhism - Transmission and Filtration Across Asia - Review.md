# What Survives the Journey: Transmission, Filtration, and the Making of Tantric Buddhism Across Asia

**Alternative Title A:** *The Broker’s Cut: How Tantric Buddhism Was Made in Translation*

**Alternative Title B:** *Absence as Evidence: The Anuttarayoga Filter and the Comparative History of Buddhist Esotericism*

-----

## Abstract (English)

This review article develops a comparative transmission history of tantric Buddhism across fifteen centuries and five major cultural transformations, tracing the tradition from its Indian Anuttarayoga matrix through the Silk Road’s layered filtration ecology, the Tang Chinese institutional reception, the Tibetan second diffusion’s complete reconstruction, and the modern colonial and post-colonial reformulations that have shaped how the tradition is understood today. Against the colonial framework’s implicit hierarchy — which measured all Buddhist traditions against a reconstructed “original Buddhism” derived from the Pāli Canon and treated tantric Buddhism as a degenerate deviation — and against Buddhist modernism’s accommodationist strategy of defending Buddhism as rational philosophy at the expense of its ritual dimensions, the article proposes a transmission-and-filtration methodology that treats the absence of content as historically significant as its presence, and that reads each tradition produced by the transmission process as a genuine historical achievement rather than a deficient approximation of a more complete original.

Four mechanisms of transmission are identified and analysed across the cases examined: a chronological mechanism, by which temporal gaps between the development of Indian tantric texts and the opening of transmission channels shaped what was available before any human decision was made; an institutional mechanism, by which the receiving culture’s political theology, patronage structures, and existing philosophical frameworks determined which aspects of the transmitted tradition were institutionally receivable; a cognitive mechanism, by which the prior religious formation of converting communities shaped reception through differential felt resonance rather than explicit doctrinal decision; and a broker mechanism, by which intermediary communities — particularly the Sogdian merchant diaspora — introduced systematic transformations through their own Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and pragmatic commercial formation. Together, these mechanisms produced the Buddhist traditions that the article examines in depth: Tang Chinese *mìjiào* (密教) and Kūkai’s Shingon Two Maṇḍala philosophy; the Silk Road frontier communities of Khotan, Turfan, and Dunhuang; the Uyghur Buddhist synthesis and its Manichaean-Sogdian filtration; the Tibetan Nyingma, Kagyü, and Gelug transmissions; and the *Kālacakra Tantra*‘s isomorphic account of cosmos, body, and awakening-nature. An epistemological reckoning with whether the path achieves what it claims distinguishes four levels of assertion — practical efficacy, psychological transformation, soteriological liberation, and metaphysical identity — and proposes honest verdicts at each level. The article concludes with a general theory of Buddhist transmission, an account of each tradition’s self-sufficiency claim understood as a civilisational achievement rather than an institutional rivalry, and an identification of three open questions — the phenomenology of advanced practice, the political economy of Buddhist institutions, and the conditions of a genuinely post-colonial Buddhist studies — whose resolution the field has not yet achieved.

## 摘要（繁體中文）

本綜述文章對密教的跨文化傳播史進行比較研究，時間跨度長達十五個世紀，涵蓋五次重大的文化轉型。文章追溯密教從印度無上瑜伽怛特羅的原始母體，經由絲綢之路多層次的過濾生態、唐代中國的制度性接受、藏傳佛教第二次弘法期的完整重建，直至現代殖民與後殖民時期的重新詮釋。

面對殖民主義學術框架的隱性等級秩序——該框架以巴利聖典重建的「原始佛教」為標準，將密教視為墮落偏離的產物——以及佛教現代主義的妥協策略——以理性哲學為名為佛教辯護，卻以犧牲其儀式維度為代價——本文提出一種「傳播與過濾」方法論。此方法論將內容的缺席視為與其存在同等重要的歷史證據，並將傳播過程中產生的每一個傳統視為真實的歷史成就，而非某個更完整原型的殘缺摹本。

文章識別並分析了四種傳播機制：一是「時序機制」——印度密教文本的形成與傳播渠道的開放之間的時間落差，在任何人為決策之前便已決定何者可供傳播；二是「制度機制」——接受文化的政治神學、贊助結構及既有哲學框架，決定了所傳內容中何者具有制度可接受性；三是「認知機制」——皈依社群的既有宗教背景，通過差異性的「共鳴感」而非明確的教義決定來塑造接受方式；四是「中介機制」——粟特商人群體等中介社群，以其祆教倫理詞彙、摩尼教光明形而上學和商業實用主義，系統性地轉化所傳內容。

上述四種機制共同塑造了文章深入考察的各個佛教傳統：唐代密教（*mìjiào*，密教）與空海的兩界曼荼羅哲學；絲綢之路上于闐、吐魯番與敦煌等邊疆社群；融入摩尼教與粟特雙重過濾的回鶻佛教綜合體；藏傳佛教的寧瑪、噶舉與格魯各派傳承；以及《時輪怛特羅》對宇宙、身體與覺性本質同構性的宏大論述。

文章對「修道是否實現了其所宣稱的目標」進行認識論上的評估，區分四個層次的主張——實踐功效、心理轉化、解脫論目標與形而上學同一性——並對每一層次給出誠實的評判。文章最終提出一套密教傳播的一般性理論，將每個傳統的「自足性宣稱」理解為文明成就而非制度競爭，並指出該領域尚待解決的三個開放性問題：高階修行的現象學、佛教制度的政治經濟學，以及建構真正後殖民佛學研究的方法論條件。

## Section 1: Introduction — The Transmission Problem as Historiographical Frame

### 1.1 What “Tantric Buddhism” Names and Why the Name Is Already a Problem

When Aurel Stein unsealed Cave 17 at Mogao in 1900 and began extracting the manuscripts that had lain undisturbed for nine centuries, he was handling documents that their depositors had never described as belonging to a thing called “tantric Buddhism.” The monks and scribes who copied dhāraṇī manuals, ritual instructions, and maṇḍala descriptions at Dunhuang during the Tang and Five Dynasties periods understood themselves as practitioners of *mìjiào* (密教, esoteric teaching), of *zhēnyán* (真言, true word), or simply of the Buddha-dharma in its most efficacious forms. The category “tantric Buddhism” — the label that would eventually gather their manuscripts alongside Tibetan completion-stage texts, Indian charnel-ground sādhanas, and Japanese Shingon initiation manuals under a single scholarly heading — was not theirs. It was produced, over the course of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, by a European and North American academic enterprise whose relationship to its subject matter was anything but neutral.

This is not a merely terminological observation. The category “tantric Buddhism” carries embedded assumptions about what the tradition is, what its boundaries are, what its most authentic expressions look like, and how its history should be told — assumptions that, once made visible, turn out to be substantially wrong or at least seriously misleading. Unpacking those assumptions is the first task of any review that aims to engage the field’s actual state rather than its self-image.

The term itself entered European scholarship through the Sanskrit *tantra* — a word whose primary meanings include “loom,” “warp,” “continuity,” and “system,” and which in Indian contexts referred to a broad range of textual and practical traditions, Buddhist and non-Buddhist, whose shared features were never fully systematised by their practitioners. The colonial philological tradition, working primarily from Sanskrit and Pāli manuscripts in the context of the comparative religion project, imposed a retrospective unity on this diversity that served the needs of a particular scholarly programme: the construction of “world religions” as distinct, bounded, doctrinally coherent entities capable of being compared with Christianity along axes that Christianity had itself defined. A “religion” in this framework required a founder, a scripture, a doctrine, and a history of development from original purity through institutional elaboration toward possible reform. “Tantric Buddhism” was constructed to fit this template, with its Indian origins, its canonical texts, its doctrinal development, and its interpretation as a late corruption of an originally pure Buddhism providing the narrative arc the template required.

The consequences of this construction are still felt in the field. The persistence of the “degeneration thesis” — the idea that tantric Buddhism represents Buddhism’s corruption by Hinduism, shamanism, or popular religion — in scholarly writing well into the late twentieth century is a direct legacy of the colonial framework, as Donald Lopez demonstrated with characteristic precision in his 1995 edited volume *Curators of the Buddha*. The privileging of textual over ritual evidence, which led decades of Buddhist scholarship to construct an “original Buddhism” with no relation to the archaeological and epigraphic record of what Buddhists actually did, as Gregory Schopen showed with cumulative force across his career, reflects the same framework’s preference for the philosophical over the practical, the written over the performed, the historically reconstructable over the institutionally living. And the assumption that the Pāli Canon represents Buddhism’s most authentic stratum — an assumption whose political function in colonial Ceylon and Burma was as significant as its scholarly justification — effectively positioned the entire tantric tradition as secondary, derivative, and suspect before the scholarly inquiry had properly begun.

None of this means that the category “tantric Buddhism” is simply an error to be discarded. Categories that have organised a field’s inquiry for over a century have real consequences: they have shaped what texts were edited and translated, what questions were asked, what comparisons were made, and what institutional and intellectual communities were formed around shared objects of study. Abandoning the category entirely would be both practically impossible and intellectually irresponsible, since it would require ignoring the genuine family resemblances — the shared texts, practices, ritual structures, and philosophical commitments — that make it meaningful to speak of traditions from India, Tibet, China, Japan, and Korea as belonging to a recognisable, if internally diverse, religious formation.

What is required instead is a more disciplined relationship to the category: using it as a provisional organising convenience while remaining alert to the ways it distorts what it organises. The present review proposes that one of the most important correctives to the category’s distortions is to approach the traditions it names through the lens of *transmission* rather than *essence* — asking not what tantric Buddhism fundamentally and originally is, but how it has moved, what has been retained and transformed in its movement, and what the pattern of its movement reveals about both the tradition and the cultures through which it has passed. This is a shift from ontology to genealogy, from the question of identity to the question of process, and it has methodological consequences that the following subsection will develop.

### 1.2 The Fourfold Tantra Classification and Its Limitations

The most widely used organisational tool in the study of Buddhist esotericism is the fourfold classification of tantra into Kriyā (作法怛特羅, *zuòfǎ dántèluó*), Caryā (行法怛特羅, *xíngfǎ*), Yoga (瑜伽怛特羅, *yúqié*), and Anuttarayoga (無上瑜伽, *wúshàng yúqié*) — an ascending hierarchy in which each class is understood as a progressively more direct, more powerful, and more philosophically demanding approach to liberation. This classification appears so frequently in both scholarly and popular presentations of Buddhist tantrism that it has acquired an aura of descriptive neutrality, as if it were simply reporting a structure inherent in the tradition rather than imposing one from outside. Examining it carefully reveals something rather different.

The fourfold classification is a Tibetan retrospective systematisation. It was developed by Tibetan scholastic commentators — most influentially in the early systematic presentations associated with the Sakya school and later elaborated by Tsongkhapa in the *Ngagrim Chenmo* — as a way of organising the extraordinarily diverse Indian tantric corpus that the second diffusion had imported into Tibet. As a Tibetan scholastic achievement, it is remarkable: it imposes genuine coherence on material that is internally diverse, identifies real differences in register, emphasis, and philosophical presupposition across different bodies of tantric literature, and provides a pedagogical map that makes an otherwise bewildering corpus navigable. These are not trivial contributions.

But as a description of Indian Buddhist self-understanding, the classification is anachronistic. Indian tantric texts do not, by and large, classify themselves according to this schema. The *Guhyasamāja*, the *Hevajra*, and the *Kālacakra* do not announce themselves as Anuttarayoga texts in contrast to Kriyā texts; the distinction is a retrospective imposition. More significantly, the classification was developed by a tradition — Tibetan Vajrayāna — that had received the full Anuttarayoga corpus and had an institutional interest in establishing the superiority of that corpus over the lower tantra classes. The hierarchy embedded in the classification — “unexcelled” (*anuttara*, literally “nothing above”) as the highest class — reflects Tibetan institutional priorities rather than a neutral historical description.

The consequences for cross-cultural comparison are serious and insufficiently acknowledged. Applying the Tibetan fourfold classification to the Chinese esoteric transmission — as scholars routinely do when noting that China received only Kriyā, Caryā, and Yoga Tantra but not Anuttarayoga — imports a Tibetan evaluative hierarchy into a Chinese institutional context that did not share it. For Kūkai and the Shingon tradition, the *Mahāvairocana Sūtra* and *Vajraśekhara* cycle were not the second and third best available options, consolation prizes for a tradition that had failed to receive the highest teaching. They were the complete and sufficient transmission of the *dharmakāya* Buddha Mahāvairocana’s direct self-expression — a claim whose philosophical sophistication and institutional completeness required no supplement from texts that the tradition did not possess and had not sought.

Conversely, applying the classification to Silk Road communities — to the Dunhuang manuscripts, the Khotanese textual tradition, the Uyghur Buddhist synthesis — produces the misleading impression that what these communities had was a lower and therefore deficient form of the tradition, rather than a distinct practice ecology that developed according to its own institutional logic and patron-driven priorities. The Dunhuang community’s syncretic dhāraṇī culture, the Khotanese community’s royal Buddhist theology, and the Uyghur community’s Manichaean-inflected *tathāgatagarbha* emphasis are not failures to achieve Anuttarayoga — they are fully functional Buddhist systems that addressed the specific religious, social, and political needs of their communities with the resources available to them.

This review will use the fourfold classification where it is analytically useful — particularly in tracing what was and was not transmitted between India and Tibet, and in characterising the specific doctrinal and practical features of the Tibetan Anuttarayoga corpus — while resisting its use as an implicit evaluative hierarchy. The question of whether what East Asian Buddhism received was less than, equivalent to, or simply different from what Tibet received cannot be answered by applying the Tibetan classification to the East Asian corpus. It requires instead a direct comparison of what each tradition claimed, what philosophical and practical resources each developed, and what soteriological results each achieved on its own terms — a comparison that this review undertakes in Sections 3 through 6.

One further limitation of the fourfold classification deserves attention. The classification organises the tantric corpus along a single axis of progressive interiority — from external ritual (Kriyā) through mixed external-internal (Caryā) through primarily internal visualisation (Yoga) to the fully interiorised and physiologically engaged (Anuttarayoga). This single axis, while capturing something real, obscures other dimensions of variation that are equally important for understanding how the traditions actually differ. The *Kālacakra*, nominally classified as Anuttarayoga, differs from the *Hevajra* and *Cakrasaṃvara* in ways that the fourfold classification cannot capture: its cosmological ambition, its astronomical system, its comprehensive isomorphism of outer, inner, and alternative reality, its eschatological political theology. These differences matter for understanding the tradition’s intellectual history, and a classification system that cannot register them is inadequate to the field’s actual complexity. The present review therefore supplements the fourfold classification with a more functionally oriented analysis that asks, for each tradition examined, what specific problems it was developed to solve and what resources it deployed in their solution.

### 1.3 Methodological Commitments: Absence, Filtration, and the Archaeology of Practice

The central methodological commitment of this review can be stated simply, though its implications are extensive: *the history of tantric Buddhism’s transmission is better understood through its absences than through its presences*. What was not transmitted, why it was not transmitted, and what was put in its place by the receiving cultures — these questions reveal more about both the transmitted tradition and the cultures that received it than a straightforward account of positive transmission can provide.

This is not a paradoxical or merely rhetorical position. It reflects a genuine historiographical insight about how religious transmission works, one that has been developed in different ways by several of the scholars whose work most directly informs this review. Gregory Schopen’s demonstration that the textual canon systematically misrepresents the practice reality of early Indian Buddhism — that archaeologically attested practices like relic veneration, image worship, and the endowment of monasteries with productive property are largely absent from the normative legal texts — established the principle that the canonical record and the practice record are not the same thing, and that the gap between them is historically informative rather than merely inconvenient. What the texts do not say about what Buddhists did tells us something important about the ideological work those texts were performing — about what image of Buddhism the textual tradition was constructing and why that construction required certain silences.

Paul Copp’s work on Dunhuang dhāraṇī manuscripts, particularly his 2014 *The Body Incantatory*, extends this insight into the esoteric domain. Copp’s central argument is that Dunhuang dhāraṇī practitioners did not experience themselves as operating at the periphery of a tradition whose centre was defined by the great canonical sūtras and philosophical treatises. They experienced themselves as wielding genuine transformative power through the ritual deployment of sacred speech — a power that did not require philosophical contextualisation, canonical authorisation, or institutional validation to be real. The absence of philosophical scaffolding in many Dunhuang dhāraṇī texts is not evidence of the practitioners’ ignorance or superficiality; it is evidence that the scaffolding was not part of the practice as these practitioners understood it. Reading that absence correctly — as a positive feature of a distinct practice ecology rather than a deficit relative to the metropolitan canonical standard — is one of the methodological moves this review consistently attempts.

Jacob Dalton’s work on early Tibetan tantric manuscripts, most fully developed in *The Taming of the Demons* (2011), provides a complementary methodological model for reading practice evidence against later systematised canonical accounts. Dalton’s comparison of Dunhuang-period Tibetan tantric manuscripts with the mature Nyingma canonical presentation reveals a practice world that is less philosophically coherent, more ritually raw, and in some respects more explicitly transgressive than the canonical presentation suggests — a world in which the violent subjugation of demons, the ritual consumption of meat and alcohol, and what appear to be *karmamudra*-adjacent practices appear in forms not yet domesticated by the systematic commentarial tradition. This finding does not discredit the later systematisation, but it reveals that systematisation as an active process of selection and reframing rather than a passive documentation of what was already there.

The broker-as-filter model, developed in this review’s analysis of Sogdian intermediaries, represents a further methodological extension of the absence-and-filtration framework. The Sogdians are, in the conventional historiography of Buddhist transmission, largely invisible — mentioned as caravan leaders and wealthy donors but rarely analysed as agents whose religious formation, linguistic competence, and social interests shaped what they transmitted. Making them visible requires reading their traces in Old Uyghur Buddhist vocabulary, in the structural preferences of the Sogdian-language Buddhist literary tradition, and in the commercial and diplomatic networks through which Buddhist texts and teachers moved. None of these traces is individually decisive; together they constitute a pattern of systematic influence whose reconstruction is speculative at points but whose overall shape is sufficiently clear to transform how the Uyghur Buddhist synthesis is understood. The methodological principle is that absence of direct documentation of an intermediary’s influence is not evidence of the absence of influence — it may instead reflect the structural invisibility of intermediaries in a historiographical tradition that privileges endpoints over the processes that connect them.

These methodological commitments — reading absences, attending to practice below the canonical surface, recovering invisible intermediaries, and resisting the fourfold classification’s implicit evaluative hierarchy — together constitute what this review proposes as an *archaeology of tantric transmission*: an approach that treats the historical record not as a transparent window onto what happened but as a layered deposit of evidence whose most significant features are often those least immediately visible. The following sections apply this methodology to the specific transmission histories of Chinese *mìjiào*, the Silk Road frontier communities, the Tibetan reception, and the modern colonial encounter, in each case asking what the transmission’s shape — including its silences, its distortions, and its creative transformations — reveals about both the tradition being transmitted and the cultures through which it passed.

A final methodological note concerns the relationship between this review’s historical and philosophical dimensions. The transmission history developed in Sections 2 through 5 is not merely contextual background for the philosophical analysis in Section 6 — it is itself philosophically significant. How a tradition understands the relationship between ritual efficacy and philosophical depth, how it establishes the epistemological credentials of its soteriological claims, and how it responds to the verification problem that those claims inevitably generate are not questions that can be answered in the abstract. They must be answered with reference to the specific institutional, cultural, and intellectual contexts that shaped the tradition’s actual development — contexts that the transmission history makes legible. Philosophy and history are not separate inquiries that this review awkwardly combines; they are aspects of a single inquiry into what tantric Buddhism is, how it became what it is, and what can honestly be said about what it claims.

## Section 2: The Indian Matrix — What Was Available to Transmit

### 2.1 The Historical Development of Anuttarayoga: Chronology and Textual Corpus

The question of what was available for transmission from India to East Asia and Tibet is, before anything else, a question about chronology — and the chronology of Anuttarayoga’s development turns out to be one of the most consequential and least appreciated facts in the comparative history of Buddhist esotericism. The standard narrative of the East Asian transmission tends to treat the absence of Anuttarayoga from the Chinese and Japanese canonical traditions as a decision made about a pre-existing body of texts — as if the full Anuttarayoga corpus were sitting in Nālandā’s library when Amoghavajra arrived in Chang’an in 720 CE, and he simply chose not to bring it. The historical record suggests something considerably more complicated: the corpus that Tibet received in the second diffusion of the tenth and eleventh centuries was not fully available during the Tang dynasty’s florescence, and the gap between what the Tang masters could have transmitted and what they actually did transmit is smaller than the absence of Anuttarayoga from the Chinese canon implies.

The earliest text that scholars identify as belonging to the Anuttarayoga register is the *Guhyasamāja Tantra* (秘密集會怛特羅, *Mìmì jíhuì dántèluó*, “Tantra of the Secret Assembly”), whose composition Ronald Davidson and other scholars of Indian Buddhism date to somewhere in the seventh century CE, with significant subsequent expansion and editorial development continuing through the eighth and ninth centuries. The *Guhyasamāja* is the oldest and in certain respects the most philosophically foundational of the Anuttarayoga texts — it provides the framework of the generation and completion stages in their earliest systematic form, establishes the central role of the *vajrācārya* (qualified tantric master) as the necessary mediator of initiation, and introduces the five-buddha maṇḍala system in its Anuttarayoga register, where the five buddhas are understood not merely as cosmic emanations of Vairocana but as the five wisdoms in their fully embodied, physiologically grounded form. But even granting the seventh-century dating for the *Guhyasamāja*‘s earliest stratum, the text’s mature form — with its full philosophical apparatus, its elaborate commentarial tradition, and its integration into the broader Anuttarayoga system — was not stabilised until the ninth century at the earliest, precisely the period during which Tang dynasty Buddhism was suffering the devastation of the Huichang persecution.

The *Hevajra Tantra* (喜金剛怛特羅, *Xǐ jīngāng*), which together with the *Cakrasaṃvara* constitutes the core of what Tibetan classification calls the *yoginī* or “mother” tantra class within Anuttarayoga, appears to have reached its current form somewhere in the late eighth or early ninth century. David Snellgrove’s landmark 1959 edition and translation remains foundational, but the text’s compositional history is sufficiently complex — involving multiple layers of composition, commentary, and interpolation — that a precise date is difficult to establish. What can be said with confidence is that the *Hevajra* in anything like the form that circulated in the Tibetan tradition was not available as a stable, transmissible text during the period of Amoghavajra’s activity in Chang’an. The same holds for the *Cakrasaṃvara Tantra*, the *Caṇḍamahāroṣaṇa*, and the other texts central to the mother tantra transmission that the Kagyü school would later make the core of its lineage.

The *Kālacakra Tantra* (時輪怛特羅, *Shílún dántèluó*, “Tantra of the Wheel of Time”) occupies a position at the outer temporal limit of Indian Buddhist philosophical development that makes its non-transmission to East Asia a matter of simple historical impossibility rather than curatorial decision. The most authoritative scholarship, represented by John Newman’s detailed study of the text’s historical references and Alexis Sanderson’s broader analysis of the Śaiva material the *Kālacakra* engages with and responds to, places the text’s composition firmly in the late tenth or early eleventh century CE. Atiśa brought it to Tibet in 1042 CE; its arrival in the Tibetan world is contemporaneous with the final collapse of Khotan and the sealing of the Dunhuang cave. The *Kālacakra* was, by this point, not merely unavailable to the Tang transmission — the Tang dynasty itself had been dead for a century and a half. It represents the culmination of a process of Indian Buddhist philosophical development that unfolded after the channels of transmission between India and China had effectively closed.

This chronological picture has a significant implication that the field has been slow to draw with full clarity: the absence of the mature Anuttarayoga corpus from the Chinese Buddhist canon is not, in the first instance, evidence of a Chinese decision. It is evidence of a temporal gap between the maturation of the Indian tradition and the period during which the East Asian transmission channels were most fully open. The Tang dynasty’s great translation project was at its most active and institutionally robust between approximately 650 and 845 CE — a period that overlaps with only the earliest, least systematised stratum of the Anuttarayoga tradition. By the time the higher tantras had developed into the coherent, philosophically integrated system that Tibet received in the second diffusion, the institutional conditions in China that would have been required to receive them — a functioning imperial translation bureau, active diplomatic contact with Indian monastic centres, a community of qualified Indian masters willing to travel to Chang’an — had been severely damaged by the Huichang persecution and the subsequent collapse of the Tang state.

This does not mean that the later curatorial and institutional filters were insignificant — the evidence for deliberate selection, discussed in Section 3, is real and important. It means that the chronological filter was primary, and that subsequent filters operated on a corpus that was already incomplete by historical necessity before any deliberate choices were made. Understanding this sequence is important because it changes the moral and intellectual character of the East Asian tradition’s relationship to Anuttarayoga: the Chinese and Japanese traditions did not turn away from a fully available higher teaching. They built, with extraordinary philosophical and ritual sophistication, on the materials that the historical circumstances of the late seventh and eighth centuries made available — and what they built deserves evaluation on its own terms rather than as a deficient version of something they never had the opportunity to receive.

The textual corpus that did transmit successfully to East Asia — the *Mahāvairocana Sūtra* (大毘盧遮那成佛神變加持經) and the *Vajraśekhara* (金剛頂經, *Jīngāng dǐng jīng*) cycle — belongs primarily to the Yoga Tantra classification in the Tibetan schema. What these texts share, and what distinguishes them from the Anuttarayoga corpus, is their orientation toward the construction and inhabitation of the maṇḍala as a purified cosmological field — the practitioner approaches the central deity from a position of relative externality, ritually constructing a pure environment and inviting the deity’s presence and blessing, rather than dissolving the boundary between practitioner and deity through the physiological technologies of the completion stage. This difference in spatial and relational logic reflects a fundamentally different understanding of what the practitioner is and where liberation is located — a difference whose philosophical implications Section 3’s analysis of Kūkai’s Two Maṇḍala philosophy will develop in full.

### 2.2 The Philosophical Integration of Śūnyatā and Somatic Practice

The most important and most frequently misunderstood feature of Indian Anuttarayoga Buddhism is the relationship between its transgressive somatic practices and its Madhyamaka philosophical framework. The misunderstanding takes two forms, each misleading in a different direction. The first, characteristic of colonial and early modern scholarship, treats the transgressive elements as evidence of philosophical degeneration — the intrusion of Śaiva tantrism’s antinomian energies into a Buddhist tradition that had previously been characterised by ethical rigour and rational analysis. The second, characteristic of certain contemporary apologetic presentations, treats the philosophical framework as an after-the-fact rationalisation of practices whose real motivation was something else entirely. Both misreadings fail to take seriously what the tradition itself insists upon: that the philosophical and the somatic are not two separate things that happen to coexist in the Anuttarayoga texts but a single integrated system whose elements are mutually necessary and mutually intelligible only in relation to each other.

The philosophical justification for Anuttarayoga’s transgressive elements rests on a specific reading of *śūnyatā* (空性, *kōngxìng*, emptiness) that is neither the most conservative nor the most radical interpretation available within the Madhyamaka tradition. The Anuttarayoga tradition works with what the Tibetan tradition identifies as the *prāsaṅgika* Madhyamaka of Candrakīrti: emptiness as the absence of inherent existence (*svabhāva*, 自性, *zìxìng*) in all phenomena without exception — not merely persons but dharmas, not merely conditioned phenomena but the unconditioned, not merely the objects of ordinary experience but the categories through which we organise that experience, including the categories “pure” and “impure,” “sacred” and “profane,” “permitted” and “transgressive.” If these categories are themselves empty of inherent existence — if the distinction between a ritual offering of flowers and a ritual offering of sexual fluids is a conventional designation rather than an ultimate fact — then the transgressive gesture of offering what is conventionally impure is, from the perspective of ultimate truth, no different in kind from the conventional gesture of offering what is conventionally pure. The transgression is not the point; the recognition that the category “transgressive” is itself empty is the point.

This is not, it must be emphasised, a licence for ethical anarchism, and the tradition’s most careful philosophical representatives are explicit on this. Tsongkhapa, whose careful analysis of the Anuttarayoga philosophical framework in the *Ngagrim Chenmo* represents the field’s most rigorous treatment, argues at length that the recognition of emptiness at the ultimate level does not dissolve the practical validity of conventional ethical distinctions. The conventional level remains operative and practically binding for beings who have not yet achieved the non-conceptual recognition of ultimate reality. The transgressive elements of Anuttarayoga are carefully delimited ritual contexts in which the recognition is *enacted* rather than *assumed*, under the guidance of a qualified teacher who can distinguish genuine enactment from self-deceptive performance.

The somatic dimension of Anuttarayoga — its use of the body’s most powerful energies as the medium of recognition — follows from the philosophical framework rather than contradicting it. If the goal of practice is the recognition of bliss-emptiness inseparability (*bde stong dbyer med*, 樂空不二, *lèkōng bùèr*), then a practice that generates the most intense available experience of bliss and combines it with the recognition of that bliss’s empty nature is, by the tradition’s own logic, the most efficient available path. The bliss generated by the completion stage’s physiological technologies — the experience that arises when the winds enter the central channel and the drops are activated at the *cakra* nodes — is not chosen arbitrarily or for its intrinsic pleasantness. It is chosen because it is the most powerful available medium for the specific kind of recognition the path requires: a recognition that is not merely intellectual but experiential, not merely cognitive but somatic, not merely philosophical but lived.

This integration of philosophical rigour and somatic precision is what makes Anuttarayoga, at its best, something genuinely distinctive in the history of Buddhist thought — not a corruption of Buddhist philosophy by Hindu tantrism but an extension of Buddhist philosophical analysis into dimensions of human experience that the *sūtra* vehicle had systematically bracketed. Understanding this integration is also prerequisite to understanding why the transgressive and somatic elements of Anuttarayoga could not simply be stripped away and the philosophical framework transmitted independently. The philosophical claim — that afflictions are wisdom, that bliss and emptiness are inseparable, that saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are non-dual — is not merely a theoretical proposition that could be held intellectually without any corresponding change in how the practitioner relates to their body and its energies. It is a claim about the nature of experience that requires a corresponding experiential confirmation to become more than a philosophical position.

### 2.3 The Mahāsiddha Tradition: Charismatic Transmission Outside Institutional Frameworks

The monastic university system of Nālandā, Vikramaśīla, and Odantapurī provided the institutional infrastructure within which the Anuttarayoga philosophical tradition was systematised, commented upon, and eventually transmitted to Tibet. But it was not the only transmission ecology available in Indian Buddhism, and understanding its relationship to the parallel tradition of the *mahāsiddha* — the great accomplished one whose realisation manifests outside and sometimes against the institutional norms of monastic Buddhism — is essential for understanding both what Anuttarayoga is and why it has the character it has.

The eighty-four *mahāsiddhas* whose biographies are collected in Abhayadatta’s twelfth-century *Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti* represent a distinct modality of Buddhist religious life that coexisted with, sometimes overlapped with, and frequently stood in tension with the monastic institutional tradition. The *siddha* — the term means “one who has accomplished,” specifically one who has accomplished the extraordinary capacities (*siddhi*, 悉地, *xīdì*) that the tantric path promises — is typically presented as a figure whose realisation breaks the boundaries of conventional social and religious order. Virūpa pours libations of wine rather than water. Saraha works as an arrow-maker and lives with a low-caste woman rather than in a monastery. Tilopa eats fish entrails on the bank of the Ganges. Nāropā undergoes twelve years of extreme physical trials at the instruction of his teacher before receiving the pointing-out instruction that triggers his realisation. These are not merely colourful biographical details; they encode the tradition’s understanding of how realisation works and what it looks like when it arrives.

The *mahāsiddha* tradition makes a claim about transmission that is structurally different from the monastic institutional claim. In the institutional model, transmission occurs through a regulated sequence: the student receives the appropriate initiations from a qualified *ācārya*, studies the relevant texts, undergoes the prescribed meditative training, and achieves the designated stages of the path in a sequence that can be verified by reference to standard texts and by assessment from experienced teachers. In the *mahāsiddha* model, by contrast, transmission occurs through a direct encounter between the student’s readiness and the teacher’s capacity to recognise and catalyse it, in a moment that is by its nature unpredictable, unscheduled, and frequently involves precisely the disruption of ordinary expectations that allows something genuinely new to happen.

The *mahāsiddha* tradition also plays a crucial role in understanding the internal diversity of the Anuttarayoga corpus itself. The texts that the *siddhas* produced or were associated with — the *dohā* (spontaneous verse), the *caryāgīti* (songs of practice), and the *upadeśa* (direct instructions) — represent a dimension of the tradition in which philosophical precision and lyrical spontaneity coexist in ways that systematic commentarial writing cannot fully capture. These texts transmit something that the institutional tradition’s canonical corpus does not quite transmit: the texture of realisation as it presents itself from inside a life dedicated to the path, in all its difficulty, its humour, its physical extremity, and its moments of recognition that arrive unexpected and transform everything they touch.

## Section 3: The Tang Chinese Reception — Sophisticated Filtering

### 3.1 The Three Tang Masters and the Architecture of Chinese Esotericism

The transmission of esoteric Buddhism to Tang China was not a process of passive reception but of active construction, and the three Indian masters who accomplished it — Śubhākarasiṃha (善無畏, *Shànwúwèi*, 637–735), Vajrabodhi (金剛智, *Jīngāngzhì*, 671–741), and Amoghavajra (不空金剛, *Bùkōng Jīngāng*, 705–774) — were not conduits through whom an Indian tradition flowed unchanged into a Chinese vessel. They were intellectuals of the first order who brought specific lineages from specific institutional contexts in India, navigated the specific political and intellectual environment of the Tang court, made deliberate decisions about what to translate and what to leave aside, and shaped the tradition they established in ways that reflected both the resources they brought and the constraints they faced.

Śubhākarasiṃha arrived in Chang’an in 716 CE, bearing the *Mahāvairocana Sūtra* and its associated ritual system, which he had received through a lineage tracing back to Nāgārjuna’s disciple Nāgabodhi. His translation of the *Mahāvairocana Sūtra* in 724 CE, produced in collaboration with the Chinese monk Yixing (一行, 683–727), established the Womb Realm Maṇḍala system as one of the two pillars of Chinese esotericism. Vajrabodhi arrived two years later, in 720 CE, bringing the *Vajraśekhara* cycle from a South Indian lineage and establishing the Diamond Realm Maṇḍala system as the transmission’s second pillar.

Amoghavajra’s career at the Tang court is one of the most remarkable stories in the history of religious transmission. Born in Ceylon or possibly northern India, trained in the *Vajraśekhara* lineage under Vajrabodhi and subsequently in South Asia where he sought out additional transmissions, he served three consecutive Tang emperors — Xuanzong, Suzong, and Daizong — as a ritual specialist, diplomatic intermediary, and institutional architect of Chinese esotericism. His translation output was prodigious, and his ritual activity equally consequential: the *hùguó* (護國) rituals he performed for the imperial court established the political theology of esoteric Buddhism as state-protective technology that would shape both Chinese and Japanese esotericism for centuries.

What Amoghavajra did not translate is, for this review’s purposes, as significant as what he did. The *Vajraśekhara* cycle in its full Indian form comprises eighteen assemblies (*astādaśa yoga*), of which Amoghavajra translated only the first. Later scholarly work, including Yūkei Matsunaga’s careful philological analysis, has established that Amoghavajra’s decision to translate only the first assembly was not ignorance of the rest — he demonstrably knew the fuller system — but a curatorial choice whose motivations, while not documented in explicit statement, are legible from the context in which he worked. The Tang imperial court required Buddhist ritual that was cosmologically sophisticated, politically useful, aesthetically impressive, and socially acceptable within the framework of Confucian bodily ethics and hierarchical social organisation. A translation of the *Vajraśekhara*’s later assemblies, with their more explicit treatment of the consort principle and their closer approach to the Anuttarayoga register, would have provided exactly the ammunition that Buddhist critics had always had available: the accusation that Indian Buddhism was a form of moral depravity dressed in philosophical clothing.

### 3.2 Yixing’s *Darijing shu* and the Huayan Intellectualisation of Esoteric Content

The *Darijing shu* (大日經疏, “Commentary on the Sūtra of the Great Sun”) produced by Yixing (一行, 683–727) in collaboration with Śubhākarasiṃha is the most important single document for understanding what Chinese Buddhist intellectual culture did with the esoteric transmission it received. Yixing was simultaneously a serious Buddhist scholar trained in the full range of Chinese Buddhist philosophical traditions — Tiantai, Huayan, Yogācāra, as well as the early Chan currents — and a mathematician and astronomer of the first rank. This combination gave him resources for reading the *Mahāvairocana Sūtra* that no purely devotional or purely ritual approach could have produced.

The *Darijing shu*’s central interpretive move is the identification of the *Mahāvairocana Sūtra*‘s teachings with the Huayan philosophical analysis of the *dharmadhātu* (法界, *fǎjiè*) — the total field of phenomena as it is understood from the perspective of awakened awareness. In Huayan philosophy, the *dharmadhātu* is characterised by the principle of *shìshì wú’ài* (事事無礙, “unobstructed interpenetration of phenomena”) — the recognition that every phenomenon in the universe is simultaneously itself and a reflection of every other phenomenon. Yixing reads the Womb Realm Maṇḍala through this Huayan lens: every figure in the maṇḍala is simultaneously itself and a reflection of Mahāvairocana, and the practitioner who constructs the maṇḍala in visualisation is recognising the structure of their own mind as it actually is — a *dharmadhātu* in which every mental event is simultaneously itself and a manifestation of the buddha-nature that is its ultimate ground.

This interpretive move has a specific consequence for the *Darijing shu*‘s treatment of somatic and transgressive elements. Where the Indian reading of these elements would invoke the *kleśa*-as-wisdom principle, Yixing’s reading consistently abstracts them into cognitive and cosmological terms. The maṇḍala’s female figures, which in an Anuttarayoga context would invoke the consort principle, are read as representations of wisdom aspects of the practitioner’s own awakened mind. This abstraction is not evasion — it is applying the conceptual resources available to him to a system whose full Indian context he partially but not completely understood, in an environment where the somatic and transgressive dimensions of that full context were not transmissible. The result is a reading that is genuinely philosophically coherent on its own terms and that generates insights — particularly about the relationship between the maṇḍala’s visual structure and the *dharmadhātu*’s philosophical structure — that the Indian tradition had not explicitly articulated.

### 3.3 The Political Theology of *Hùguó* Esotericism and Its Doctrinal Consequences

The relationship between esoteric Buddhism and Tang imperial power was a constitutive feature of how Chinese esotericism understood its own function in the world. The political theology of *hùguó* (護國, nation-protecting) esotericism is not a corruption of Buddhism’s spiritual essence by worldly pragmatism but a specific application of Buddhist cosmic vision to the problem of social order — an application whose philosophical foundations shape the entire received corpus in ways that extend well beyond explicitly political texts.

The philosophical basis of *hùguó* esotericism is the claim that the *cakravartin* (轉輪聖王, wheel-turning holy king) and the Buddha stand in a relationship of cosmic complementarity: the Buddha’s spiritual sovereignty over the realm of dharma and the king’s political sovereignty over the realm of society are not competing claims but mutually supporting aspects of a single cosmic order. In the esoteric context, the relationship is rendered more intimate: the emperor, protected by Vairocana’s cosmic power as mediated through the rituals performed by the *ācārya*, becomes a participant in the cosmic maṇḍala’s order — not merely a political ruler supported by religious legitimation but a figure whose sovereignty is an expression of the maṇḍala’s own structure.

This political theology has a specific doctrinal consequence that has not been fully appreciated: it makes the *purity of the ritual form* the primary criterion of efficacy, rather than the *depth of the practitioner’s realisation*. In the Anuttarayoga framework, the efficacy of the path depends fundamentally on the depth of the practitioner’s philosophical realisation. In the *hùguó* ritual framework, by contrast, the efficacy depends on the correct performance of the ritual form. This shift from realisation-based to form-based efficacy is not philosophically indefensible, but it has significant doctrinal consequences: it makes esoteric practice accessible to any properly ordained monk who has received the appropriate initiations and learned the correct ritual procedures, and it makes the institutional structures that certify correct form the primary guarantors of practice legitimacy.

The *hùguó* political theology also shaped the iconographic programme of Chinese esoteric Buddhism. The central figure of Chinese esoteric ritual is Mahāvairocana (大日如來) — luminous, seated in royal composure at the centre of a perfectly ordered maṇḍala, radiating the five wisdoms in all directions. This is an image of cosmic sovereignty: the Buddha as the cosmic king whose ordered maṇḍala reflects and guarantees the ordered empire. The wrathful deities present in the outer courts of the Chinese esoteric maṇḍala are subordinated as enforcers of cosmic order rather than primary objects of identification and transformation — a choice that reflects the *hùguó* political theology’s fundamental requirement that the Buddhist cosmos mirror and support rather than challenge the social order the emperor maintained.

### 3.4 Kūkai’s Two Maṇḍala Philosophy as East Asian Esotericism’s Philosophical Apex

When Kūkai (空海, 774–835 CE) arrived in Chang’an in 804 CE and sought out Huiguo (惠果, 746–805 CE) at the Qinglong monastery, he was completing a trajectory that the entire Tang esoteric transmission had been building toward — the encounter between a fully prepared student and the master who held the complete transmission of both the Womb Realm and Diamond Realm systems in their philosophical integration. Huiguo transmitted both maṇḍala systems in their complete form over several months before his death, and Kūkai subsequently established Shingon (真言) as a distinct Japanese Buddhist school, founding the monastic complex at Mount Kōya (高野山) that has preserved the Tang esoteric transmission in a form more complete than what survived in China after the Huichang persecution.

The foundation of Kūkai’s philosophical contribution is his formulation of the *ri-chi funi* (理智不二, “non-duality of principle and wisdom”) doctrine as the explicit philosophical content encoded in the relationship between the two maṇḍalas. The Womb Realm Maṇḍala (胎藏界曼荼羅, *Taizōkai mandara*) encodes *ri* (理, principle/essence/noumenon) — the ground of reality as it always already is, complete and perfect, the matrix from which all phenomena emerge and to which all phenomena return, the buddha-nature that is the ultimate nature of every sentient being’s experience. The Diamond Realm Maṇḍala (金剛界曼荼羅, *Kongōkai mandara*) encodes *chi* (智, wisdom/knowing/awareness) — the awakened consciousness that recognises *ri*, the diamond-clear awareness that is indestructible because it is in principle unaffectable by the conditions that afflict ordinary consciousness. The *funi* (不二, non-duality) that joins them is not the merger of two previously separate things into a third thing but the recognition that they were never ultimately distinct.

Kūkai’s philosophical synthesis is, among other things, a response to the complementary inadequacy of the two most important alternative Chinese Buddhist philosophical traditions. Huayan philosophy provides extraordinary resources for understanding the Womb Realm’s *dharmadhātu* cosmology but is less clear about what it is for reality to be *known* by an awakened mind. Chan philosophy is supremely attentive to the quality of direct knowing but lacks resources for understanding the Womb Realm’s dimension — the *ri* that is the ground of the recognition. Kūkai’s Two Maṇḍala philosophy holds Huayan’s cosmological comprehensiveness and Chan’s epistemological directness in non-dual tension — a synthesis in which the cosmological and the epistemological are aspects of a single reality that must be held together to be understood at all.

The doctrine of *sokushin jōbutsu* (即身成佛, “buddhahood in this very body”) that Kūkai articulates in his text of the same name is the soteriological culmination of the Two Maṇḍala philosophy. It follows directly from the *ri-chi funi* doctrine: if the practitioner’s body, speech, and mind are, in their ultimate nature, already the Buddha’s body, speech, and mind, then the Three Mysteries practice that aligns the practitioner’s three mysteries with the Buddha’s three mysteries is not a movement toward a distant goal but a recognition of what is already the case. Buddhahood in this very body is not an achievement produced by practice but a recognition enabled by it — and recognition, unlike accumulation, is in principle instantaneous.

## Section 4: The Silk Road Ecology — Transmission Below the Canonical Surface

### 4.1 The Dunhuang Cave Library as Sediment Record of Lived Practice

When Aurel Stein negotiated access to Cave 17 at Mogao in 1907 and began systematically cataloguing its contents, he was working with an assumption that shaped both his selection of materials for acquisition and his subsequent scholarly framing of the find: that the cave’s most important contents were its oldest and most canonical texts, the documents most closely approximating what he took to be the authentic Indian Buddhist tradition in its Chinese transmission. This assumption — shared by Paul Pelliot and by most of the early scholarship — was not arbitrary. It reflected the same hierarchy of value that had shaped European Buddhist studies from its inception: the canonical over the apocryphal, the philosophical over the ritual, the textually authoritative over the practically effective.

The consequence was a significant delay in recognising what the Dunhuang cache actually is: not a library of canonical texts supplemented by miscellaneous practical materials, but a sediment record of what Buddhist practice looked like across several centuries in a specific regional context — a record in which the canonical and the apocryphal, the philosophical and the practical, the orthodox and the syncretic are distributed not according to the hierarchical values of metropolitan Buddhist institutions but according to the actual needs and capacities of the communities that produced and used the documents.

The reorientation of Dunhuang studies that began in the 1970s has progressively corrected this bias. Paul Copp’s contribution in *The Body Incantatory* (2014) is exemplary in method as well as finding: approaching Dunhuang dhāraṇī manuscripts as evidence of a distinct practice culture with its own logic rather than as deficient versions of the canonical esoteric texts that authorised them. Jacob Dalton and Sam van Schaik’s systematic study of the Tibetan Dunhuang manuscripts documented the presence of *Mahāyoga* ritual manuals, wrathful deity sādhanas, and completion-stage-adjacent material in the Tibetan manuscripts that has no equivalent in the Chinese-language materials from the same cave. This finding — that Tibetan-language practitioners at Dunhuang had access to a register of esoteric practice that their Chinese-language counterparts did not — establishes that the absence of Anuttarayoga-level content from the Chinese esoteric tradition was not a function of geographical inaccessibility but of practitioner-level cultural filtering that maintained the distinction even in a frontier context nominally free from metropolitan institutional oversight.

The most historically important conclusion that emerges from reading the Dunhuang evidence together is that the boundary between the Tang Chinese esoteric tradition and the Anuttarayoga register was maintained not by any single institutional or political decision but by a distributed network of practice choices made by individual practitioners and communities across an extended frontier region over several generations. The filtering was not a filter — a single mechanism through which transmission passed — but a filtering *disposition* distributed across the entire practice ecology.

### 4.2 Comparative Frontier Communities: Khotan, Turfan, and Dunhuang

The comparative analysis of frontier Buddhist communities requires a methodological discipline that is easily lost in the richness of the individual sites’ evidence: the discipline of distinguishing between features that appear across multiple frontier communities and are therefore likely to be structural products of the frontier condition itself, and features that appear in only one community and are therefore likely to reflect that community’s specific cultural, political, or demographic situation.

Four features appear across all three sites — Khotan, Turfan, and Dunhuang — and are absent or significantly less pronounced in the metropolitan Chinese Buddhist tradition: the autonomisation of ritual practice from its canonical philosophical scaffolding; the normalisation of multilingual textual transmission rather than the metropolitan model of canonical authorisation in a single prestige language; the direct shaping of doctrinal emphasis by patron needs rather than institutional hierarchies insulated from lay patronage; and the active rather than passive relationship of frontier communities to textual production.

Khotan’s distinctive feature within the comparative framework is the depth and duration of its royal Buddhist theological commitment. The Khotanese kingdom’s self-understanding as a *buddhakṣetra* — a buddha-field guaranteed by the bodhisattva Vaiśravaṇa and constituted by the dharma’s presence — produced a Buddhist cultural formation of unusual institutional stability and unusual vulnerability. The stability is visible in the depth and continuity of Khotanese Buddhist artistic and literary production across nine centuries. The vulnerability is visible in the speed of the collapse that followed the Qarakhanid conquest around 1006 CE: a Buddhism whose institutional life was concentrated in royal patronage had no resilience against the withdrawal of that patronage.

Turfan’s distinctive feature is the genuine religious pluralism of its practice environment. Where Khotan was effectively a Buddhist polity and Dunhuang was a Buddhist community within a broader Chinese institutional framework, Turfan was an environment in which Buddhism competed with Manichaeism, Zoroastrianism, and Nestorian Christianity in conditions of genuine openness. This competitive environment produced a Buddhism that was more attentive to practical efficacy — a Buddhism of protective dhāraṇī, merit-making donation, and narrative moral instruction — and unusually open to cross-traditional intellectual exchange.

The counterintuitive finding that emerges from comparing all three sites is that frontier conditions were *less* capable of sustaining Anuttarayoga than metropolitan centres, not more. The higher tantras required sustained guru-student lineages and initiatory infrastructure that demographic thinness and patron-dependence could not support. The absence of Anuttarayoga even at these relatively uncontrolled frontier sites suggests not suppression but infrastructural incompatibility.

### 4.3 The Broker-as-Filter: Sogdian Intermediaries and the Third Filtration Layer

The Sogdian merchant community constitutes what this review identifies as the most historically important and least adequately studied agent of Buddhist transmission across the Central Asian Silk Road. Their importance is not difficult to establish — the Sogdian commercial networks were the primary infrastructure through which goods, people, and ideas moved between India, Iran, the Turkic steppe, and China — but their specific role as religious and cultural brokers has been systematically underestimated in Buddhist transmission histories more attentive to institutional and textual lineages than to the commercial and social networks through which those lineages moved.

The Sogdians were not merely Buddhist in their religious formation — they maintained simultaneously Zoroastrian ancestral community religion, Manichaean transregional commercial religion, and Buddhist trade-partner religion, all operating within the same community and sometimes the same individual. This genuine religious plurality gave Sogdian intermediaries three features whose compound effect on what they transmitted is recoverable through careful analysis. Zoroastrian ethical vocabulary shaped how they rendered Buddhist moral categories, introducing *aṣ̌a*-inflected ethical tonality into Buddhist dharma teaching. Manichaean light metaphysics provided a rich lexical field for describing luminosity applied to Buddhist descriptions of enlightened awareness. And merchant pragmatism oriented the transmitted tradition toward practical efficacy — protective dhāraṇī, narrative moral instruction, merit-making practices — at the expense of the systematic philosophical analysis that required extended training to appreciate.

The Sogdian Buddhist manuscripts preserved at Dunhuang and Turfan — including translations of the *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa*, portions of the *Aparimitāyuḥ Sūtra*, and substantial dhāraṇī collections — constitute a distinct Sogdian-language Buddhist literary tradition with its own translation conventions and its own philosophical emphases, showing that Buddhist practice in Sogdian was not merely a peripheral phenomenon but a genuine literary tradition. Several Old Uyghur Buddhist texts derive from Sogdian intermediary translations rather than directly from Chinese or Sanskrit, meaning the Uyghur reception was sometimes double-filtered: Sanskrit → Sogdian → Old Uyghur.

The theoretical implication extends beyond the specific case: if the Sogdian broker layer introduced systematic transformations into what it transmitted, then every Buddhist community that received its Buddhism through Sogdian mediation has a Sogdian filter embedded in its tradition, whether or not that filter is visible in the tradition’s self-understanding.

### 4.4 Conversion Buddhism: The Uyghur Case as Theoretical Model

The Uyghur Buddhist synthesis at Turfan represents the most theoretically productive case study available for understanding how a community converts from one sophisticated philosophical tradition to another. The case is theoretically productive precisely because of what makes it unusual: the converting community brought to its encounter with Buddhism not a relatively diffuse folk religious background but a highly articulated, textually sophisticated, institutionally established religious tradition in Manichaeism. The Manichaean prior formation was not a blank slate onto which Buddhism could be written but a complex cognitive and cultural framework whose specific features determined, in systematic and recoverable ways, which aspects of Buddhism felt immediately compelling, which felt puzzling, and which felt actively threatening to commitments too deep to be readily identified as commitments.

The theoretical claim this case supports is that religious conversion does not erase prior formation but transforms it into an interpretive lens through which the new tradition is received. This lens operates primarily through *differential felt resonance* — the pre-reflective experience of certain aspects of the new tradition as immediately and obviously true, and of other aspects as puzzling or subtly wrong — at a level below explicit doctrinal decision-making.

In the Uyghur case, the *tathāgatagarbha* doctrine’s prominence in the Old Uyghur Buddhist corpus at the expense of Madhyamaka philosophical analysis reflects this mechanism precisely. The teaching that every sentient being possesses buddha-nature as a luminous, originally pure nature obscured by adventitious defilements is structurally close enough to the Manichaean understanding of the light-soul imprisoned in material darkness to activate the prior formation’s deepest intuitions. Madhyamaka’s radical deconstruction of all substantial entities — including any putative inner divine essence — was structurally threatening to these same intuitions, producing a gravitational avoidance that left the Old Uyghur corpus philosophically thinner than the Chinese or Tibetan canonical traditions in its engagement with emptiness philosophy.

The Uyghur case illuminates a general principle: what any community constructs in religious conversion is not the new tradition but a synthesis that carries the prior formation’s deepest commitments encoded in the new tradition’s surface vocabulary. The synthesis is genuine — the Uyghur Buddhist community was Buddhist, not crypto-Manichaean — but it is a Buddhism shaped in systematic and recoverable ways by what came before it.

### 4.5 Civilisational Fragility: The Khotan Collapse as Case Study

The fall of Khotan to the Qarakhanid Khanate around 1006 CE represents a moment of historical rupture whose significance for understanding the conditions that sustain or destroy Buddhist civilisations has not been fully absorbed by the field. The speed and completeness of the collapse — nine centuries of uninterrupted Buddhist civilisation effectively destroyed within a generation — constitutes a natural experiment in civilisational fragility whose results bear directly on the questions about institutional conditions for Buddhist transmission that this review’s comparative analysis has been developing.

The institutional ecology that Khotanese Buddhism depended on was characterised by three features whose absence proved fatal. Royal patronage was the primary and essentially exclusive source of institutional support — producing extraordinary cultural depth but no economic resilience when patronage was withdrawn. The theological embedding of Buddhist institutional life in the specific political order of the Khotanese royal dynasty tied the tradition’s deepest self-understanding to a specific political arrangement, leaving it without a portable version of itself when that arrangement was replaced. And the absence of a practice lineage tradition capable of sustaining itself through periods of institutional disruption meant that when the monasteries were destroyed, there was no lineage of initiated practitioners capable of sustaining the tradition’s essential content outside the institutional context.

The connection between the Khotanese collapse and the Dunhuang cave sealing is one of the most historically significant links in the Silk Road Buddhist transmission story. The cave was sealed, by the most careful current dating, between 1002 and 1006 CE — the same years in which the Qarakhanid conquest of Khotan was occurring. Rong Xinjiang’s analysis of the cave’s contents has identified a group of Khotanese-language manuscripts that appear to have been deposited as a discrete archive — suggesting a deliberate act of preservation by Khotanese refugees who arrived at Dunhuang carrying their community’s textual heritage. Reading the cave sealing in this context transforms its significance from a local military precaution into a regional Buddhist crisis response: the Dunhuang community was preserving a portion of a Buddhist world that was actively being destroyed, recognising that Buddhist civilisation was fragile in ways that required active preservation rather than confident assumption of institutional continuity.

## Section 5: The Tibetan Laboratory — Where the Complete Transmission Landed

### 5.1 Why Tibet Succeeded: Four Structural Conditions

The question of why Tibet received the complete Anuttarayoga transmission when every other Buddhist culture examined by this review did not has a complex answer involving four structural conditions whose simultaneous presence created the institutional ecology within which the complete transmission could not only arrive but take root, develop, and eventually produce the most philosophically sophisticated and institutionally durable Buddhist tradition in the world.

Royal political will as sustained civilisational project distinguishes the Tibetan reception from any mere patronage relationship. King Trisong Detsen’s invitation to Śāntarakṣita and Padmasambhava, his construction of Samye monastery as the first institution designed to sustain the full range of Buddhist practice on Tibetan soil, and his convening of the Samye debate to establish the intellectual framework — these were acts of civilisational construction, not religious patronage. This civilisational ambition had a specific consequence: it created the institutional conditions for receiving the full Anuttarayoga transmission rather than a selected portion. The transgressive elements of Anuttarayoga, which the Tang court context made untransmissible, were in the Tibetan civilisational project context not merely permissible but necessary — precisely because their power and their philosophical depth were part of what made Buddhism worth building a civilisation around.

Geographical proximity to living Indian sources meant that Tibetan translator-monks could make the journey to the places where the Anuttarayoga tradition was alive in its most developed form. Marpa made three journeys to India. Drokmi Lotsawa studied at Vikramaśīla. Rinchen Zangpo made multiple extended study visits to Kashmir. These individuals were going as students, spending years in sustained study with specific teachers, receiving initiations that required extended personal relationship to transmit, and returning with living lineages rather than merely authorised textual translations.

The second diffusion’s timing coincided with the moment of the Anuttarayoga tradition’s greatest systematic completeness, before the destruction of the Indian monastic infrastructure closed the transmission channels forever. Tibet received the finished product of Indian Buddhist philosophical development, not a work in progress.

Finally, Tibet’s indigenous religious culture — the pre-Buddhist Bön tradition and more diffuse shamanic practices — maintained an understanding of the boundary between human practitioners and non-human powers as highly permeable and the practitioner’s capacity to engage with those powers as one of the most important qualifications of a religious specialist. This made the wrathful deity iconography, the charnel-ground symbolism, and the shamanic elements of Anuttarayoga culturally legible in ways they were not in Tang China. Padmasambhava’s legendary subjugation of indigenous spirits is the mythological encoding of this structural compatibility — the Anuttarayoga transformation principle applied at the civilisational level.

-----

### 5.2 The Nyingma School: First Diffusion, Mahāyoga, and Dzogchen

The Nyingma school (རྙིང་མ་, *rnying ma*, “Ancient School”) represents the Tibetan tradition’s most direct institutional connection to the first diffusion and to Padmasambhava’s legendary transmission. Its nine-vehicle classification — placing Dzogchen (རྫོགས་ཆེན་, “Great Perfection”) at the apex of a hierarchy that includes the standard Buddhist yāna classification and three higher tantra classes (Mahāyoga, Anuyoga, and Atiyoga/Dzogchen) — is itself a philosophical statement rather than merely a taxonomical convenience, encoding the claim that what the generation stage constructs and the completion stage dissolves, Dzogchen simply recognises as always already present.

The *terma* (གཏེར་མ་, treasure text) tradition — the system of teachings attributed to Padmasambhava’s concealment for future discovery by destined *tertön* (གཏེར་སྟོན་, treasure-revealers) — represents a sophisticated theory of delayed transmission built into the tradition’s self-understanding. The *terma* theory is not merely a mechanism for introducing new teachings while maintaining the authority of Padmasambhava as their ultimate source. It implicitly challenges the standard model of transmission as a linear sequence by proposing that transmission can occur across centuries, calibrated to future rather than present conditions — a Buddhist theory of institutional resilience that responds directly to the civilisational fragility demonstrated by the Khotan collapse.

Dzogchen’s central claim — that the nature of mind (*rigpa*, རིག་པ་, pure awareness) is primordially pure, luminously aware, and already perfectly complete, and that liberation consists in the direct recognition of this always-already-present nature — creates a structurally significant tension with both the Anuttarayoga generation and completion stages that precede it and with the Gelug tradition’s insistence on *prāsaṅgika* Madhyamaka as the necessary philosophical ground for any genuine recognition. Tsongkhapa and his successors argued that what Dzogchen practitioners might experience as the recognition of *rigpa* could actually be a subtle form of *ālayavijñāna* — a relatively clear state of consciousness that feels like the nature of mind but has not yet been purified of its most subtle defilements. The Nyingma response, developed most fully by Mipham Rinpoche (1846–1912), is that *rigpa* is not a state among states but the nature of all states — the unconditioned nature of all experience rather than a particular meditative condition. This exchange represents one of the most sophisticated internal philosophical debates in Tibetan Buddhist history, and it remains unresolved in the sense that both positions continue to be defended by sophisticated practitioners.

-----

### 5.3 The Kagyü School: Mahāmudrā and the Six Yogas of Nāropā

The Kagyü school (བཀའ་བརྒྱུད་, *bka’ brgyud*, “transmitted word lineage”) traces its origin through a transmission whose lineage narrative encodes a specific understanding of how the Anuttarayoga path works: from the primordial buddha Vajradhara through the Indian mahāsiddhas Tilopa and Nāropā, to the Tibetan translator Marpa and his student Milarepa, and thence to Gampopa who institutionalised the school’s teachings.

The Six Yogas of Nāropā (ན་རོ་ཆོས་དྲུག་, *nā ro chos drug*) constitute the completion-stage physiological technology that is the Kagyü transmission’s most distinctive practical contribution. The six yogas — *tummo* (གཏུམ་མོ་, fierce/candālī heat), *gyulü* (སྒྱུ་ལུས་, illusory body), *ösel* (འོད་གསལ་, clear light), *milam* (རྨི་ལམ་, dream yoga), *bardo* (བར་དོ་, intermediate state practice), and *phowa* (འཕོ་བ་, consciousness transference) — systematically target the transitional states of consciousness as sites of practice opportunity: the moments of falling asleep, recognising one is dreaming, and dying, when ordinary habitual patterns are suspended and the mind’s natural luminosity has the opportunity to be recognised without the habitual overlay.

The Mahāmudrā (ཕྱག་རྒྱ་ཆེན་པོ་, “Great Seal”) teaching represents the Kagyü tradition’s most direct pointing at the nature of mind. Its relationship to the Six Yogas reflects a broader tension within the Anuttarayoga tradition between physiological and non-physiological approaches to the completion stage’s recognition goal. Some Kagyü masters have taught Mahāmudrā as a standalone path accessible without the Six Yogas for practitioners of sufficient capacity. Others have insisted on the Six Yogas as necessary prerequisites for the stability of Mahāmudrā recognition. Gampopa’s institutionalisation of the Kagyü teaching involved integrating Mahāmudrā with the *lam rim* framework that Atiśa’s tradition had developed — a pragmatic resolution that represents the Kagyü tradition’s accommodation of the direct recognition ideal to the institutional requirements of transmitting that recognition to large numbers of students.

-----

### 5.4 Tsongkhapa and the Gelug Systematisation: Madhyamaka Grounds Anuttarayoga

Tsongkhapa Losang Drakpa (རྗེ་ཙོང་ཁ་པ་བློ་བཟང་གྲགས་པ་, 1357–1419) is the figure in Tibetan Buddhist intellectual history whose work most fully exemplifies the tradition’s capacity for rigorous systematic philosophy, and whose specific contribution to the Anuttarayoga tradition is as important as it is frequently misunderstood. His reforming activity was a response to what he perceived as actual ethical deterioration in certain sectors of the 14th-century Tibetan Buddhist world, where the antinomian interpretation of emptiness was being used to justify behaviour he regarded as the most dangerous form of Buddhist corruption.

Tsongkhapa’s solution is philosophically precise rather than merely morally conservative. His argument is not that the Anuttarayoga tradition’s central philosophical claims are wrong but that they have been widely misunderstood in ways that produce antinomian misapplication, and that the correct understanding requires the *prāsaṅgika* Madhyamaka interpretation of emptiness as articulated by Candrakīrti as its philosophical foundation. The key move that connects this foundation to the Anuttarayoga completion stage is his analysis of what bliss-emptiness inseparability (*bde stong dbyer med*) actually means: not the recognition of emptiness as an abstract philosophical principle, achievable through analytical meditation alone, but the recognition of emptiness as the nature of bliss — the recognition that the bliss itself, the most powerful positive experience the human body-mind system can generate, is empty of the inherent existence that ordinary cognitive habit attributes to positive experiences.

This analysis has two important consequences. First, it establishes that the completion stage’s recognition requires as its prerequisite a genuine analytical recognition of emptiness achieved through the sutra-vehicle path — effectively ruling out the antinomian misuse by establishing that a practitioner who has genuinely achieved *prāsaṅgika* realisation understands that conventional ethical categories remain valid precisely because they are conventional designations rather than inherently existent facts. Second, it establishes a criterion for distinguishing genuine completion-stage recognition from its imitation. The *Ngagrim Chenmo* (སྔགས་རིམ་ཆེན་མོ་, “Great Treatise on the Stages of Mantra”) in which Tsongkhapa develops this analysis most fully remains among the least studied of his major works in Western scholarship — a gap that represents one of the most important unfinished tasks in the field.

### 5.5 The *Kālacakra Tantra*: Isomorphism as Soteriological Method

The *Kālacakra Tantra* (དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་, *Dus kyi ’khor lo*, “Tantra of the Wheel of Time”) occupies a position in the Tibetan Buddhist canonical tradition that is simultaneously unique and representative — unique because of its extraordinary philosophical ambition and encyclopaedic scope, representative because it brings to their most explicit and most systematic articulation the principles that run through the Anuttarayoga tradition as a whole. Composed in approximately the 10th century CE and brought to Tibet by Atiśa in 1042, it represents the culmination of Indian Buddhist philosophical development, the attempt to articulate a comprehensive account of reality that integrates cosmology, physiology, astronomy, embryology, linguistics, and soteriology into a single isomorphic structure.

The *Kālacakra*’s philosophical foundation is the principle of tripartite isomorphism: the claim that the outer cosmos (ཕྱི་རོལ་གྱི་དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་), the inner body (ནང་གི་དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་), and the alternative awakening system (གཞན་གྱི་དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་) are not merely parallel in structure but structurally identical in ways that have practical consequences. The isomorphism is not metaphorical but mathematical: the 21,600 breaths a healthy human takes per day correspond to the 21,600 minutes in a 360-day year; the 12 months of the year correspond to the 12 major channel nodes in the subtle body; the 7 planets of Indian astronomy correspond to the 7 *cakras*. These correspondences are structural identities whose practical implication is that working with any one level simultaneously affects all three.

The *Kālacakra*’s astronomical system — its detailed calculations of planetary positions, eclipse cycles, and the precessional movement of the equinoxes — is not peripheral but necessary: if the isomorphism between cosmic rhythms and subtle-body physiology is literal, then accurate astronomical knowledge is soteriologically required. This makes the *Kālacakra* unique among Buddhist texts in treating empirical accuracy about the physical world as a soteriological requirement.

The Shambhala narrative — the *Kālacakra*‘s eschatological account of a future war between the forces of *dharma* and *adharma*, culminating in the emergence of the twenty-fifth *rigden* king Rudra Cakrin — should be understood simultaneously as a meditation instruction, a political theology, and a comparative religious polemic. As a meditation instruction, Shambhala is not only a geographical location but an internal state: the practitioner who realises the alternative Kālacakra has found Shambhala within. As a political theology, its deployments from Mongolian imperial ideology to contemporary Tibetan resistance to Chinese rule demonstrate its historical potency. As a comparative religious polemic, the text’s explicit naming of the Islamic *shahāda* as the identifying marker of the eschatological adversary places it firmly in the context of Islamic expansion across Central Asia that had, by the late 10th century CE, destroyed the Buddhist civilisations of Bactria and Sogdiana and was threatening the Buddhist institutions of northern India and the Silk Road.

## Section 6: The Epistemological Reckoning — Does the Path Achieve What It Claims?

### 6.1 Stratifying the Claims: Four Levels of Assertion

Any serious philosophical engagement with tantric Buddhism’s soteriological claims must begin by resisting the temptation to treat those claims as a single, uniformly evaluable proposition. The question “does the tantric path achieve what it claims?” is not one question but at least four, arranged in ascending order of philosophical ambition and descending order of verifiability.

Level 1 — *practical efficacy claims* — concerns specific physiological and psychological results that specific practices produce. The evidence at this level is substantially positive. Herbert Benson’s research documented tummo practitioners generating core body temperature increases beyond the range of ordinary voluntary thermoregulation. Richard Davidson and Antoine Lutz’s neuroimaging studies documented dramatically elevated gamma-band neural oscillation in experienced meditators — oscillation patterns qualitatively different from untrained controls in their spatial distribution and temporal stability. The qualification “substantially, though not uniformly, positive” is important: the research record includes null results and mixed findings, and methodological limitations are significant. What Level 1 evidence establishes is that the tradition’s claims about specific physiological and psychological effects are not simply false and that the practices are more sophisticated and more effective than the colonial reception’s “primitive superstition” characterisation allows.

Level 2 — *psychological transformation claims* — concerns lasting changes in the practitioner’s psychological structure. The evidence here is genuinely mixed. There is robust evidence that sustained meditative practice produces measurable changes in attention regulation and emotional reactivity. There is considerably weaker evidence for the tradition’s strongest claims — permanent elimination of specific afflictive emotions, complete dissolution of self-grasping. The documented cases of highly regarded Tibetan masters who displayed recognisable afflictive emotions raise legitimate questions about whether the claimed transformation is rarer than the tradition’s institutional culture acknowledges, achieved at a level less complete than the philosophical accounts describe, or understood by practitioners in ways not captured by external behavioural observation.

Level 3 — *soteriological claims* — concerns liberation from saṃsāric rebirth and the achievement of buddhahood. These claims are not empirically falsifiable in any straightforward sense. What can be said is that they are philosophically coherent given the tradition’s philosophical premises, and that the coherence of a sophisticated philosophical system with well-developed internal standards of rigour is itself a form of evidence for the claims’ reliability — though not decisive evidence. The testimony of those who claim to have achieved the relevant states, embedded in a broader framework of intellectual and ethical credibility, constitutes a further form of evidence that the tradition’s epistemological framework treats as valid.

Level 4 — *metaphysical identity claims* — represents the most ambitious assertions: that the practitioner who achieves buddhahood is, in some genuine sense, identical with the cosmic buddha, that individual and cosmos are non-dual, that the practitioner’s awareness and Mahāvairocana’s awareness are not two things but one. The Madhyamaka-level defence — that neither the practitioner nor the cosmic buddha is an independently existing entity, and that their apparent separateness is a conventional designation rather than an ultimate fact — is philosophically coherent and does not require special pleading. The *tathāgatagarbha*-level defence is more philosophically demanding and more contested within the tradition itself, as the Gelug-Nyingma-Jonang debates demonstrate.

-----

### 6.2 The Verification Problem: Participant-Dependent Epistemic Access

The tradition’s standard response to the question of how its Level 3 and Level 4 claims can be assessed is epistemologically sophisticated and deserves to be taken seriously as a philosophical position. The response is that the relevant phenomena — the nature of bliss-emptiness recognition in the completion stage, the quality of Mahāmudrā or *rigpa* recognition, the character of buddhahood as the path’s culmination — are not accessible to ordinary, untrained awareness, and that the question of whether the path achieves what it claims can therefore only be adequately addressed from within the path. This is the position of *participant-dependent epistemic access*: certain forms of knowledge are available only to practitioners who have developed the specific cognitive and experiential capacities that the relevant phenomena require for their recognition.

The philosophical tradition of epistemology provides several frameworks for thinking about participant-dependent epistemic access. The most directly relevant is the tradition of *experiential knowledge* or *knowledge by acquaintance* — the distinction between knowledge about something and knowledge of something, illustrated most vividly by Frank Jackson’s thought-experiment about the colour-blind neuroscientist Mary. The tantric tradition’s claim is structurally analogous: not that outsiders lack access to specific pieces of information but that they lack the form of acquaintance with the relevant phenomena that only direct experience of the path can provide.

The philosophical strength of the participant-dependent epistemic access position is that it is not special pleading — it is a feature of many domains of genuine knowledge that relevant phenomena are accessible only through forms of engagement that cannot be fully replaced by propositional description. The philosophical limitation is equally genuine: participant-dependent epistemic access creates a structural closure to external scrutiny that, if taken without qualification, makes the tradition’s claims in principle immune to disconfirmation. This immunity, while not conclusively showing the claims are false, is a significant epistemological liability that the tradition must address by providing some account of how genuine participant access can be distinguished from mistaken participant access.

The category error objection — that asking whether the tantric path achieves what it claims deploys a subject-object epistemological framework that the path claims to dissolve, making the question structurally self-undermining — has genuine philosophical force. It corresponds to a real insight about the limits of propositional and third-person inquiry when the subject matter is the structure of subjectivity itself. Heidegger’s critique of traditional epistemology, Wittgenstein’s demonstration of the limits of propositional language, and Nagel’s argument about the irreducibility of first-person perspective all make structurally related points. But the category error objection is also self-limiting in a way the tradition must acknowledge: if the path’s result is genuinely beyond subject-object knowing, then the tradition’s own positive descriptions of that result are themselves produced from within the framework they claim to transcend. The most intellectually honest position — the one that the best accounts within the tradition actually take — is that the descriptions are approximations, conventions that point toward something they cannot fully capture.

-----

### 6.3 The Honesty Problem: What the Historical Record Shows

The most philosophically uncomfortable dimension of any honest assessment is what the tradition’s own historical record shows — the gap between the extraordinary claims the tradition makes and the institutional behaviour the historical record documents.

On one side, the institutional record documents individuals of remarkable ethical, intellectual, and contemplative achievement. Milarepa’s documented transformation from a practitioner of destructive black magic to a poet-saint whose songs are among the most extraordinary spiritual literature in any language is the tradition’s canonical example of radical psychological transformation. Tsongkhapa’s intellectual output represents a level of sustained philosophical achievement difficult to explain by ordinary cognitive means. These figures provide genuine, if indirect, evidence of psychological transformation of unusual depth.

On the other side, the institutional record documents patterns of behaviour by figures presented as highly realised masters that are not easily reconcilable with the tradition’s claims. The abuse scandals in contemporary Tibetan Buddhist communities in Western contexts — cases involving figures presented as *tulkus* and recognised masters whose systematic exploitation of students constitutes demonstrable harm — are not isolated anomalies but a pattern sufficiently widespread to raise genuine questions about the gap between the tradition’s claims and its practice.

The tradition’s responses to these cases — the *crazy wisdom* defence and the “not genuinely realised” response — both reveal the same underlying problem: the tradition lacks adequate mechanisms for the distinction between genuine realisation and convincing performance. This insufficiency is not merely a contingent institutional failure but is connected to structural features of the tradition’s epistemological framework — the participant-dependent epistemic access that protects genuine recognition from external disconfirmation also protects sophisticated performance from the same disconfirmation.

The most philosophically honest conclusion is this: the tantric tradition demonstrably achieves significant results at Levels 1 and 2. At Level 3, the claims are philosophically coherent but structurally unverifiable from outside. At Level 4, the honest assessment is that the claims are at the frontier of what philosophical analysis can reach, and that uncertainty is the intellectually appropriate response — not the uncertainty of suspension between equally weighted options but the uncertainty of genuine philosophical difficulty in which the question is clearer than any available answer. The tradition points at something real and important. Whether what it points at is exactly what it says it is — that is the question that each practitioner, across the full course of a committed life, is in the end the only one positioned to approach.

## Section 7: Colonial and Modern Reception — The Making of Buddhist Modernity

### 7.1 The Colonial Construction of “Original Buddhism”

The story of how European and North American scholarship constructed “original Buddhism” is not primarily a story about intellectual error but about how entire intellectual programmes, when embedded in specific historical and institutional contexts, generate systematic distortions that are invisible from within those programmes because the distortions are constitutive of the framework within which the inquiry proceeds.

European encounter with Buddhist texts began seriously in the early nineteenth century with scholars trained in the comparative philology developed primarily for the study of ancient Greek, Latin, and Sanskrit texts. The primary assumption was that the most valuable form of a text was its earliest recoverable form, that subsequent development represented elaboration and distortion of an original pure message. Applied to Buddhist texts, this generated a research programme with a specific institutional shape: the recovery of the earliest Buddhist literary tradition — understood as the Pāli Canon — and the identification of the “original teaching” of the historical Śākyamuni within that canon. The Pāli Text Society, founded in 1881 by T.W. Rhys Davids, undertook the systematic editing and translation of the Pāli Canon and thereby established it as the canonical reference point for all subsequent Western Buddhist scholarship, against which everything else — the Sanskrit Mahāyāna sūtras, the Tibetan Anuttarayoga corpus, the Chinese esoteric tradition — was measured and found secondary or degenerate.

The political function of this scholarly programme in the colonial context served multiple colonial interests simultaneously. For Christian missionaries, it provided a version of Buddhism contrasted unfavourably with Christianity on the grounds that it lacked a personal God, while simultaneously being contrasted favourably with the “degenerate” popular Buddhism missionaries encountered, supporting the claim that Asian populations needed cultural reformation. For colonial administrators, it positioned Western scholarship as the arbiter of what authentic Buddhism was. For Romantic intellectuals, it provided a Buddhism purified of institutional “superstitions” — a Buddhism of individual rational inquiry, noble resignation, and philosophical sophistication compatible with Enlightenment values.

Gregory Schopen’s archaeological and epigraphic scholarship demonstrated that the Buddhism practised in early India — as evidenced by material culture — was dramatically different from the Buddhism described in the canonical texts. The early Indian Buddhists that the archaeological evidence reveals were preoccupied with relic veneration, image worship, merit acquisition through donations, invocation of protective powers through ritual, and endowment of monasteries with productive economic property — preoccupations that the canonical literature largely ignores. The canonical literature, Schopen argued, was not describing what Buddhists did but constructing a normative ideal, and treating this normative construction as historical description had fundamentally misled the field for a century.

-----

### 7.2 Buddhist Modernism: Accepting the Colonial Framework to Defeat It

Buddhist modernism — the reformulation of Buddhist teaching and practice by Asian intellectuals in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries in response to colonial critique — adopted a specific intellectual strategy: meeting the colonial critique on its own epistemological ground. Where the colonial framework said ritual was inferior to philosophy, Buddhist modernism agreed but argued authentic Buddhism was at its core philosophy. Where the colonial framework said individual rational inquiry was superior to institutional tradition, Buddhist modernism agreed but argued Buddhism exemplified such inquiry. In each case the move was not resistance to the colonial framework but appropriation of it — taking the colonial criteria and demonstrating that Buddhism, properly understood, met them better than its colonial critics had claimed.

Anagarika Dharmapala (1864–1933) in Ceylon developed his Buddhist reform movement by explicitly distinguishing the “pure” Buddhism of the Pāli canon from the “corrupt” popular Buddhism of the Sinhalese village. His programme combined anti-colonial political rhetoric with an internal critique of Buddhist institutions that mirrored, sometimes almost verbatim, the critiques that Protestant missionaries had been making for decades. The anti-colonial nationalism and the Protestant-inflected internal critique were structurally complementary rather than contradictory: both premised on the same distinction between authentic and inauthentic Buddhism, with Dharmapala claiming that the authentic version was recoverable through return to the Pāli texts — exactly as the colonial philologists had argued, except that the recovery was for the Sinhalese rather than for the Europeans.

D.T. Suzuki (1870–1966) pursued the modernist strategy with a specifically philosophical focus that gave his work influence in Western intellectual culture far exceeding any Buddhist modernist contemporary. His presentation of Zen Buddhism — primarily through his *Essays in Zen Buddhism* — established the image of Buddhism as primarily concerned with direct, non-conceptual, unmediated experience of reality. This characterisation was shaped by the neo-Kantian idealism of his early philosophical education, by comparative religion frameworks that had established “religious experience” as the primary datum of religious study, and by the Protestant theological tradition that located the core of religious life in the individual’s interior encounter with ultimate reality. His many volumes on Zen contain almost no discussion of Zen monasticism’s actual daily ritual life — the liturgical services, the communal meals, the elaborate systems of bowing, the relationship between Zen practice and Japanese cultural and social order. The Zen that resulted was not invented — the kōan interview and direct pointing at mind’s nature are genuine features of the Rinzai tradition — but it was radically selective in ways that produced a Buddhism suited to a Western intellectual audience precisely by removing elements that audience found most alien.

The Fourteenth Dalai Lama’s public teaching represents a third model — more philosophically sophisticated than either Dharmapala’s revivalism or Suzuki’s aestheticism, more alert to the costs as well as the benefits of the modernist strategy. His characteristic approach presents Madhyamaka philosophical analysis as the intellectual core of Buddhism, compassion as its ethical core, and tantrism as a technically advanced system grounded in and inseparable from the preceding philosophical and ethical foundation. This three-move structure is rhetorically effective and in certain respects philosophically honest — the insistence on prerequisites for tantric practice is a genuine element of the Tibetan tradition’s self-understanding. But it positions the philosophical and ethical dimensions as what Buddhism “really is” and the tantric ritual system as a sophisticated technique in its service, rather than understanding the relationship as the non-dual integration that the tradition’s most sophisticated internal accounts describe.

-----

### 7.3 The Ritual-Philosophy Integration: What the Best of the Tradition Actually Offers

Having traced the colonial construction of original Buddhism and the modernist response to it, this review is now in a position to articulate what is obscured by both: the relationship between ritual practice and philosophical understanding as the tantric tradition’s most sophisticated accounts describe it. This is not the relationship that the colonial framework assumed — ritual as primitive, philosophy as advanced, the two standing in a developmental hierarchy. Nor is it the relationship that Buddhist modernism proposed — philosophy as the tradition’s essential core, ritual as a culturally conditioned vehicle for philosophical insight that can in principle be replaced by other vehicles without loss. It is a more complex relationship in which the two dimensions are mutually constitutive: each requires the other to be fully itself, and the tradition achieves its most important insights and its most powerful effects precisely at the point where the two are most fully integrated.

The clearest account of this integration is Kūkai’s formulation of the Three Mysteries practice in relation to the Two Maṇḍala philosophy. The Three Mysteries — the practitioner’s body enacting the deity’s mudrā, the practitioner’s speech reciting the deity’s mantra, the practitioner’s mind visualising the deity’s form — are not techniques that produce the philosophical recognition of *ri-chi* non-duality as their result. They are the philosophical recognition in embodied form: the non-duality of the Womb Realm’s principle and the Diamond Realm’s wisdom is a reality that the ritual enacts — that is present in and as the ritual performance when the practitioner’s three mysteries are genuinely aligned with the Buddha’s three mysteries — and whose propositional articulation in the Two Maṇḍala philosophy is an approximation of the enactment rather than its foundation.

Tsongkhapa’s integration of *prāsaṅgika* Madhyamaka with the Anuttarayoga completion stage provides the Tibetan tradition’s most rigorous account of what this integration means in practice. The bliss of the completion stage, when the practitioner has established the *prāsaṅgika* realisation through prior analytical meditation, is the recognition of emptiness in its most powerful and most direct available form: emptiness recognised not through the medium of conceptual analysis but through and within the most intense positive experience the human body-mind system can generate. Without the analytical foundation, the bliss is just bliss, a pleasant meditative state whose philosophical significance the practitioner cannot recognise. Without the bliss, the analytical recognition of emptiness is correct but not transformative in the deepest sense — it is emptiness understood rather than emptiness lived. The mutual dependence is not a deficiency of either dimension but the point: the tradition claims that liberation requires both the intellectual recognition of how things are and the experiential living of that recognition in the body’s most powerful energetic register simultaneously.

The recovery of this integration — the recognition that the tantric tradition’s most important contribution is precisely the claim that philosophical understanding and somatic practice are mutually constitutive — is the central intellectual task for any serious contemporary engagement with the tradition. It requires rejecting both the colonial framework’s evaluation of ritual as primitive and the modernist framework’s domestication of ritual as psychologically efficient philosophy, and developing instead a philosophical framework adequate to the tradition’s actual claims: one in which the body is not merely the subject of philosophical analysis but the medium of philosophical realisation, and in which ritual practice is not the illustration of philosophical insight but its most complete available expression.

### 7.4 The Mindfulness Movement as Buddhist Modernism’s Terminus

The secular mindfulness movement — the extraction of Buddhist meditative techniques from their doctrinal, institutional, and ethical context and their reframing as secular psychological technology — represents the logical conclusion of the trajectory that Buddhist modernism initiated. If the colonial framework established that philosophy was more legitimate than ritual, and if Buddhist modernism responded by identifying philosophy as Buddhism’s essential core while retaining ritual as a vehicle for philosophical insight, then the secular mindfulness movement completes the trajectory by abandoning the philosophical core as well and retaining only the technique — the meditative practice stripped of its Buddhist doctrinal content, its institutional affiliations, its ethical framework, and its soteriological ambition.

The movement’s founding moment is conventionally located in Jon Kabat-Zinn’s development of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) at the University of Massachusetts Medical School in 1979, where he adapted vipassanā and other meditative techniques for clinical application with chronic pain patients, deliberately removing the Buddhist institutional and doctrinal context in order to make the techniques acceptable in a secular medical setting. The decision was not made in ignorance of the Buddhist context — Kabat-Zinn was a committed practitioner with extensive training in multiple Buddhist traditions — but in the assessment that the techniques’ beneficial effects were separable from their doctrinal and institutional context.

The philosophical problem with the secular mindfulness movement is not that it is ineffective — the Level 1 evidence for its effectiveness within its own defined parameters is reasonably strong — but that it achieves its effectiveness by dismantling precisely the framework within which the techniques it uses have the meaning and the efficacy that the Buddhist tradition attributes to them. The mindfulness that MBSR teaches is not the same thing as the *sati* (Pāli) or *smṛti* (Sanskrit) that the Buddhist tradition calls mindfulness, though they share a name in English. Buddhist mindfulness is a specific quality of awareness cultivated in the context of a path whose goal is liberation from suffering through the recognition of impermanence, suffering, and non-self — a goal that gives the practice its direction, its ethical context, and its ultimate significance. Secular mindfulness is a specific quality of attention cultivated in the context of a therapeutic intervention whose goal is symptom reduction and wellbeing improvement.

The economic dimensions of the secular mindfulness movement constitute a further philosophical problem. The $4 billion American mindfulness industry represents a commodification of Buddhist practice that the tradition’s own analysis of suffering should recognise as philosophically significant. The Buddha’s diagnosis of suffering’s root cause — *taṇhā*, craving or thirst, the habitual grasping after pleasant experience and aversion from unpleasant experience — applies with full force to the consumer relationship that the mindfulness industry cultivates with its products. A practice designed to dissolve the habitual patterns of craving and aversion has been repackaged as a product that consumers purchase in order to increase pleasant experience and decrease unpleasant experience — reproducing at the level of the market relationship precisely the pattern of grasping that the practice is supposed to dissolve.

The most philosophically honest conclusion is not that the secular mindfulness movement should adopt Buddhist doctrinal commitments — that would ignore the genuine value of making beneficial practices accessible to populations who would not otherwise encounter them. It is that the movement should be honest about what it is: a set of attentional training techniques derived from but no longer connected to the Buddhist tradition, capable of producing specific and valuable psychological effects within a limited therapeutic context, but neither claiming nor capable of delivering what the tradition from which those techniques derived claims the practices in their full context can achieve.

## Section 8: Conclusion — What the Comparative History Reveals

### 8.1 The Broker’s Cut: A General Theory of Buddhist Transmission

The seven sections of this review have traced the movement of tantric Buddhism across fifteen centuries and at least five major cultural transformations, producing not merely a more detailed account of a transmission history the field already understood in outline but a general theory of how Buddhist transmission works — a theory whose implications extend beyond the specific case of tantric Buddhism to the study of religious transmission across cultural boundaries wherever it occurs.

The theory’s central claim is that Buddhist transmission is never a transfer of content from one location to another but always a transformation of content through the specific human, institutional, and cultural mediations through which it passes. This claim has been made in various forms by scholars of religion and Buddhist studies. What this review adds is a more precise account of the *mechanisms* through which transformation occurs and a more rigorous account of what those mechanisms imply for how the resulting tradition should be understood and evaluated.

The four mechanisms identified across the cases examined each operate at a distinct level with its own characteristic mode of influence. The *chronological mechanism* shapes what is available to transmit before any human decision about what to transmit is made — the Anuttarayoga corpus’s developmental chronology was primary in determining what the Tang Chinese transmission could and could not include. The *institutional mechanism* determines which aspects of the transmitted tradition the receiving culture’s political theology, patronage structures, and philosophical frameworks can accommodate — the *hùguó* political theology of Tang court esotericism shaped Amoghavajra’s curatorial choices as powerfully as any philosophical judgment. The *cognitive mechanism* operates through the prior religious formation of converting communities, shaping reception through pre-reflective differential felt resonance rather than explicit doctrinal decision — the Manichaean prior formation produced the Uyghur Buddhist corpus’s systematic emphases in ways that operated below the level of conscious choice. The *broker mechanism* introduces transformation through the specific features of intermediary communities’ own formation — the Sogdian merchant diaspora’s Zoroastrian ethical vocabulary, Manichaean light metaphysics, and commercial pragmatism constituted a third filtration layer between Indian sources and Uyghur reception whose traces are recoverable through careful vocabulary analysis and textual genealogy.

These four mechanisms interact in ways that produce the complex, layered, always partially transformed traditions that the comparative history reveals. The theoretical implication is direct and important: no received tradition is a deficient version of the originating tradition, and no tradition should be evaluated by the standard of proximity to an origin that is itself a reconstruction rather than a recoverable fact. Each tradition is what the specific interaction of chronological, institutional, cognitive, and broker mechanisms has produced in a specific historical context, and its value and significance should be assessed on the basis of what it achieves within that context.

This general theory is not specific to Buddhism — its mechanisms are operative in any long-distance cultural transmission. The study of how Neoplatonism was transmitted from Alexandria to the Islamic world through Syriac intermediaries and from the Islamic world to medieval European scholasticism through Arabic intermediaries exhibits the same four-mechanism structure. The tantric Buddhist case is a particularly rich instance of a general phenomenon, and the theoretical framework it allows to be developed has implications for how cultural transmission is studied wherever it occurs.

### 8.2 Soteriological Sufficiency as Civilisational Claim

One of the most important findings of this review’s comparative analysis is that each of the Buddhist traditions examined understood itself as *sufficient* rather than *lacking* — as providing a complete path to liberation rather than an incomplete version of a more complete path available elsewhere. Kūkai’s Shingon claimed, through the *sokushin jōbutsu* doctrine, that buddhahood was achievable in this very body through the Three Mysteries practice. The Tibetan tradition’s multiple schools each claimed, in different ways and with different philosophical justifications, that their specific lineage preserved the complete transmission adequate to the goal of liberation.

These self-sufficiency claims are not merely institutional self-promotion. They are philosophical claims about the nature of the path and its relationship to the goal — claims whose evaluation requires taking seriously the specific philosophical resources and practical achievements of each tradition rather than assessing them against a standard derived from a different tradition’s self-understanding.

Kūkai’s *sokushin jōbutsu* claim rests on the *ri-chi funi* doctrine: if the Womb Realm’s principle and the Diamond Realm’s wisdom are genuinely non-dual, and if the Three Mysteries practice enacts rather than represents this non-duality, then the practitioner who genuinely performs the Three Mysteries is genuinely non-dual with Mahāvairocana’s awakened body, speech, and mind — and genuine non-duality with an awakened being’s body, speech, and mind is, by definition, buddhahood. The argument is valid given its premises. The philosophical question is whether the premises can be established — whether the enactment is genuinely non-dual or merely representationally non-dual, whether the ritual performance achieves the recognition the doctrine describes or merely points toward it. Kūkai’s most careful accounts suggest that the enactment requires a practitioner whose philosophical understanding is sufficiently deep to make the Three Mysteries performance genuinely recognitory rather than merely mechanical — a requirement that reintroduces the question of what kind of understanding is sufficient, and whether it requires something like the *prāsaṅgika* Madhyamaka realisation that Tsongkhapa identified as the necessary ground for completion-stage recognition.

The comparative analysis reveals that the self-sufficiency claims are not straightforwardly comparable across traditions because they are made against different background assumptions about what the goal is and what conditions are necessary and sufficient for its achievement. The most philosophically honest conclusion is neither that all traditions are equivalent nor that some have the real path and others have deficient approximations. It is that each tradition has developed, within the specific conditions of its historical formation and with the specific philosophical and practical resources it had available, genuine achievements that cannot be straightforwardly replicated by any other tradition and cannot be adequately evaluated by any external standard that does not take those specific conditions seriously.

### 8.3 Open Questions and Future Directions

The comparative transmission history developed across this review’s eight sections has produced, alongside its substantive findings, a set of genuinely open questions whose importance the review’s analysis has established but whose resolution it has not achieved. Acknowledging these open questions honestly is not a concession to incompleteness but an intellectual responsibility — the responsibility to be clear about where the analysis has reached the limits of available evidence, where the conceptual frameworks the field has developed are insufficient to the phenomena being studied, and where the institutional conditions of scholarship have prevented questions from being asked that the phenomena themselves require.

The first and most fundamental open question concerns the phenomenology of advanced practice: what does the path actually feel like from inside, at its most demanding levels, for practitioners who have traversed it seriously across extended periods? This review has engaged extensively with the historical, philosophical, and institutional dimensions of the traditions it has examined, and those dimensions are genuinely important. But they are not, by themselves, sufficient for understanding what the traditions most essentially claim: that specific practices produce specific transformations in specific practitioners, and that those transformations can be known — from the inside — in ways that third-person historical and philosophical analysis cannot fully capture. The systematic suppression of the first-person phenomenological perspective across this review’s turns reflects a genuine methodological limitation whose correction requires developing the tools to take seriously as philosophical documents the first-person testimonial literature that the traditions themselves produced: Milarepa’s *Mgur ’bum*, Longchenpa’s *Kindly Bent to Ease Us*, Kūkai’s *Sokushin jōbutsu gi*, the *mahāsiddha* *dohā* literature.

The second open question concerns the political economy of Buddhist institutions and its relationship to doctrinal development. This review has traced the institutional mechanisms of transmission — translation bureaux, royal patronage systems, monastic universities, lineage structures — and shown how they shaped what was transmitted and what was received. But it has treated these institutional mechanisms primarily as formal structures, without adequately addressing how the economic organisation of Buddhist institutions — how monasteries were funded, how monks were supported, how the costs of elaborate ritual were met, how the power to control access to initiations was distributed — shaped doctrinal development. The Dunhuang administrative documents recording monastic land holdings, labour obligations, and donation records remain significantly underused relative to their potential contribution to understanding how institutional economics shaped practice and doctrine at the frontier communities this review has examined.

The third open question — the post-colonial methodological question — concerns the most fundamental conditions of the scholarship itself: what frameworks of evaluation, what hierarchies of value, what institutional arrangements, and what intellectual commitments allow Buddhist studies to address the traditions it studies honestly rather than through the distorting lens of inherited colonial evaluations. The critical scholarship of Lopez, Schopen, Sharf, and their successors has demonstrated convincingly that the colonial framework distorted Buddhist studies in ways the field is still working to correct. What has not yet been achieved is a positive account of what Buddhist studies would look like if the corrections were complete — if the field had genuinely abandoned the colonial framework’s evaluative hierarchy and developed new frameworks adequate to the traditions it studies on their own terms.

Such a framework would need to be genuinely comparative without being hierarchical, genuinely interdisciplinary without being methodologically incoherent, genuinely collaborative between scholars and practitioners without conflating scholarship with practice, and genuinely global without being falsely universalist. No single review article can develop such a framework completely. What this review can do — and has attempted — is to demonstrate, through sustained application of the transmission-and-filtration methodology to a complex comparative case, what a more adequate framework would need to include.

The most important of the questions that framework would need to be capable of asking is also the simplest, and it is the question with which this review began and to which it returns in conclusion: what survives the journey? When a tradition crosses a cultural boundary — through chronological accident, institutional filtering, cognitive prior formation, and broker mediation — what of the tradition that arrives is still the tradition that departed, and what is something genuinely new? The question has no general answer, and its specific answers for specific cases require exactly the kind of careful, multi-level, evidence-sensitive comparative analysis that this review has attempted. But the question itself — asked honestly, without the colonial framework’s assumption that proximity to the origin is the measure of value, and without the modernist framework’s assumption that the essential core is separable from the historical forms it takes — is the question that the comparative history of tantric Buddhism most insistently raises, and that the field’s most important future scholarship must find new ways to address.

The tradition being studied has, across fifteen centuries and at least five major cultural transformations, maintained a claim whose philosophical seriousness this review has attempted to honour: that awakening is possible, that specific practices produce genuine transformation, and that the integration of philosophical understanding with embodied practice is not a pedagogical strategy but the nature of the path itself. Whether that claim is true — in whatever sense “true” can meaningfully apply to a claim about the deepest possibilities of human experience — is not a question this review can answer. What this review can conclude is that the claim is serious, that the traditions that have carried it across centuries and cultures are more philosophically sophisticated and more institutionally complex than any of the frameworks through which they have been received in the modern West have recognised, and that the study of how those traditions have moved through history — what they have preserved, what they have transformed, and what they have lost — is one of the most intellectually demanding and humanly important tasks available to contemporary scholarship.
