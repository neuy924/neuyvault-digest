# One Mind, Many Gates

## The Philosophical and Artistic Traditions of East Asian Buddhism

*A Review Article*

-----

**Abstract**

This article reviews the major intellectual, doctrinal, and artistic traditions of East Asian Buddhism from the Chinese reception of the Dharma in the 1st century CE through the Kamakura period flowering in Japan (13th century). Focusing on the four dominant schools — Tiāntái (天台), Huáyán (華嚴), Chán (禪), and Pure Land (淨土) — the article traces their Indian philosophical inheritances, their distinctively Chinese and Japanese transformations, and the key syntheses that shaped what became the dominant form of Buddhism across the Sinosphere. Special attention is paid to Yǒngmíng Yánshòu’s (永明延壽) Chan-Pure Land synthesis and to the raigō (來迎) painting tradition as a case study in doctrine made material. The article argues that East Asian Buddhism’s signal contribution is the development of multiple simultaneous “gates” (門) to a single awakened reality — a pluralism that is not relativism but the practical expression of the Mahāyāna doctrine of skillful means (*upāya*, 方便).

-----

## Section 1 · Transmission and Translation: Buddhism Crosses the Silk Road

*How the Dharma travelled from India to China, and what was gained and lost in the crossing.*

### 1.1 · The Translator-Monks and Their Methods

The transmission of Buddhism from India to China was not a passive diffusion but an act of sustained creative interpretation. The men who carried the Dharma eastward were not merely linguists but theologians operating across two radically different metaphysical universes, making consequential choices at every turn of the brush.

The first major translations into Chinese — undertaken by Parthian monks An Shigao (安世高, fl. 148–180 CE) and Lokakṣema (支婁迦讖, fl. 178–189 CE) — were rough and provisional. The technical vocabulary of Sanskrit Buddhist philosophy had no ready Chinese equivalent. *Śūnyatā* (शून्यता, emptiness), *dharma* (धर्म), *vijñāna* (विज्ञान, consciousness), *nirvāṇa* (निर्वाण) — these terms carried conceptual loads that Chinese characters could not simply absorb without distortion.

The solution adopted in the earliest period was *geyi* (格義, “matching concepts”): the deliberate mapping of Buddhist terms onto Daoist equivalents. *Śūnyatā* was rendered through the Daoist *wú* (無, non-being); *dharma* approximated through *dào* (道, the Way); meditative absorption (*dhyāna*, ध्यान) associated with Daoist quietism. This made Buddhist texts legible to Chinese readers but at the cost of systematic misreading — early Chinese Buddhists absorbed a Daoist-tinted Buddhism that the tradition would spend centuries correcting.

The corrective came through **Kumārajīva** (鳩摩羅什, 344–413 CE), perhaps the most important figure in the entire history of Chinese Buddhism. Born in Kucha (庫車) to an Indian father and Kuchean mother, trained in both Hīnayāna and Mahāyāna, fluent in Sanskrit and Tocharian, he was brought to Chang’an (長安) as a political captive and installed as a state-sponsored translator. His workshop — staffed by hundreds of monk-scholars — produced translations of the *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa* (維摩詰經), the *Prajñāpāramitā* sūtras (般若經), Nāgārjuna’s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (中論), and the *Lotus Sūtra* (妙法蓮華經) that remain in liturgical use today.

Kumārajīva’s method was deliberately non-literal. He chose resonant Chinese phrases over technical accuracy, sacrificing precision for comprehensibility. His *Lotus Sūtra* is widely regarded as a work of classical Chinese prose in its own right — an achievement that helped ensure its cultural penetration. Against geyi, he insisted on coining new Buddhist technical terms rather than borrowing Daoist ones, though traces of the earlier practice persisted in colloquial usage.

**Xuánzàng** (玄奘, 602–664 CE) went further in the opposite direction — toward rigorous philological accuracy. After his famous sixteen-year pilgrimage to India (629–645 CE), he returned with 657 texts and a command of Sanskrit that no Chinese before him had possessed. His translations prioritised terminological consistency and doctrinal precision, producing a body of Yogācāra texts — including Vasubandhu’s (वसुबन्धु) *Vijñaptimātratāsiddhi* (成唯識論) — of lasting scholarly value.

The cumulative effect of two centuries of translation was the creation of a Chinese Buddhist canon (*Dàzàng jīng*, 大藏經) that was simultaneously a translation of Indian thought and an original Chinese intellectual achievement — the product of choices, emphases, and elisions that no Indian would have made in quite the same way.

### 1.2 · The Apocryphal Question: Chinese Buddhist Creativity

Among the most consequential texts in East Asian Buddhism, several were almost certainly composed in China — presented as translations of lost Indian originals, but bearing unmistakable signs of Chinese authorship in their conceptual vocabulary, literary structure, and doctrinal preoccupations. This phenomenon, which modern scholars call “apocrypha” (*yíjīng*, 疑經, “suspect scriptures”), was for centuries either unknown or quietly ignored. Its recognition in the 20th century forced a fundamental reassessment of what “authenticity” means in the Buddhist textual tradition.

The paradigm case is the **大乘起信論** (*Dasheng Qixin Lun*, “Awakening of Faith in the Mahāyāna”), attributed by tradition to the Indian poet-philosopher Aśvaghoṣa (馬鳴, c. 1st–2nd c. CE) and translated, supposedly, by Paramārtha (真諦) in 553 CE. No Sanskrit original has been found. The text’s philosophical architecture — its doctrine of One Mind (一心) with two aspects (Mind as Suchness 心真如門; Mind as Arising-and-Ceasing 心生滅門), its synthesis of Mādhyamaka and Yogācāra, its elaboration of the *ālayavijñāna* (阿賴耶識, storehouse-consciousness) — reflects a stage of Chinese Buddhist synthesis that the named translator’s period makes chronologically improbable. The scholarly consensus, following Mochizuki Shinkō’s (望月信亨) early 20th-century analysis, is that the text is a Chinese composition, probably of the late 5th or early 6th century.

Yet the *Awakening of Faith* became the single most cited and commented-upon text in Chinese Buddhist history. It provided the conceptual infrastructure — particularly the One Mind doctrine — that made possible Tiāntái, Huáyán, Chan, and the Yánshòu synthesis. Its “inauthenticity” did not diminish its authority; arguably, its Chinese authorship explains its extraordinary influence, precisely because it answered the questions Chinese practitioners were actually asking.

The **觀無量壽經** (*Guān Wúliàngshòu Jīng*, *Contemplation Sūtra*), attributed to Kālayaśas (畺良耶舍, translated 424 CE), presents a similar case. No Sanskrit original survives. The text’s frame narrative — centred on Queen Vaidehī (韋提希) and the political tragedy of the Ajātaśatru cycle — and its systematic visualisation curriculum reflect specifically Chinese soteriological concerns. Fujita Kōtatsu’s (藤田宏達) careful comparative analysis of the text’s doctrinal vocabulary has persuaded most scholars that it is a Central Asian or Chinese composition. This did not prevent it from becoming the most practically influential of the three Pure Land canonical sūtras, generating the entire tradition of contemplative visualisation and, eventually, raigō art.

What these cases reveal is a tradition that understood *upāya* (方便, skillful means) not merely as a pedagogical principle but as a creative license. If all teaching is adapted to the needs of its audience, then a text composed for Chinese practitioners that addresses their specific conditions is no less valid than an Indian original addressing Indian ones. The apocrypha are not frauds — they are Chinese Buddhism thinking for itself, using the available authority structures to do so.

### 1.3 · Daoist and Confucian Interfaces

Buddhism did not enter a philosophical vacuum. China already possessed two of the world’s most sophisticated intellectual traditions, and the encounter between them and the Dharma was not a simple case of replacement or assimilation but a prolonged, complex negotiation that shaped all three parties.

The resonances between Chan and **Daoism** (道家) are the most visible and have attracted the most scholarly attention — sometimes to the point of overstating them. Chan’s valorisation of *wúwéi* (無為, non-doing), spontaneity (*zìrán*, 自然), and the inadequacy of conceptual language does indeed parallel central Daoist themes in the *Dàodéjīng* (道德經) and *Zhuāngzǐ* (莊子). Huìnéng’s celebrated verse in the *Platform Sūtra* (六祖壇經) — “Originally there is not a single thing; where could dust alight?” — has a distinctly Daoist flavour, as does the kōan tradition’s playful destruction of conceptual categories.

But the parallel should not be pressed too far. Chan’s *wúxīn* (無心, “no-mind”) is grounded in the Mādhyamaka doctrine of śūnyatā — the absence of inherent self-nature — not in the Daoist conception of primordial nature (*zìrán*). Chan awakening is not a return to nature but a recognition of the mind’s empty luminosity. The Daoist vocabulary was a bridge, not a destination.

The engagement with **Confucianism** (儒家) was more politically fraught. The most acute point of conflict concerned monastic exemptions from filial piety (*xiào*, 孝) — the paramount Confucian virtue. Monks shaved their heads (a violation of the Confucian injunction not to harm the body given by parents), left their families (abandoning filial duty), and did not bow to parents or rulers (undermining the hierarchical order). These were not trivial objections in a society that understood cosmic and social order as continuous.

Buddhism’s accommodation took two main forms. First, the doctrinal reframing of filial piety: merit-transfer (*huíxiàng*, 迴向) — dedicating the merit of practice to deceased parents — was presented as a *superior* form of filial piety, more efficacious than any Confucian memorial rite. The *Ullambana Sūtra* (盂蘭盆經, *Yulanpen Jing*) — almost certainly another Chinese apocryphon — told the story of Maudgalyāyana (目犍連) rescuing his mother from hell through Buddhist ritual, directly translating the filial narrative into Buddhist terms.

Second, the institutional accommodation: Chinese Buddhist monks were progressively integrated into the state ritual apparatus, performing prayers for imperial prosperity and rain-making ceremonies that positioned Buddhism as complementary rather than subversive to Confucian social order. The *sengzheng* (僧正, “Sangha Controller”) system — state oversight of monastic ordination and registration — gave the state authority over Buddhist institutions while giving Buddhism state protection.

The synthesis was never complete. The three major persecutions of Buddhism in Chinese history (the **三武一宗法難**, *sān wǔ yī zōng fǎnàn*) — under Tang Emperor Wǔzōng (842–845 CE) in particular — were explicitly motivated by Confucian and Daoist arguments that Buddhism was foreign, anti-social, and economically parasitic. The encounter was productive, but the tension never fully resolved.

## Section 2 · The Chinese Schools: Four Philosophical Architectures

*The four major schools as four distinct answers to the question: what is the relationship between the awakened mind and ordinary experience?*

### 2.1 · Tiāntái (天台宗): The Holographic Cosmos

**Tiāntái** (天台宗), systematised by the monk **Zhìyǐ** (智顗, 538–597 CE) on Mount Tiāntái (天台山) in Zhejiang province, represents perhaps the most ambitious attempt in all of Buddhist history to construct a single, internally consistent doctrinal architecture that accounts for the entire diversity of Buddhist teaching. It is a system that aspires to be a metasystem — not one school among many but the framework within which all schools find their proper place.

Zhìyǐ’s philosophical foundation is the **Three Truths** (*sān dì*, 三諦), derived from Nāgārjuna’s (नागार्जुन) Mādhyamaka but given a distinctly Chinese elaboration. The three truths are:

- **Emptiness** (*kōng*, 空): phenomena lack inherent, self-standing existence — *śūnyatā* as Nāgārjuna articulated it
- **Conventional existence** (*jiǎ*, 假): phenomena nonetheless appear, function, and are conventionally real
- **The Middle** (*zhōng*, 中): these two are not sequential views but simultaneous aspects of every phenomenon — the Middle Truth holds them together without collapsing one into the other

The critical move is that these three truths are not three separate perspectives to be adopted in sequence. They interpenetrate: each truth contains the other two. To see emptiness fully is already to see conventional existence and the Middle. This mutual containment — *sān dì yuánróng* (三諦圓融, “perfect interfusion of the three truths”) — became the template for Tiāntái’s signature doctrine.

That doctrine is **一念三千** (*yī niàn sān qiān*, “three thousand realms in a single moment of thought”). The number 3,000 is derived arithmetically:

> 10 dharma-realms (hell through Buddhahood) × 10 (each contains all others) × 10 aspects of existence × 3 (worlds of beings, aggregates, and space) = 3,000

The point is not the arithmetic but the claim: every moment of ordinary experience — a flash of anger, a glance at a flower, the sound of rain — *contains* the full complexity of all possible modes of existence, from the deepest hell to complete Buddhahood. Nothing is absent from any moment. The awakened mind is not somewhere else; it is fully present in the most mundane perception, waiting to be recognised as such.

Zhìyǐ’s **panjiao** (判教, “classification of teachings”) system organised the entire history of Buddhist teaching into five periods and eight categories corresponding to the stages of the Buddha’s pedagogical career — from the immediate post-enlightenment teaching (the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*, too difficult for ordinary minds) through various accommodations to the final definitive teaching of the *Lotus Sūtra* (妙法蓮華經). This hierarchical scheme positioned Tiāntái’s own preferred text at the apex, but it did so through a doctrinal argument — the *Lotus Sūtra*’s teaching of the One Vehicle (*Ekayāna*, 一乘) that encompasses and transcends the Three Vehicles — rather than mere assertion.

Tiāntái’s practical consequences were immense. The school became the primary institution of Japanese Buddhism in the early Heian period through **Saichō** (最澄, 767–822 CE), who brought its teachings from Mount Hiei (比叡山) and founded what became the nursery of all subsequent Japanese Buddhist reform movements. Hōnen (法然), Shinran (親鸞), Dōgen (道元), and Nichiren (日蓮) all trained at Mount Hiei before departing to found their own schools — carrying Tiāntái’s philosophical DNA into every corner of Japanese Buddhist life.

### 2.2 · Huáyán (華嚴宗): Mutual Interpenetration and the Aesthetics of Totality

If Tiāntái is Buddhism as systematic philosophy, **Huáyán** (華嚴宗) is Buddhism as visionary metaphysics — a tradition whose central image is so philosophically extravagant that it has continued to resonate across disciplines from medieval theology to systems theory to contemporary network science.

The school takes its name from the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* (華嚴經, *Huáyán Jīng*, “Flower Garland Sūtra”) — a vast, multi-layered text of uncertain Indian origin that describes the cosmos as perceived from the perspective of complete awakening. Its characteristic vision is of infinite Buddhas in infinite Buddha-fields, each containing all the others, interpenetrating without obstruction.

**Fǎzàng** (法藏, 643–712 CE), the school’s third and most philosophically rigorous patriarch, developed this vision into the doctrine of **事事無礙** (*shìshì wú’ài*, “unobstructed mutual interpenetration of phenomena”). The doctrine has two levels:

- **理事無礙** (*lǐshì wú’ài*): the unobstructed interpenetration of principle (*lǐ*, 理 — ultimate reality, śūnyatā) and phenomena (*shì*, 事 — particular things). This is standard Mahāyāna: phenomena are empty of inherent existence, thus “contain” the absolute.
- **事事無礙** (*shìshì wú’ài*): the unobstructed mutual interpenetration of phenomena with *each other*. This is Huáyán’s original contribution: not just that each thing is empty, but that each thing literally *contains* every other thing, while remaining fully itself.

Fǎzàng illustrated this to Empress Wǔ Zétiān (武則天) using a hall of mirrors — placing a Buddha statue at the centre, surrounding it with eight mirrors at the eight compass points, and lighting it with a torch. Each mirror reflected the statue; each reflection was reflected in every other mirror; and so on to infinity. The statue was fully present in every mirror, and each mirror was fully present in every reflection. This was *Indra’s Net* (因陀羅網, *yīntuóluó wǎng*) made visible.

The network image deserves unpacking. In Indian cosmology, Indra’s Net is a vast cosmic net stretching across all of space; at each node hangs a jewel, and each jewel reflects every other jewel, which themselves reflect every other jewel, and so on without end or remainder. Fǎzàng’s point: this is not a metaphor for something else. This *is* the structure of reality as perceived by an awakened mind — a reality in which the distinction between part and whole, container and contained, self and other has become transparently empty, revealing the mutual luminosity of all phenomena.

Huáyán as an institutional school did not survive the Tang-Song transition. It produced no major independent Chinese successor schools and was absorbed into the syncretic mainstream. Its philosophical vocabulary, however, became the common property of all subsequent East Asian Buddhist thought — particularly through its influence on Chan. The Huáyán concept of the “dharmadhātu” (法界, *fǎjiè*, “dharma-realm”) as the totalistic field within which awakening is simultaneously everywhere saturated Chan aesthetics, garden design, the tea ceremony, and ink painting in ways that are often unacknowledged.

In Korea, Huáyán became **Hwaeom** (화엄종) through the synthesis of **Ŭisang** (의상, 625–702 CE), a student of Fǎzàng, whose *Diagram of the Dharmadhātu* (法性偈, *Beopseong-ge*) — a poem arranged in the visual form of a labyrinth — compressed the entire Huáyán metaphysics into 210 Chinese characters. In Japan it became **Kegon** (華嚴宗), centred on Tōdaiji (東大寺) in Nara and its colossal Vairocana Buddha (*Birushana Butsu*, 毘盧遮那佛) — the cosmic Buddha of Huáyán at the centre of an empire conceived as a Buddha-field.

### 2.3 · The One Mind (一心) as Common Ground

Beneath the apparent diversity of the Chinese Buddhist schools runs a shared conceptual infrastructure — a set of terms, images, and frameworks so widely distributed that they constitute a kind of common language within which the schools’ arguments take place. The most important of these is **一心** (*yīxīn*, “One Mind”).

The *Awakening of Faith* (大乘起信論) — however its authorship is resolved — provided the canonical formulation. The text opens:

> *“The mind of sentient beings has two aspects. What are they? The first is the aspect of mind as suchness (心真如門, *xīn zhēnrú mén*); the second is the aspect of mind as arising and ceasing (心生滅門, *xīn shēngmiè mén*). Each of these two aspects includes all phenomena.”*

This deceptively simple statement contains an enormous philosophical claim: the same mind that is the ground of ordinary, conditioned, suffering existence (*saṃsāra*) is also, in its nature, the luminous, unconditional ground of awakening. These are not two different minds — not a “higher self” and a “lower self” in a hierarchical relationship. They are two aspects of the single reality that is mind: mind as it actually is (suchness, *zhēnrú*, 真如) and mind as it appears through the distorting lens of ignorance (arising-and-ceasing).

The traditional image — also from the *Awakening of Faith* — is water and waves. The water (*zhēnrú*) is the unchanging nature; the waves (*shēngmiè*) are the conditioned appearances. The waves are real — they arise, affect things, and cease — but they are not separate from the water. To see the waves *as* water is awakening; to mistake the waves for a reality independent of the water is ignorance.

Yǒngmíng Yánshòu (永明延壽) made this architectonic role of One Mind explicit. In the *Zongjing Lu* (宗鏡錄), he wrote:

> *“The One Mind is the source-mirror of all the Buddhas, the origin of all teachings, the master of all practices, the substance behind all phenomena.”*

The “source-mirror” image (*zōng jìng*, 宗鏡) of the title encodes the claim: the One Mind is the mirror within which all Buddhist teachings — Chan, Pure Land, Tiāntái, Huáyán — are reflections. Each school sees one face of the mirror; none exhausts it.

## Section 3 · Chán (禪宗): Direct Transmission and the Paradox of Wordless Teaching

*The school that claimed to need no doctrine — and produced more doctrinal literature than any other.*

### 3.1 · The Patriarchal Narrative and Its Construction

Chan’s founding claim is stated in four lines that became the school’s manifesto:

> 教外別傳 — *jiào wài bié chuán* — “A special transmission outside the scriptures”
> 不立文字 — *bù lì wénzì* — “Not founded on words and letters”
> 直指人心 — *zhí zhǐ rén xīn* — “Directly pointing at the human mind”
> 見性成佛 — *jiàn xìng chéng fó* — “Seeing one’s nature, becoming Buddha”

These lines do not appear as a set in any early Chan text. They were assembled retrospectively — probably in the Song dynasty — to articulate what Chan claimed to be. Their retrospective construction is itself revealing: Chan’s identity was always as much a performance as a position, and the performance required an origin story.

That origin story centred on **Bodhidharma** (菩提達磨, *Pútídámó*, c. 5th–6th c. CE), an Indian or Central Asian monk who, tradition holds, brought Chan to China and sat in wall-gazing meditation (*bìguān*, 壁觀) for nine years at Shaolin Monastery (少林寺). The most famous anecdote has his future disciple Huìkě (慧可) cutting off his own arm in the snow outside Bodhidharma’s cave to prove his sincerity — whereupon Bodhidharma asks what he wants, Huìkě says he wants his mind pacified, and Bodhidharma says: “Bring me your mind and I will pacify it.” Huìkě searches and cannot find his mind. “There,” says Bodhidharma, “I have pacified it.”

This anecdote appears in no source earlier than the 7th century. Bodhidharma himself is a semi-legendary figure. The lineage of six patriarchs — from Bodhidharma through Huìkě (慧可), Sēngcàn (僧璨), Dàoxìn (道信), Hóngrěn (弘忍), to Huìnéng (慧能) — was constructed during the Tang dynasty as a legitimating narrative, asserting that Chan possessed an unbroken transmission (*yīmài xiāngchéng*, 一脈相承) of mind-to-mind recognition from the Buddha himself, bypassing the scholastic apparatus of sūtra and commentary.

The political stakes of this narrative were high. In Tang dynasty China, Buddhist schools were ranked and supported by the state. A school that could claim direct transmission from the Buddha — and could demonstrate this through charismatic teachers rather than textual mastery — had a powerful argument for institutional authority that did not depend on the patronage structures supporting Tiāntái or Huáyán scholarship.

The **Five Houses** (*wǔ jiā*, 五家) of Tang and Song Chan — Línjì (臨濟), Cáodòng (曹洞), Wèiyǎng (潙仰), Fǎyǎn (法眼), Yúnyán (雲岩) — each developed distinct pedagogical styles while claiming descent from the same patriarchal lineage. The theological differences between them were real, but so was the institutional competition. The *yǔlù* (語錄, “recorded sayings”) literature — transcriptions of masters’ dharma-combat exchanges — served simultaneously as devotional reading, pedagogical manual, and institutional credential.

### 3.2 · Kōan, Shikantaza, and the Two Faces of Practice

**Rinzai** (臨済宗) and **Sōtō** (曹洞宗) represent not merely two institutional lineages but two fundamentally different soteriological models — two answers to the question of what Buddhist practice is *for* and how it works.

The **kōan** (公案, *gōng’àn*) — literally “public case,” drawn from the legal terminology of Tang dynasty administration — is a recorded exchange, question, or situation from the Chan patriarchal literature, assigned to a student for intensive investigation. Classic examples include:

> *Zhào zhōu’s dog* (趙州狗子): A monk asked Zhaozhou (趙州, 778–897): “Does a dog have Buddha-nature?” Zhaozhou replied: “*Wú*” (無, “no/nothing”).

> *The original face* (本來面目): “What was your face before your parents were born?”

> *One hand* (隻手): “What is the sound of one hand clapping?”

These are not riddles with clever answers. The point is not to find the “correct” response but to exhaust the ordinary mind’s habit of seeking a conceptual answer — to work the question until the conceptual apparatus itself breaks down, revealing the direct perception of mind’s nature (*kenshō*, 見性) that the question was pointing toward all along. Hakuin Ekaku (白隱慧鶴, 1686–1769) systematised approximately 1,700 kōans into a curriculum of progressive difficulty — a pedagogical architecture that remains the structure of contemporary Rinzai training.

The face-to-face interview (*dokusan*, 獨參, or *sanzen*, 參禪) in which the student presents their understanding to the master is the crucible of this system. No conceptual performance passes. The master tests not for the right answer but for the right *presence* — a quality of unmediated recognition that cannot be faked. The relationship between master and student is thus the pedagogical instrument itself: the master’s awakening functions as a kind of diagnostic field within which the student’s understanding — or lack of it — becomes visible.

**Dōgen** (道元, 1200–1253) rejected this model entirely — or rather, dissolved its fundamental premise. In his view, the Rinzai framework still operated within a subtle dualism: practice as a means to awakening, awakening as the goal practice is aiming at. This implied that practice and awakening were two different things — that the practitioner sits in *unawakened* meditation in hopes of achieving an *awakening* that is not yet present.

Dōgen’s counter-claim — stated most economically in the phrase **修証一等** (*shushō ittō*, “practice and realisation are one”) — was that this dualism is the fundamental error. Sitting in zazen (坐禪) *is* the expression of awakening, not the means to it. A Buddha sits. Sitting is what awakening looks like in a body. There is no gap between the practice and the goal because the practice *is* the goal, fully present in each moment of its enactment.

This led to the practice Dōgen called **只管打坐** (*shikantaza*, “just sitting”) — total, goalless presence in the act of sitting, with no object, no attainment, no evaluation. The *Fukanzazengi* (普勸坐禪儀, “Universal Recommendations for Zazen”), Dōgen’s first major composition after returning from China in 1227, states:

> *“Do not think that you will necessarily be aware of your own enlightenment. Although the Way of the Buddhas is actualized, do not think that this is something to be grasped.”*

The philosophical depth of Dōgen’s position is worked out in extraordinary detail across the 95 fascicles of the *Shōbōgenzō* (正法眼蔵, “Treasury of the True Dharma Eye”) — one of the most philosophically ambitious texts in the history of Buddhist literature, engaging questions of time, language, Buddha-nature, and the nature of practice with a rigour that has attracted serious attention from contemporary Western philosophers.

-----

### 3.3 · Chan Travels: Seon, Zen, Thiền, and Western Adaptations

Chan’s journey beyond China produced three distinct regional traditions — each an authentic heir and each a genuine transformation.

**Korean Seon** (선종, 禪宗) arrived through multiple Chinese transmissions but was decisively shaped by **Jinul** (지눌, 1158–1210 CE), whose synthesis remains the doctrinal foundation of Korean Buddhism to this day. Jinul’s key move was to take the Rinzai-Sōtō debate — sudden awakening versus gradual cultivation — and resolve it by insisting they were addressing different questions. Awakening (*dono*, 頓悟) is sudden: the recognition of mind’s nature cannot be gradual, because it is the recognition that there was nothing to gradually achieve. But cultivation (*jeomsu*, 漸修) after awakening is necessary and gradual: old karmic habits persist even after insight has broken through, and they must be progressively worn away through sustained practice.

This **“sudden awakening, gradual cultivation”** (*dono jeomsu*, 頓悟漸修) model — expressed in Jinul’s *Secrets on Cultivating the Mind* (修心訣, *Susim-gyeol*) — gave Korean Buddhism a via media between the Chan polarity, and it became the doctrinal foundation of the **Jogye Order** (조계종), which today accounts for approximately 90% of Korean Buddhist institutional life.

**Vietnamese Thiền** (禪) arrived through multiple routes — both directly from Chinese Chan and through the influence of Chinese monks fleeing the Tang-dynasty persecutions. The tradition’s most internationally known representative is **Thich Nhat Hanh** (Thích Nhất Hạnh, 1926–2022), whose “Engaged Buddhism” (*Phật giáo dấn thân*, 佛教入世) integrated Chan practice with social activism, Gandhian nonviolence, and Western psychotherapy — producing a globally influential synthesis that bears Chan’s family resemblance while being something distinctly new.

The **Western reception** of Chan/Zen is a study in productive distortion. **D.T. Suzuki** (鈴木大拙, 1870–1966) introduced Zen to Western audiences through a series of English-language essays (beginning with *Essays in Zen Buddhism*, 1927) that emphasised its anti-rational, iconoclastic, mystical character — deliberately selecting aspects that would resonate with a Western audience formed by Romanticism and dissatisfied with institutional Christianity. Suzuki’s Zen was stripped of its monastic context, its hierarchical teacher-student relationships, its ritual infrastructure, and its deep embeddedness in Confucian social ethics. What remained was a “pure” awakening-experience that could be extracted from its cultural matrix and presented as universal.

The consequences were significant. Western Zen centres founded on Suzuki’s influence often lacked the institutional structures — the lineage verification, the ethical oversight, the communal discipline — that in Japan prevented individual teachers from abusing their authority. The sexual misconduct scandals that plagued American Zen from the 1980s onward were not incidental: they were in part the predictable result of transmitting the *experience* of Chan authority without the *structures* that historically constrained it.

The mindfulness movement, which drew primarily from Theravāda vipassanā rather than Chan, represents a different kind of Western Buddhist inheritance — one doctrinally closer to India than to East Asia. Jon Kabat-Zinn’s Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR, 1979) deliberately bracketed the metaphysical and soteriological framework of Buddhist practice, retaining the technique while discarding the context. The result is a deracinated practice of considerable clinical utility but radically different character from either the Chan or Theravāda traditions it draws upon.

## Section 4 · Pure Land (淨土宗): The Democratisation of Liberation

*How a cosmological claim about Amitābha’s vow restructured Buddhist soteriology from the ground up.*

### 4.1 · The Three Sūtras and Their Doctrinal Architecture

Pure Land Buddhism rests on three canonical sūtras that perform complementary doctrinal functions — a fact that itself reflects careful editorial construction by the tradition’s patriarchs, who selected these three texts from a larger body of Amitābha-related literature to constitute the tradition’s scriptural basis.

The **大無量壽經** (*Dà Wúliàngshòu Jīng*, *Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra*, “Sūtra of Immeasurable Life”) provides the cosmic backstory and the vow-structure. It narrates the career of the monk Dharmākara (法藏菩薩, *Fǎzàng Púsà*), who contemplated 210 billion Buddha-fields over five kalpas before formulating 48 vows staking his own Buddhahood on the liberation of all beings who turn to him. The 18th vow — the Primal Vow (本願, *Hongan*) — is the tradition’s soteriological hinge:

> *“If, when I attain Buddhahood, sentient beings of the ten directions who sincerely and joyfully entrust themselves to me, desire to be born in my land, and call my name even ten times, should not be born there — then may I not attain the supreme perfect enlightenment.”*

Since Dharmākara has become Amitābha (阿彌陀佛) — infinite light, infinite life — the vow is proven fulfilled. The logical structure is retrospective: Amitābha’s Buddhahood is the evidence that the vow was kept. Every person who calls his name therefore calls upon a proven reality, not a hope.

The **阿彌陀經** (*Āmítuó Jīng*, *Smaller Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra*) — translated by Kumārajīva in 402 CE into prose of celebrated literary beauty — performs a different function. It is brief, vivid, and its theological trump card is its frame: the teaching was not requested. Śākyamuni speaks unprompted, which Pure Land exegetes read as evidence that this teaching exceeds all pedagogical accommodation — it erupts from the Buddha’s innermost realization because it cannot be contained. The six-direction testimony, in which Buddhas in all cosmic directions extend their tongues across the triple-thousandfold world system to confirm the sūtra’s authenticity, universalises the teaching: this is not Śākyamuni’s regional instruction but the unanimous proclamation of all awakened minds.

The **觀無量壽經** (*Guān Wúliàngshòu Jīng*, *Contemplation Sūtra*) provides the practical curriculum — the sixteen contemplation stages from the setting sun to the full vision of Amitābha’s body. Its narrative frame is unique: the teaching emerges not from a question about doctrine but from a queen’s despair. Vaidehī (韋提希), imprisoned by her regicidal son Ajātaśatru (阿闍世), weeps tears of blood (*tì xuè*, 涕血) and calls out to the Buddha — who appears miraculously and teaches her, and through her all subsequent practitioners, the path to the Pure Land.

Shandao (善導, 613–681 CE) performed a crucial hermeneutical reversal on this sūtra. The sixteen contemplations are technically demanding — requiring sustained meditative concentration that most practitioners cannot achieve. Shandao argued that these visualisations are the *secondary* (*sān fú yè*, 散福業) practice, while **nembutsu recitation** (*chēng míng niànfó*, 稱名念佛) — calling the Buddha’s name aloud — is the **primary** (*zhèng dìng yè*, 正定業) practice, the one practice that Amitābha’s vow directly supports.

|Sūtra                      |Function                                  |Key Doctrine                               |Primary Practice Taught               |
|---------------------------|------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
|大無量壽經 *Larger Sūtra*       |Cosmic backstory; vow-structure           |48 Vows; Primal Vow (本願)                   |Aspiration + nembutsu                 |
|阿彌陀經 *Smaller Sūtra*       |Universal testimony; Pure Land description|Six-direction confirmation; cosmic Amitābha|Holding the name (*chí míng*, 持名)     |
|觀無量壽經 *Contemplation Sūtra*|Practical curriculum; deathbed instruction|16 contemplations; 9 grades of rebirth     |Visualisation → nembutsu (per Shandao)|

### 4.2 · Shinran and the Theology of Other-Power

**Shinran** (親鸞, 1173–1263 CE) is the most theologically radical figure in the entire Pure Land tradition — and arguably in all of Japanese Buddhism. Trained at Mount Hiei from the age of nine, he became the devoted disciple of **Hōnen** (法然, 1133–1212) at the age of twenty-nine, and spent the remaining six decades of his life systematically working out the implications of Hōnen’s *senju nembutsu* (専修念仏, “exclusive nembutsu”) teaching.

Hōnen had argued that in the age of *mappō* (末法, “Latter Days of the Dharma”) — the degenerate period when human faculties are too diminished for traditional practice — the nembutsu alone was efficacious. All other practices — meditation, precepts, scholarship — were admirable but insufficient for liberation. Shinran accepted this premise and followed it further than Hōnen had gone.

The key concept is **他力** (*tariki*, “other-power”) in radical opposition to **自力** (*jiriki*, “self-power”). Hōnen had maintained that the practitioner must sincerely recite the nembutsu — that there was still a minimal act of self-directed effort involved. Shinran dissolved even this: if Amitābha’s vow is truly unconditional, then the desire to recite nembutsu, the faith (*shinjin*, 信心) that makes it sincere, and even the aspiration for rebirth are not *our* achievements but *Amitābha’s* mind arising within us.

This reached its most compressed and provocative formulation in the *Tannishō* (歎異抄, “Lamenting the Heterodoxies”), compiled by Shinran’s disciple Yuien (唯円) from the master’s oral teachings:

> *“Even the good person attains birth in the Pure Land, how much more so the evil person.”*

> *“When a person resolves to say the nembutsu, trusting in Other Power, he obtains the benefit of being grasped, never to be abandoned.”*

The inversion (*akunin shōki*, 悪人正機, “the evil person is the primary [object of the vow]”) follows necessarily from tariki logic. If liberation depended on our virtue, the virtuous person would have the advantage. But if liberation is entirely Amitābha’s action, then the person who has no virtue to rely on — who has nothing left to trust but the vow — is *closer* to the vow’s operation than the person who still imagines their good deeds contribute something.

|Concept             |Hōnen 法然                          |Shinran 親鸞                                  |
|--------------------|----------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
|Practice required   |Exclusive nembutsu recitation     |Nembutsu as expression of received faith    |
|Nature of faith     |Cultivated by the practitioner    |Gift of Amitābha’s mind (*nyorai ekō*, 如来回向)|
|Who is saved first  |The sincere practitioner          |The evil person (*akunin*, 悪人)              |
|Monastic requirement|Maintained (Hōnen remained a monk)|Dissolved (Shinran married, ate meat)       |
|School founded      |Jōdo-shū 浄土宗                      |Jōdo Shinshū 浄土真宗                           |

Shinran’s dissolution of monastic requirements was not antinomianism but logical consistency: if self-power is entirely irrelevant, then the monastic disciplines that *express* self-power are equally irrelevant to salvation. He married a nun (Eshinni, 恵信尼), had children, ate meat when unavoidable, and explicitly called himself “neither monk nor layman” (*hisō hizoku*, 非僧非俗). Jōdo Shinshū (浄土真宗) temples are today staffed by hereditary temple families in which the priestly role passes from father to son — one of the most radical institutional expressions of tariki theology.

### 4.3 · Pure Land and Social Practice

Pure Land’s soteriological universalism — the vow reaches all beings without discrimination — generated practical social consequences that distinguish it from the more elite-oriented traditions of Chan scholarship and Tiāntái doctrinal mastery.

The **deathbed** (*rinju*, 臨終) was Pure Land’s primary ritual theatre. Genshin’s (源信) *Ōjōyōshū* (往生要集, 985 CE) specified the full protocol: the dying person is oriented west (the direction of Sukhāvatī), a raigō image of Amitābha is hung at eye level, a five-colored cord (*goshikisen*, 五色線) connects their hand to the painted Buddha’s hand, and attending practitioners chant nembutsu continuously. The communal character of this practice — dying surrounded by chanting friends and family — contrasted sharply with both the solitary Chan meditator and the scholarly monk’s deathbed review of doctrine.

**Merit-transfer** (*ekō*, 回向 / *huíxiàng*, 迴向) extended Pure Land’s social reach temporally. The living could dedicate the merit of their nembutsu practice to deceased relatives, ensuring that the dead received benefit from the living’s practice. This directly addressed the Confucian filial concern: nembutsu was simultaneously a practice of self-liberation and an act of filial piety toward the ancestors. The *Ullambana* (盂蘭盆, *Obon*) festival — observed across East Asia in late summer — institutionalised this merging of Pure Land practice and ancestor-veneration into one of the most widely observed annual ritual occasions in the Buddhist world.

The **nembutsu confraternity** (*nembutsu-kō*, 念仏講) emerged as a lay organisational form in which groups of laypeople gathered regularly to chant nembutsu together, support one another at deathbeds, and perform collective merit-transfer. These groups — operating outside formal monastic structures — became a primary vehicle of popular Buddhist practice throughout the medieval period, creating networks of mutual religious support that also functioned as informal social welfare organisations, providing assistance to members in material need.

## Section 5 · Yǒngmíng Yánshòu and the Chan-Pure Land Synthesis

*The most consequential theological move in East Asian Buddhist history.*

### 5.1 · The *Zongjing Lu* (宗鏡錄) as Encyclopaedic Synthesis

**Yǒngmíng Yánshòu** (永明延壽, 904–975 CE) lived through the Five Dynasties period (五代十國, 907–960 CE) — a time of repeated political catastrophe, military violence, and the dissolution of the centralised Tang imperial order that had provided Buddhist institutions with their primary patronage structure. Chan monasteries competed for patronage with an intensity that generated sectarian conflict. Pure Land and Chan practitioners regarded each other with mutual suspicion. Buddhist institutional life was fragmented, doctrinally contentious, and politically vulnerable.

Yánshòu’s response to this crisis was the *Zongjing Lu* (宗鏡錄, “Record of the Source-Mirror”), completed around 960 CE — a work of 100 fascicles (卷) and approximately 600,000 Chinese characters, making it one of the longest works of Buddhist prose in the East Asian tradition. The scale is itself a statement: the tradition’s diversity, properly understood, is not a weakness requiring resolution but a richness requiring comprehension.

The text’s structure mirrors its argument. **三百問** (*sānbǎi wèn*, “three hundred questions”) covers the full range of doctrinal disputes that divided the schools: Is mind one or many? Does the Pure Land exist outside mind? What is the relationship between sudden awakening and gradual practice? Is Buddha-nature universal or acquired? Each question is genuine — not a strawman — and Yánshòu voices it with the precision of someone who has personally inhabited the difficulty.

The responses do not argue for one school against the others. Instead, Yánshòu cites — by his own count — over 300 different texts, from Āgamas to Mahāyāna sūtras to Chan patriarchal literature, demonstrating that each school, read with sufficient depth, points toward the same reality: the One Mind (*yīxīn*, 一心) that is simultaneously the ground of all existence and the nature of awakening. Sectarian disagreements arise not from genuine doctrinal incompatibility but from the mistake of taking a partial view for the whole — of mistaking one reflection in the mirror for the mirror itself.

> *“The One Mind encompasses all dharmas; all dharmas return to the One Mind. There is not a single dharma outside the One Mind; there is not a single Mind outside all dharmas.”*
> — *Zongjing Lu*, fascicle 1

The title encodes the thesis. *Zōng* (宗) means “source,” “patriarchal lineage,” or “fundamental teaching.” *Jìng* (鏡) means “mirror.” The source-mirror is the One Mind: the original luminosity that all Buddhist teachings are attempting to point toward, and within which all apparently conflicting teachings are reflections of a single reality. To take any reflection for the thing itself — to mistake Chan’s radical anti-conceptualism, or Pure Land’s devotional framework, for the final word — is to confuse map for territory.

-----

### 5.2 · The Four Combinations Verse (四料簡) and Its Legacy

Yánshòu’s most widely disseminated teaching — the one that shaped the practice of Chinese Buddhist monasticism for the following millennium — is a verse of four stanzas called the **四料簡** (*sì liào jiǎn*, “four assessments”) or **四料偈** (*sì liào jì*, “four assessment verses”). It assesses the four possible combinations of Chan and Pure Land practice:

> 有禪有淨土，猶如戴角虎，現世為人師，來生作佛祖。
> *You chán yǒu jìngtǔ, yóurú dài jiǎo hǔ, xiànshì wéi rén shī, láishēng zuò fó zǔ.*
> “Chan with Pure Land — like a tiger that has grown horns. In this life a teacher of humans; in the next, a patriarch of Buddhas.”

> 無禪有淨土，萬修萬人去，若得見彌陀，何愁不開悟。
> *Wú chán yǒu jìngtǔ, wàn xiū wàn rén qù, ruò dé jiàn mítuó, hé chóu bù kāiwù.*
> “Pure Land without Chan — ten thousand practice, ten thousand go [to rebirth]. When they see Amitābha, why worry about not awakening?”

> 有禪無淨土，十人九蹉路，陰境若現前，瞥爾隨他去。
> *Yǒu chán wú jìngtǔ, shírén jiǔ cuò lù, yīn jìng ruò xiàn qián, piē ěr suí tā qù.*
> “Chan without Pure Land — nine out of ten go astray. When the bardo-state appears, they are swept away in an instant.”

> 無禪無淨土，鐵床並銅柱，萬劫與千生，沒個人依怙。
> *Wú chán wú jìngtǔ, tiě chuáng bìng tóng zhù, wàn jié yǔ qiān shēng, méi gè rén yīhù.*
> “Neither Chan nor Pure Land — an iron bed and copper pillar [of hell await]. Ten thousand kalpas, a thousand lives — no one to rely on.”

The argument is pragmatic rather than metaphysical. Chan without Pure Land is *dangerous* — not because Chan is wrong but because Chan practice requires a specific kind of stability at the moment of death that most practitioners, however sincere, have not achieved. When the mind’s ordinary cognitive functions dissolve in the dying process, only thoroughly penetrated awakening — not the *belief* that one has penetrated awakening — survives the dissolution. The Chan practitioner who is not yet fully through risks being swept away by the bardo-states (*yīn jìng*, 陰境) that arise at death, losing whatever progress they had made.

Pure Land without Chan is *safe* — Amitābha’s vow guarantees rebirth regardless of the practitioner’s meditative attainment — but it leaves the practitioner dependent on external support indefinitely. It is the practice appropriate to those who cannot generate stable insight, which Yánshòu understood to include most practitioners in the age of *mappō*.

Chan *with* Pure Land combines the tiger’s inherent strength with the wings of the vow: the practitioner brings genuine insight to the moment of death, AND is caught by Amitābha’s vow if that insight is not yet complete. The combination is the most resilient soteriological position.

The verse’s legacy was direct and measurable: from the Song dynasty onward, the dominant form of Chinese monastic practice became **禪淨雙修** (*chán jìng shuāng xiū*, “dual cultivation of Chan and Pure Land”) — the morning and evening recitation of nembutsu alongside Chan meditation practice. This is still the normative form of Chinese Buddhist institutional life.

-----

### 5.3 · The “Who Is Reciting?” Kōan and Syncretic Practice

The synthesis did not remain at the level of institutional prescription. It generated a new contemplative method that was simultaneously Chan investigation and Pure Land practice: the kōan **念佛者誰** (*niànfó zhě shuí*, “Who is the one reciting the Buddha’s name?”).

The question is attributed to Dahui Zonggao (大慧宗杲, 1089–1163 CE) but became the signature practice of the Chan-Pure Land synthesis in the Yuan, Ming, and Qing dynasties. Its logic is elegant: the nembutsu practitioner is already doing something — reciting the name. But who is doing it? Turn the light of inquiry back on the reciter. Do not seek the answer conceptually. Hold the question — *who is reciting?* — with the same intensity with which a Rinzai student holds *“Wú”* (無). Let the question undermine the assumption of a fixed, stable “I” that recites, without abandoning the recitation itself.

**Zhūhóng** (袾宏, 1535–1615 CE), abbot of Yunqi Monastery (雲棲寺) near Hangzhou, was the Ming dynasty’s most important architect of this synthesis. His *Zhúchuāng Suíbǐ* (竹窗隨筆, “Random Notes from the Bamboo Window”) systematically argued that nembutsu investigation and Chan kōan practice were functionally identical — both aimed at breaking the conceptual habit that mistakes the appearance of a self for a reality. The only difference was the vehicle: the Chan student uses *“Wú”*; the Pure Land student uses *“Who is reciting?”* Both arrive at the same recognition.

**Xūyún** (虛雲, 1840–1959 CE) — the most celebrated Chan master of the modern period, who reportedly lived to 119 years old and witnessed the full arc from late imperial to communist China — practised and transmitted both approaches. His account of his own awakening — triggered by the sound of a teacup breaking during a Chan retreat — employed the classic Chan sudden-awakening narrative. Yet he consistently recommended nembutsu investigation to students without deep Chan foundations, and his monasteries maintained both practices.

The epistemological achievement of the synthesis deserves emphasis. Chan’s anti-institutional bias — its valorisation of direct experience over external authority — creates a structural vulnerability: who verifies that a practitioner’s claimed awakening is genuine? The Chan answer — the teacher’s recognition in *dokusan* — works when the teacher is genuinely awakened, but provides no safeguard when the teacher is self-deceived or fraudulent. Yánshòu’s synthesis introduced a complementary check: Amitābha’s external wisdom, which does not depend on self-assessment, catches what the practitioner’s own evaluation might miss. The vow operates regardless of whether the practitioner thinks they are awakened. It is not deceivable.

## Section 6 · Doctrine Made Material: The Raigō Painting Tradition

*How the Pure Land sūtras generated a millennium of visual art — and what that art did.*

### 6.1 · The Taima Mandala and the Visualisation Curriculum

The **當麻曼荼羅** (*Taima Mandara*, Taima Mandala) is the founding image of Japanese Pure Land art — and one of the most complex devotional objects in the history of Buddhist material culture. Its traditional origin legend places its creation in 763 CE, woven overnight by the noblewoman **Chūjōhime** (中将姫) with the miraculous assistance of Amitābha himself, who appeared as an old woman and helped complete the work from lotus fibers gathered at a sacred pond. Historical analysis places its actual origins in Tang dynasty China — it is a woven textile mandala (*ori mandara*, 織曼荼羅) that arrived in Japan and was subsequently copied in paint for over a millennium, the original at Taimadera (當麻寺) now too fragile for display.

The mandala is a complete visual concordance of the *Contemplation Sūtra*, organised in three zones that correspond to the sūtra’s three major components:

**The central field** is Sukhāvatī in full splendor: Amitābha enthroned on a lotus at the centre, flanked by Avalokiteśvara (観音菩薩, *Kannon Bosatsu*) and Mahāsthāmaprāpta (大勢至菩薩, *Seishi Bosatsu*), surrounded by bodhisattvas, celestial musicians, jewel-trees, and lotus ponds. Every visual element corresponds to a passage in the *Larger Sūtra*: the seven-tiered railings, the four-colored lotuses, the pearl-nets over the ponds. The image is literally *readable* as scripture.

**The right border** shows the thirteen visualisation stages (*jūsan kan*, 十三観) in sequential panels from top to bottom — the meditator facing the setting sun, the solidifying of the water-vision, the lapis lazuli ground, the jewel-trees, the lotus throne, progressively toward the full vision of Amitābha’s body. This strip is the *method* — the contemplative curriculum that leads to the central vision.

**The left border** narrates the *Contemplation Sūtra*‘s frame story: Ajātaśatru (阿闍世) imprisoning his father, Vaidehī (韋提希) in her grief, the Buddha’s miraculous appearance, the teaching of the Pure Land. This strip is the *occasion* — the human suffering that makes the teaching necessary.

The mandala was not a wall decoration. It was a **contemplative instrument**: the practitioner sat before it and worked systematically from the border’s first stage inward toward the centre, using the painted image as a support for the sūtra’s sixteen contemplations. The *Taima Kōshiki* (当麻講式) ceremony — a liturgical enactment of the mandala’s content still performed at Taimadera — institutionalised this relationship between image, text, and community practice into a living ritual form.

The theological claim implicit in this arrangement is precise: the image does not represent something absent. It renders present what sincere contemplation makes accessible. The practitioner who has worked through the visualisation stages is not imagining Sukhāvatī — they are perceiving it, through the mind purified by the practice. Image and reality interpenetrate in the same way that, for Huáyán, all phenomena interpenetrate: the mandala is Sukhāvatī, conditionally, for a mind capable of receiving it as such.

-----

### 6.2 · Genshin’s *Ōjōyōshū* and the Deathbed Protocol

The **往生要集** (*Ōjōyōshū*, “Essentials of Birth in the Pure Land”), completed by the Tendai monk **Genshin** (源信, 942–1017 CE), is the hinge-text between Pure Land doctrine and Pure Land material culture. It transformed the abstract promise of the vow into a specific, embodied, communal practice for the dying — and in doing so, gave raigō painting its primary function.

The text opens — strategically — not with the Pure Land but with the hells. Genshin drew on Chinese and Indian sources to produce descriptions of extraordinary vividness:

> *“In the Saṃjīva Hell (等活地獄), the sinners kill each other with iron claws. When they have all fallen down as if dead, a voice from the sky cries ‘Revive!’ and they come back to life and repeat the suffering.”*

Each hell’s torments are calibrated to specific moral failures: liars have their tongues torn out and ploughed, adulterers are crushed between copper pillars of fire, the violent are dismembered on iron thorns. The taxonomy is didactic — the punishment mirrors and reveals the sin — but the purpose is motivational. Before the Pure Land can be genuinely desired, the alternative must be genuinely feared. Genshin’s hell-descriptions gave Japanese Buddhist artists the iconographic programme for the **地獄草紙** (*jigoku zōshi*, hell-scrolls) of the late Heian period — some of the most graphically violent images in the history of Asian art, functioning as the dark mirror of raigō’s luminosity.

The deathbed instructions that conclude the *Ōjōyōshū* are a practical ritual manual of striking specificity:

> *“When a person is dying, their associates should arrange their room carefully. Set up an image of Amitābha, facing west. In the image’s hand, attach a five-colored cord. Place the cord in the dying person’s hand, so that they hold it. The person should face west and contemplate the Buddha. Friends should chant the nembutsu on their behalf.”*

The **五色糸** (*goshikisen*, five-colored cord) — white, blue, yellow, red, black, corresponding to the five wisdoms of Vajrayāna iconography — stretched from the painted Amitābha’s hand to the dying person’s palm, making the Buddha’s promised reception physically tangible. The raigō painting was not hung on the wall for general viewing: it was positioned at the dying person’s eye level, west-facing, as the contemplative object for their final moments of consciousness.

Genshin’s most influential pastoral move was his reading of **Vaidehī** (韋提希). Where earlier Chinese exegetes had sometimes treated her as an advanced bodhisattva demonstrating practice for others, Genshin read her as an ordinary human being in extremity — her tears of blood (*tì xuè*, 涕血) the universal response to the discovery that human relationships can destroy themselves, that sons can imprison fathers, that love is no protection against violence. This identification of reader with Vaidehī gave Japanese Pure Land its characteristic pastoral register: a tradition that does not speak *about* suffering from a safe distance but from within it, for people currently drowning in it.

-----

### 6.3 · Kamakura Masterworks and the Art of Dying

The **Kamakura period** (鎌倉時代, 1185–1333 CE) produced the raigō tradition’s technical and iconographic masterworks — images of such quality that they have never been surpassed and rarely equalled. Two developments drove this: the institutional expansion of Pure Land practice through Hōnen’s and Shinran’s movements, which created unprecedented popular demand, and the concurrent flourishing of professional Buddhist painting workshops (*busshi*, 仏師, and *eshi*, 絵師) capable of meeting that demand at the highest level.

The supreme example is the **Chion-in triptych** (知恩院蔵・阿弥陀二十五菩薩来迎図) — a three-panel hanging scroll composition, approximately 210 × 210 cm per panel, depicting Amitābha’s descent with twenty-five bodhisattva attendants. The central panel shows Amitābha in dynamic downward motion — robes streaming, the *raigō-in* (来迎印) mudra explicit and urgent, figure slightly tilted as if mid-stride. The left and right panels show the twenty-five bodhisattvas: some bearing the lotus pedestal (*rendai*, 蓮台) that will receive the dying person’s consciousness, some playing celestial instruments (*biwa* 琵琶, *koto* 箏, *fue* 笛), all caught in expressions of concentrated, joyful purpose.

The technical achievement centres on **截金** (*kirikane*, cut-gold). Gold leaf was cut into strips as fine as human hair — sometimes geometrically patterned, sometimes free-form — and applied in multiple layers to the surface of robes, halos, and architectural elements. The result is a surface that does not merely reflect light but appears to generate it: as the viewer moves, the gold catches light from different angles, creating a continuous shimmer that mimics the *Contemplation Sūtra*‘s description of Amitābha’s body emitting “eighty-four thousand rays of light” from each of its eighty-four thousand marks. The theological claim is encoded in the material: gold is not aestheticisation but accuracy.

The **yamagoshi Amida** (山越阿弥陀, “mountain-crossing Amida”) type — developed in the late Heian and Kamakura periods — represents the tradition’s most distinctly Japanese iconographic innovation. In these images, Amitābha appears rising above or behind a mountain ridge, the upper half of his body visible, face turned downward toward the viewer with an expression of concentrated compassion. No Chinese or Indian precedent exists for this configuration.

The theological content of the yamagoshi form is threshold theology: the mountain is the boundary between this world’s perceptual field and the reality of Amitābha’s presence — not a geographical border but the horizon of ordinary consciousness. The Buddha does not descend *through* the sky but rises *over* the boundary, like the setting sun in reverse — consistent with his name’s etymology as “infinite light” (*amitābha*, अमिताभ). The practitioner’s contemplation is figured as an expansion of vision sufficient to see just over the horizon — and what is there is the face of the Buddha, already turned toward them.

The triptych format used in the Chion-in and related works created an **immersive deathbed environment** — the three panels hung around the dying person’s room on three sides, creating a 270-degree visual field of Amitābha’s approaching presence. The five-colored cord connected the dying person’s hand to the central panel’s Amitābha. The distinction between the room and the approaching Pure Land was engineered to dissolve. This is raigō painting at its functional apex: not art contemplated from a distance but a total sensory apparatus designed to make the promised moment real at the threshold of death.

## Section 7 · East Asian Buddhism in the Contemporary World

*What survives, what has been transformed, and what the tradition still has to say.*

### 7.1 · The Sinosphere Today: Syncretism as the Norm

The dominant form of Buddhism across the contemporary Sinosphere — China, Taiwan, Vietnam, Singapore, and diaspora communities worldwide — is not any single school but the Chan-Pure Land synthesis that Yǒngmíng Yánshòu formulated and Song dynasty institutional life solidified. Chinese Buddhist monasteries typically begin and end the day with nembutsu recitation, conduct Chan retreats (*chányī*, 禪七) for intensive meditation, and maintain the doctrinal position that these practices are complementary expressions of One Mind rather than competing alternatives. The sectarian map that appears clear in historical accounts is considerably blurrier on the ground.

The most globally significant contemporary institutional expression of Chinese Buddhism is **人間佛教** (*Rénjiān fójiào*, “Humanistic Buddhism”), a movement with roots in the reforming vision of **Taixu** (太虛, 1890–1947). Taixu argued that Buddhism had become too focused on ritual services for the dead and too withdrawn from social engagement — producing an institution that addressed the afterlife but neglected the living. His reform programme emphasised education, social welfare, and the reorientation of Buddhist practice toward the “Pure Land in the human realm” (*rénjiān jìngtǔ*, 人間淨土) — a this-worldly transformation rather than a post-mortem rebirth.

Taixu’s disciple **Hsing Yun** (星雲, 1927–2023) institutionalised this vision at unprecedented scale through **Fo Guang Shan** (佛光山, “Buddha’s Light Mountain”) — a global Buddhist organisation operating hundreds of temples across five continents, universities in Taiwan and Australia, a cable television network, and publishing houses in multiple languages. Hsing Yun’s Buddhism is lay-centred, educationally ambitious, politically engaged, and deliberately accessible — the Chan-Pure Land synthesis translated into a 21st-century organisational form.

**Korean Buddhism** presents a different picture. The **Jogye Order** (조계종, 曹溪宗) — founded in its current form after liberation from Japanese colonial rule — dominates Korean Buddhist institutional life with approximately 90% of Korean Buddhist temples and monasteries under its authority. Its doctrinal position is Seon (선종) as systematised by Jinul (지눌), with the hwadu (화두, “topic of inquiry”) — the Korean equivalent of the kōan — as the primary meditative practice. The Jogye Order has been a significant vector for the global spread of Korean Buddhism through the **Kwan Um School of Zen** founded by Seungsahn (숭산, 1927–2004) in the United States.

**Japanese Buddhism** is the most institutionally fragmented of the Sinosphere traditions — a consequence of both its medieval history of school differentiation and the disruptions of the Meiji period’s forced separation of Buddhism and Shinto (*shinbutsu bunri*, 神仏分離, 1868). The major schools — Jōdo Shinshū (浄土真宗), Sōtō (曹洞宗), Rinzai (臨済宗), Nichiren (日蓮宗), and the lay organisation Sōka Gakkai (創価学会) — exist in genuine institutional separation, with distinct liturgical cultures, doctrinal emphases, and social demographics.

### 7.2 · Western Reception and Its Discontents

The encounter between East Asian Buddhism and Western intellectual culture has produced extraordinary richness — and significant distortion. Understanding the distortions is as important as appreciating the richness.

**D.T. Suzuki** (鈴木大拙, 1870–1966) is the single most influential figure in the Western reception of East Asian Buddhism, and his influence is inseparable from the particular construction of Zen he presented. Suzuki’s Zen — developed across *Essays in Zen Buddhism* (1927, 1933, 1934) and *Zen and Japanese Culture* (1959) — emphasised: sudden awakening (*satori*, 悟り) as a universal psychological event; the anti-rational, iconoclastic quality of kōan practice; Zen’s purported compatibility with Western individualism and its supposed distance from institutional religion; and the aesthetic dimension of Zen’s influence on Japanese culture (tea ceremony, archery, swordsmanship, ink painting).

What Suzuki systematically de-emphasised: the monastic structure within which Zen practice is embedded; the hierarchical teacher-student relationship and its ethical demands; the Confucian social ethics that historically accompanied Zen institutions; the role of ritual, liturgy, and precept-observance in Chan/Zen life; and the theological sophistication of Chan’s engagement with Tiantai, Huayan, and Pure Land. The result was a “Zen” that resonated powerfully with Western Romanticism’s valorisation of individual transcendent experience — and that was, in important respects, a selective construction rather than a description.

Bernard Faure’s *The Rhetoric of Immediacy* (1991) remains the most rigorous scholarly critique of this construction, demonstrating how Chan’s claims to “directness” and “spontaneity” were themselves highly mediated cultural performances embedded in specific institutional and literary contexts. The kōan is not a naked encounter with reality; it is a sophisticated literary artefact embedded in a centuries-old pedagogical tradition with its own conventions, authorities, and institutional politics.

The **mindfulness movement** represents a different mode of Western Buddhist inheritance. Jon Kabat-Zinn’s Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR, 1979) drew primarily from Theravāda vipassanā — not from East Asian Chan — and deliberately bracketed the metaphysical and soteriological framework of Buddhist practice, retaining the attentional training while discarding doctrinal context. The result has been a clinical intervention of considerable efficacy for anxiety, depression, and chronic pain — and a practice that its Buddhist sources would recognise as partial to the point of misrepresentation. Stripping mindfulness of its ethical context (*śīla*, 戒), its wisdom orientation (*prajñā*, 般若), and its soteriological goal (*nirvāṇa*) produces something genuinely useful but also genuinely different from what any Buddhist school teaches.

The Pure Land tradition has faced particular challenges in Western transmission. Amitābha’s vow, the promise of rebirth in Sukhāvatī, and nembutsu recitation all map awkwardly onto Western categories: Is Amitābha a god? Is Pure Land rebirth heaven? Is nembutsu prayer? These categorical mappings produce systematic misunderstanding, since the doctrinal framework within which Amitābha functions — the Mahāyāna Buddha-field cosmology, the Tathāgatagarbha doctrine, the Mind-Only reading of the Pure Land — has no simple Western equivalent. The tradition has attracted a smaller Western convert community than Chan partly for this reason, though Jōdo Shinshū has maintained a significant presence in Japanese-American communities since the Meiji period.

### 7.3 · Open Questions and Research Frontiers

Three clusters of unresolved questions continue to generate productive scholarly debate and point toward significant future research.

The first concerns **Buddha-nature** (佛性, *fóxìng*) and the parallel East Asian version of the Tibetan Rangtong/Shentong debate. The Tibetan philosophical tradition debated whether ultimate reality is “empty of self” (*rangtong*, རང་སྟོང་) — the standard Mādhyamaka position — or “empty of other” (*shentong*, གཞན་སྟོང་) — meaning that ultimate reality is empty of all conditioned qualities but positively luminous in its own nature. An exactly parallel debate runs through East Asian Buddhist history. Does the One Mind (*yīxīn*, 一心) of the *Awakening of Faith* posit a positive luminous ground — a Buddhist equivalent of the Ātman that Mādhyamaka should in principle deny? Or is it simply a conventional designation for the absence of inherent existence? Taixu and his successors tended toward the positive reading; Yogācāra scholars insisted on the negative. The question has not been resolved and continues to structure contemporary Chinese Buddhist philosophical discussion.

The second cluster concerns **women’s roles** in East Asian Buddhist history. The institutional record is dominated by male monks and patriarchs — the lineage narratives of Chan, the scholarly traditions of Tiāntái, the priestly institutions of Pure Land. But women were primary patrons (Queen Vaidehī is the *Contemplation Sūtra*’s protagonist), primary practitioners of deathbed nembutsu, primary commissioners of raigō art, and primary members of nembutsu confraternities. The abbesses of major convent institutions — systematically underrepresented in the patriarchal lineage literature — sustained continuous traditions of practice and scholarship whose recovery is ongoing. **Miaozhı** (妙智, 12th c.) and **Zhiyuan Xinggang** (智緣行剛, 1597–1654) represent the kind of figures whose significance is only now being assessed in recent scholarship by scholars including Beata Grant.

The third frontier is **digital humanities**. The *Zongjing Lu*’s (宗鏡錄) 300+ citations constitute a snapshot of the Chinese Buddhist canon as it existed in the 10th century — which texts were available, which were considered authoritative, which were treated as complementary rather than competing. Network analysis of these citations could map the intellectual topology of Chinese Buddhism at its synthetic apex in ways that close reading cannot. The Chinese Buddhist Electronic Text Association (CBETA, 中華電子佛典協會) has digitised the full Chinese Buddhist canon; computational tools for citation analysis, conceptual mapping, and stylometric authorship attribution are now available that could address questions — including the apocrypha problem — that traditional philology cannot resolve alone.

## Conclusion · One Mind, Many Gates

East Asian Buddhism’s most original contribution is not any single doctrine — not the three-thousand-realms-in-one-thought of Tiāntái, not Huáyán’s Indra’s Net, not Chan’s sudden awakening, not Pure Land’s radical other-power. It is the *meta-doctrinal* claim, worked out most explicitly by Yǒngmíng Yánshòu but implicit in the tradition’s entire trajectory, that multiple valid gates to the same awakened reality can and should coexist.

This is not relativism — the claim that all paths are equally valid regardless of their content. Yánshòu was perfectly capable of distinguishing better from worse practice, more from less realised understanding. The Four Combinations verse makes clear distinctions: Chan with Pure Land is better than either alone; neither is better than nothing at all. The claim is rather that the diversity of valid approaches reflects a diversity in the conditions of practitioners — their karmic inheritances, cognitive styles, social locations, and spiritual maturity — that no single method can address.

This is the practical application of *upāya* (方便, skillful means) at the institutional level. If the Dharma must be adapted to the conditions of its recipients — as every Mahāyāna school agrees — then a tradition that offers only one approach is a tradition that reaches only one kind of person. The pluralism of East Asian Buddhism is not doctrinal confusion but pastoral intelligence: the recognition that awakening looks different when it arrives through the kōan’s sudden break, the nembutsu’s accumulated sincerity, the mandala’s contemplative absorption, and the zazen cushion’s silent insistence.

What unites these gates is not a doctrinal formula but an experience that each approach is, in its own way, attempting to point toward: the recognition that the awakened mind is not elsewhere, not future, not achieved — but here, in the present moment of experience, whether that moment is a teacup breaking, Amitābha’s face appearing at the moment of death, the jewel-trees singing the Dharma in the Pure Land wind, or simply sitting, just sitting, the question of practice and awakening already resolved in the sitting itself.

The tiger needs its horns.
