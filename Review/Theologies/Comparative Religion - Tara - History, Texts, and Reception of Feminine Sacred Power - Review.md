# Proposed Titles

1. **Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）in Buddhism: History, Texts, Reception, and Lived Meaning**
2. **From Savioress to Sacred System: Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）Across India, Tibet, and East Asia**
3. **Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）and the Making of Buddhist Feminine Sacred Power**

# Abstract

This review article examines Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）as a major Buddhist feminine savioress whose identity developed across texts, rituals, images, and regions. Rather than treating Tārā as a single static “goddess,” the article argues that she is better understood as a historically evolving religious formation. It first traces her emergence in early medieval India within networks of protective ritual, Avalokiteśvara（अवलोकितेश्वर／觀自在／觀音／観音【かんのん】）devotion, and wider tantric-goddess exchange. It then examines the cluster of scriptures and ritual texts that stabilized her cult, including *Tārā Who Protects from the Eight Dangers*, *Praise to Tārā with Twenty-One Verses of Homage*, and short dhāraṇī texts that made her devotion portable and repeatable.

The article next explores how Tārā’s forms multiplied through Green Tārā, White Tārā, color symbolism, and the Twenty-One Tārās. This multiplicity is interpreted not as inconsistency but as a ritual system that organizes different kinds of enlightened activity. Comparative analysis with Guanyin（觀音／観音【かんのん】）, Prajñāpāramitā（प्रज्ञापारमिता／般若波羅蜜多）, and Durgā（दुर्गा／杜爾迦）shows that Tārā occupies a distinctive place at the intersection of compassion, protection, wisdom, and feminine sacred power.

A major focus of the article is Tārā’s uneven regional reception. Although she was textually present in Chinese canonical and esoteric sources, she remained relatively marginal in China, Korea, and Japan compared with her centrality in Tibetan Buddhism. This contrast is used to show how Buddhist figures are reshaped by local devotional and institutional worlds. A comparison with Zhunti / Cundī（準提／准提／じゅんてい【準提】）further highlights how some Buddhist goddesses were more successfully localized in China than others.

The article concludes by examining the lived religious significance of Tārā. Devotion to her functions protectively, emotionally, ritually, and gender-symbolically, helping practitioners respond to fear and uncertainty while also offering an image of feminine awakened power. Tārā thus serves as an especially effective case study for understanding Buddhism as a changing and transregional religious tradition.

# 0 Introduction

## 0.1 Framing the topic

Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）is often introduced in simple terms as a Buddhist goddess of compassion, protection, or rescue. That description is not wrong, but it is incomplete. Tārā is better understood as a **layered Buddhist savioress** whose identity took shape across different historical settings, textual traditions, ritual systems, and regional cultures. In some contexts she appears as a swift responder to danger; in others she is a tantric deity with multiple forms and specialized ritual functions; elsewhere she is present in scripture but never becomes a central public cult figure. To study Tārā, then, is not only to study one deity. It is to examine how Buddhism creates, transmits, and reshapes sacred figures across time and space.

This makes Tārā an unusually useful subject for undergraduate study. She stands at the meeting point of several major questions in Buddhist studies: how Mahāyāna and tantric traditions developed, how gender functions in Buddhist religious imagination, how scriptures and ritual practices interact, and how one figure can be received very differently in Tibet, China, Korea, and Japan. Tārā also challenges any overly static picture of Buddhism. She shows that Buddhist traditions do not simply preserve fixed doctrines; they also produce evolving systems of devotion, ritual, and symbolic meaning.

At the same time, Tārā should not be treated as if she meant the same thing everywhere. In Tibetan Buddhism, especially, she became one of the most beloved and ritually central deities. In East Asia, by contrast, she was certainly transmitted and preserved in canonical and esoteric materials, but she remained relatively marginal compared with figures such as Guanyin（觀音／観音【かんのん】）. This uneven reception is not a minor detail. It is one of the main reasons Tārā is historically interesting. Her career shows that religious figures are not merely transmitted; they are **selectively adopted, reinterpreted, localized, or subordinated** within particular religious worlds.

A further reason Tārā matters is that she offers a strong case study in **Buddhist feminine sacrality**. She is neither simply a passive mother figure nor merely a decorative consort. Rather, she often appears as an active and powerful agent of rescue, healing, and liberation. Yet the presence of a powerful female Buddhist deity does not automatically mean that Buddhist institutions were socially egalitarian for women. Tārā therefore invites a more nuanced discussion of the relationship between gender symbolism and lived social reality.

## 0.2 Thesis and scope

This article argues that **Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）is best understood not as a single static Buddhist goddess, but as a historically evolving savioress whose meaning changed across texts, rituals, and regions; her different reception in Tibet and East Asia shows how Buddhist figures are reshaped by local devotional and institutional worlds**. This is a light thesis rather than a strongly polemical one. The goal is not to prove one controversial claim, but to organize the evidence in a clear way and show how apparently separate issues—history, scripture, iconography, reception, and lived practice—fit together.

The article has four main aims. First, it examines how Tārā emerged in the religious world of early medieval Indian Buddhism. Second, it surveys the main scriptures and ritual texts that shaped her development. Third, it considers how Tārā’s many forms, especially the better-known Green Tārā and White Tārā, became part of a larger symbolic and liturgical system. Fourth, it compares her reception across regions, paying particular attention to the contrast between Tibetan centrality and East Asian marginality. A final section considers what Tārā devotion does in lived practice, especially in psychological and gendered terms.

This scope requires some limits. The article does not attempt to cover every Tārā text, every lineage of the Twenty-One Tārās, or every regional variation in full detail. Nor does it offer a complete art-historical catalogue. Instead, it focuses on the main themes needed for an undergraduate review: formation in India, textual foundations, major forms, comparative perspectives, East Asian reception, and lived religious significance. Within those limits, the aim is to show both **continuity and variation**: continuity in Tārā’s association with rescue, compassion, and ritual efficacy, and variation in the ways communities understood and used her.

## 0.3 Key names and terms

Because Tārā moved across multiple linguistic and cultural worlds, terminology is especially important. The same figure may appear under Sanskrit, Tibetan, Chinese, or Japanese names, and these names sometimes carry different emphases. A short glossary at the outset helps prevent confusion.

Several of these terms require a brief comment. **Tārā（तारा）** and **Drolma（སྒྲོལ་མ་）** refer to the same broad figure, but the Tibetan term is embedded in a specific ritual and doctrinal world in which Tārā becomes especially central. **多羅菩薩** is the most common classical Chinese canonical rendering, while **度母** is more common in later Chinese Buddhist usage. Similarly, **Avalokiteśvara（अवलोकितेश्वर）**, **觀自在**, **觀音**, and **観音【かんのん】** are not four different figures but different linguistic forms and regional receptions of the same bodhisattva tradition.

Two further terms also frame this article. The first is **Mahāyāna**（महायान）, the broad current of Buddhism in which bodhisattvas and large scriptural developments became increasingly important. The second is **Vajrayāna**（वज्रयान）, the tantric or esoteric Buddhist world in which deity yoga, dhāraṇī, mantra, and ritual specialization became central. Tārā belongs to both of these worlds, but she becomes especially important in the latter. Understanding her therefore requires attention to both mainstream Mahāyāna devotion and tantric ritual developments.

This article uses these names not merely as labels, but as evidence of transmission. The movement from **तारा** to **སྒྲོལ་མ་**, from **多羅菩薩** to **度母**, and from **Avalokiteśvara** to **觀音／観音【かんのん】**, reflects the larger movement of Buddhism itself across regions and languages. Tārā’s history is therefore also a history of translation, adaptation, and reinterpretation.

In that sense, the study of Tārā is not a narrow study of one deity. It is a way into the broader question of how Buddhist traditions produce sacred figures that are at once textual, visual, ritual, emotional, and regional. The sections that follow examine that process step by step: first in India, then in scripture and iconography, then in comparison with other feminine sacred figures, and finally in the different devotional worlds of Tibet and East Asia.

# 1 Early Formation: How Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་） emerged in India

## 1.1 The religious world of early medieval Indian Buddhism

Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）did not appear fully formed at the beginning of Buddhism. The most cautious scholarly starting point is that her worship in India is securely traceable to **at least the sixth century CE**, after which she became increasingly prominent in the Buddhist pantheon. This places her rise within the religious world of **early medieval Indian Mahāyāna and emerging Vajrayāna**, a period marked by the growth of dhāraṇī, protective ritual, deity cults, and increasingly elaborate tantric forms of practice.

This context matters because Tārā makes more sense as the product of a changing Buddhist environment than as an isolated invention. By the early medieval period, Buddhist communities were already making heavy use of **portable ritual technologies**—short protective texts, recitations, visualizations, and images that could circulate beyond a single monastery or sacred site. Tārā’s later prominence fits this wider shift toward practices that were emotionally immediate, ritually efficient, and adaptable across communities. This is one reason she became especially successful as a savioress: she could be invoked quickly and repeatedly in situations of danger or uncertainty.

At the same time, Buddhism in India was not developing in isolation. From about the **600s onward**, tantric Buddhism evolved in close contact with Brahmanical tantric and goddess traditions. Tārā’s emergence should therefore be placed inside a larger world of experimentation, overlap, and mutual religious borrowing rather than imagined as a purely internal doctrinal development.

## 1.2 Tārā and Avalokiteśvara（अवलोकितेश्वर／觀自在／觀音／観音【かんのん】）

One of the strongest clues to Tārā’s early formation is her close relationship with **Avalokiteśvara（अवलोकितेश्वर）**, the bodhisattva of compassion. Later Buddhist tradition famously tells that Tārā arose from Avalokiteśvara’s tears, and although this myth should not be read as straightforward historical fact, it reveals how Buddhist communities understood her role: she was an extension or embodiment of active, responsive compassion. In this sense, Tārā can be read as compassion becoming more immediate, mobile, and personified in feminine form.

The visual evidence supports this connection. Early **Aṣṭamahābhaya Tārā** imagery, Tārā in her form as rescuer from the eight great perils, appears in conjunction with **Avalokiteśvara imagery in rock-cut sanctuaries in western India**. That conjunction is historically significant. It suggests that Tārā did not initially emerge as a wholly independent goddess-cult, but within an Avalokiteśvara-centered network of compassion, danger-aversion, and protection.

This does not mean Tārā was merely secondary. Rather, the Avalokiteśvara link may have helped her crystallize more quickly. A Buddhist world that already valued a compassionate savior bodhisattva could readily generate a feminine figure who responded with even greater immediacy to worldly fear. Tārā’s later reputation for swiftness and practical rescue may thus be seen as a specialized intensification of the broader Avalokiteśvara tradition.

## 1.3 Protection, travel, and the “eight dangers”

One of the earliest and most revealing features of Tārā is her role as a **protector from danger**, especially in the motif known as the **eight great perils**. These dangers include threats such as lions, snakes, thieves, fire, shipwreck, and rampaging elephants. The fact that this imagery appears early and prominently suggests that Tārā first gained traction not primarily as an abstract philosophical figure, but as a **practical savioress** whose value was felt in situations of risk.

This has often been linked to **merchants, including mariners**, who regularly faced such dangers and particularly venerated her. This opens an important historical possibility: Tārā’s early spread may have been tied to **commercial and travel networks**, including roads, ports, and other zones where portable protective religion was especially useful. A deity who rescues from shipwreck, thieves, and wilderness dangers is well suited to the needs of mobile communities.

This helps explain why Tārā became so transmissible. She could travel as a short recitation, a small image, a remembered praise, or a protective ritual. Unlike a deity tied only to one major temple or one highly localized myth, Tārā was adaptable. Her promise of rescue could move with monks, traders, pilgrims, and patrons. In this sense, the “eight dangers” motif is not just symbolic; it may preserve something of the social world in which her cult first gained momentum.

A further point is that the eight dangers later came to be interpreted both **externally and internally**. On one level they are literal dangers. On another, they can be read as metaphors for afflictions such as fear, anger, delusion, or attachment. This dual reading helps explain Tārā’s durability: she could respond both to the physical risks of travel and to the moral-psychological dangers emphasized by Buddhist soteriology.

| Early formation factor | Why it mattered for Tārā’s rise |
|---|---|
| Protective ritual culture | Created a ready-made Buddhist framework for danger-aversion and rescue |
| Avalokiteśvara networks | Linked Tārā to an already powerful compassion tradition |
| Merchant and maritime mobility | Helped spread a portable savioress cult across travel routes |
| Dhāraṇī and praise texts | Made devotion easy to transmit, memorize, and perform |
| Tantric expansion | Allowed Tārā to become ritually specialized and multiplied in form |

## 1.4 Historiography of origins

The historiography of Tārā’s origin has often been shaped by an overly simple question: **Was she originally Buddhist, or was she borrowed from Hindu goddess traditions?** Older scholarship frequently framed the issue in exactly these terms. The problem with that model is that it assumes traditions are discrete and that influence moves in a single direction. The religious world of early medieval India was much less tidy than that. Buddhist, Śaiva, and Śākta communities inhabited overlapping symbolic and ritual environments, and deities, images, and practices were constantly being adapted and reinterpreted.

A better explanation is a **networked formation model**. On this reading, Tārā emerged through the convergence of several streams: older Buddhist protection religion, Avalokiteśvara-centered compassion, the growth of tantric ritual, and the wider South Asian goddess field. This model does not deny influence from non-Buddhist traditions, but it also does not reduce Tārā to a simple borrowing. Instead, it sees her as a distinctively Buddhist reconfiguration of materials that were already circulating widely in the religious world of the time.

This approach also helps explain why Tārā later developed so differently across regions. If she was already a composite and flexible figure at the point of formation, then it is not surprising that Tibet, Nepal, and East Asia received and reshaped her in different ways. Her origin, in other words, was already **plural and adaptive**. That is why a review article should resist the temptation to search for one single moment when “the real Tārā” first appeared. It is more historically accurate to speak of a **process of crystallization** than a single act of creation.

A concise way to summarize Section 1 is this: Tārā first emerged in India not as a fixed doctrinal figure, but as a **Buddhist savioress shaped by protective ritual, compassion devotion, social mobility, and a shared tantric-goddess environment**. Her early formation was gradual, relational, and historically embedded—and that very flexibility helps explain her later success.

# 2 Textual Foundations: The scriptures and ritual texts of Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）

If Section 1 explained how Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）formed within the religious world of early medieval India, Section 2 asks a different question: **how did that emerging savioress become textually stable and ritually portable?** The answer lies in a cluster of scriptures, praises, and dhāraṇī texts that made Tārā reproducible across communities. These texts do not all do the same thing. Some present her as a rescuer from danger, some as a liturgical focus of praise, and some as a deity invoked through short recitation formulas. Together, however, they transformed Tārā from a developing cult figure into a figure with a recognizable **textual body**.

A useful way to read the Tārā corpus is not to ask which one text is “the” Tārā scripture, but to see how different genres cooperate. **Sūtra-like texts** frame Tārā within teaching scenes and moral instruction. **Praises** create devotional affect and liturgical repetition. **Dhāraṇī texts** make her quickly invocable in daily or crisis practice. **Ritual manuals** show how the deity is visualized, recited, and embodied in more esoteric settings. This layered textual ecology helps explain why Tārā could travel so successfully across regions and lineages.

| Text | Genre / function | Main contribution to Tārā’s development |
|---|---|---|
| *Tārā Who Protects from the Eight Dangers* | sūtra / protective teaching | Defines Tārā as savioress and moral teacher |
| *Praise to Tārā with Twenty-One Verses of Homage* | liturgical praise | Stabilizes the twenty-one-form devotional imagination |
| *The Dhāraṇī of Tārā* | short dhāraṇī | Makes Tārā ritually portable and easy to invoke |
| *The Dhāraṇī “Tārā’s Own Promise”* | dhāraṇī + benefit text | Gives devotional assurance and pledged efficacy |
| *The Hundred and Eight Names of the Goddess Tārā* | names text / praise | Multiplies and systematizes Tārā’s attributes |
| 《佛說聖多羅菩薩經》, 《金剛頂經多羅菩薩念誦法》 | Chinese canonical witnesses | Show transmission into East Asia and esoteric adaptation |

## 2.1 *Tārā Who Protects from the Eight Dangers*

Among the core Tārā texts, *Tārā Who Protects from the Eight Dangers* is especially important because it presents Tārā not only as a rescuer but also as a **teacher of Dharma**. The text warns against relying on mantra without **pure conduct**. That summary immediately shows that the text is doing more than promising magical safety. It links protection to ethics, conduct, and liberation.

This matters because it prevents us from misunderstanding Tārā as merely a Buddhist emergency deity. In this text, she addresses suffering at two levels at once. On the surface, she protects from concrete forms of danger. At a deeper level, she explains the wider structure of saṃsāra and insists that recitation without moral discipline is insufficient. In review-article terms, this text is one of the clearest witnesses to Tārā’s role as a **moralized savioress**: she saves, but she also instructs.

The “eight dangers” theme is also central to Tārā’s textual identity because it gives her a vivid and memorable profile. A deity linked to lions, snakes, thieves, fire, floods, and similar dangers is immediately legible to a wide audience. At the same time, later Buddhist readings frequently interpret these dangers inwardly as afflictions or states of delusion. This double readability—literal and symbolic—helps explain why the text remained useful across very different communities.

## 2.2 *Praise to Tārā with Twenty-One Verses of Homage*

If the “eight dangers” text makes Tārā morally and practically compelling, *Praise to Tārā with Twenty-One Verses of Homage* gives her a **liturgical and poetic body**. Its first twenty-one verses are homages to the twenty-one forms of Tārā, while the remaining verses explain how and when the praise should be recited and what benefits it brings. This is a key step in Tārā’s development, because it turns her from a deity with a few strong associations into a deity whose forms can be **systematically praised, repeated, memorized, and ritualized**.

The importance of this praise in Tibetan Buddhism is difficult to overstate. It is one of the most widely recited Tārā texts, and its influence reaches far beyond liturgy into iconography and meditation. The first twenty-one verses do not merely list names. They describe features, gestures, colors, powers, and emotional registers, allowing Tārā to become a **plural yet coherent** divine system. That is one reason later traditions could elaborate the Twenty-One Tārās so extensively.

There is also a conceptual shift here. In the “eight dangers” tradition, Tārā is strongly tied to rescue. In the Twenty-One Verses, she becomes more visibly a deity of **multiple activities**—peaceful, forceful, swift, fierce, beautiful, and sovereign. This text therefore expands the meaning of Tārā from a protective savioress into a broader liturgical complex. It is a major bridge between early protective devotion and later tantric multiplicity.

## 2.3 Dhāraṇī, mantra, and ritual portability

Short recitation texts such as *The Dhāraṇī of Tārā* and *The Dhāraṇī “Tārā’s Own Promise”* are crucial for understanding why Tārā spread so effectively. They invoke the deity Tārā for the purpose of dispelling obstacles and pacifying negative forces. This is a compact definition of ritual portability: a text short enough to be remembered, carried, performed, and reused in many situations.

The significance of dhāraṇī is not only brevity. Dhāraṇī texts make Tārā feel **close and immediately available**. They shorten the distance between devotee and deity. Instead of requiring a full narrative setting or complex doctrinal preparation, they allow a practitioner to invoke Tārā directly through formulaic speech. This is one of the main reasons Tārā became so adaptable across monasteries, households, and mobile religious settings.

*The Dhāraṇī “Tārā’s Own Promise”* intensifies this dynamic even further. Its core assurance is that “By merely recalling this dhāraṇī all dangers will be eliminated, all accomplishments will be attained.” This language of promise is especially important in lived religion, because it gives Tārā not just efficacy but **reliability**. She is presented as a deity whose help is pledged in advance. For a practitioner, this changes the emotional structure of devotion: Tārā is not only admirable, but dependable.

From the standpoint of Buddhist textual history, then, dhāraṇī is one of the main media through which Tārā became a transregional figure. It made her cult compact, repeatable, and affectively immediate. In that sense, dhāraṇī did not merely accompany Tārā devotion; it was one of the principal technologies by which that devotion was stabilized and disseminated.

## 2.4 Chinese canonical witnesses

The Tārā textual tradition also includes important **Chinese canonical witnesses**, even though Tārā never became a major public cult figure in East Asia. CBETA preserves texts such as **《佛說聖多羅菩薩經》** and **《金剛頂經多羅菩薩念誦法》**, demonstrating that Tārā entered the Chinese Buddhist canon under the name **多羅菩薩**. The opening of **《佛說聖多羅菩薩經》** begins with the standard sūtra formula “如是我聞,” showing that at least some Chinese Tārā materials were framed as proper Buddhist scripture rather than only as specialist ritual notes.

At the same time, **《金剛頂經多羅菩薩念誦法》** shows a different side of the tradition: esoteric ritual instruction. The text includes visualization language and ritual directions, indicating that Tārā in China was also preserved in **practice-manual form**, not only in scripture. This is highly significant for reception history. It means that East Asian Buddhism did not ignore Tārā; rather, it often received her through more specialized esoteric channels.

These Chinese materials matter for two reasons. First, they confirm that Tārā’s cult was sufficiently established to warrant translation and ritual adaptation in East Asia. Second, they illustrate a theme that will become important later in this review: **textual presence does not necessarily lead to broad social centrality**. Tārā entered the canon, but she did not develop in China the same mass devotional role that she later achieved in Tibet. The Chinese witnesses therefore serve both as proof of transmission and as evidence for the unevenness of Buddhist reception.

## Section takeaway

Taken together, these texts show how Tārā was stabilized through several complementary genres. The “eight dangers” scripture made her morally serious and practically vivid. The Twenty-One Verses made her liturgically memorable and multiplicative. The dhāraṇī texts made her portable, intimate, and dependable. The Chinese witnesses show that this textual complex traveled well beyond India, even where its social outcomes were different. In short, Tārā became a major Buddhist figure not because of one single scripture, but because a **cluster of texts created a repeatable religious system around her**.

# 3 Forms and Functions: Green Tārā, White Tārā, and the Twenty-One Tārās

If Section 2 showed how texts stabilized the cult of Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）, this section asks how those texts also helped **multiply her forms**. Tārā is not only one savioress but a religious system in which one deity can appear in many colors, moods, and functions. The textual basis for this development is especially clear in *Praise to Tārā with Twenty-One Verses of Homage*. The text does not just praise Tārā; it trains readers and practitioners to imagine her as **multiple yet coherent**.

This multiplicity should not be mistaken for doctrinal confusion. In tantric Buddhism, deities often appear in different forms because enlightened activity is understood to be flexible and situation-specific. The basic symbolic framework is that **white** corresponds to peaceful or pacifying activity, **yellow** to increasing or enriching activity, **red** to powerful or magnetizing activity, and **black** to wrathful or subjugating activity; **green** is often understood as combining these activities together. This color logic helps explain why Tārā could become many forms without losing her identity. She remains Tārā, but her mode of action changes according to ritual need.

## 3.1 Major forms of Tārā

In practice, the two most prominent forms are **Green Tārā** and **White Tārā**. Green Tārā is especially associated with all types of activity, while White Tārā is associated with longevity. Green Tārā is often treated as the **primary or default form** of Tārā in later Tibetan practice. Green is understood as the color of all activities together. This makes Green Tārā especially suitable as the form of **swift, general aid**: she is the Tārā most easily imagined as ready to rise, intervene, and respond. White Tārā, by contrast, is more commonly associated with peace, healing, merit, and long life.

For undergraduates, the most important point is that these two forms are not separate deities in the strong sense. They are better understood as **differentiated expressions of one enlightened feminine power**. Their distinction illustrates a general feature of tantric religious imagination: deities are often one and many at once. Tārā’s plurality is therefore part of her function, not a sign of instability.

| Form | Usual association | General activity | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green Tārā | swift aid, rescue, responsiveness | all activities / especially active intervention | most common general form of Tārā |
| White Tārā | longevity, healing, serenity | peaceful / pacifying | major long-life and protective form |
| Red Tārā | power, attraction, force | powerful / magnetizing | shows Tārā’s expansion beyond rescue into influence and mastery |
| Yellow Tārā | prosperity, increase, enrichment | increasing | links Tārā to merit, abundance, and flourishing |
| Black / dark-blue Tārā | fierce protection, obstacle removal | wrathful / subjugating | shows Tārā’s fierce side in tantric ritual systems |

## 3.2 Color symbolism and tantric functions

The symbolic connection between color and activity is one of the clearest ways tantric Buddhism organizes divine multiplicity. The four principal tantric activities are peaceful-white, increasing-yellow, powerful-red, and wrathful-black. These are not merely artistic choices. They encode a ritual theory in which enlightened power can pacify illness or conflict, increase prosperity or life, magnetize desirable conditions, or subdue dangerous obstacles. Tārā’s colored forms become legible within this system.

This is where Green Tārā becomes especially interesting. Because green is described as a combination of the four principal colors, she can be understood as the form of Tārā who encompasses **all activities together**. That symbolism helps explain her privileged status in later Tibetan devotion. She is not confined to one specific function but can respond broadly and quickly to changing needs. In practical terms, this makes Green Tārā the most flexible and devotional “entry point” into the wider Tārā system.

At the same time, students should avoid turning this into a rigid codebook. Color-activity correspondences are real and important, but actual iconography varies by lineage, sādhana, and historical period. It is more accurate to say that color symbolism gives us a **shared ritual grammar**, not a universally fixed master chart.

## 3.3 The Twenty-One Tārās

The most influential expansion of Tārā’s multiplicity is the cycle of the **Twenty-One Tārās**. The *Praise to Tārā with Twenty-One Verses of Homage* describes twenty-one forms in verse. There are at least three well-known lineages of the Twenty-One Tārās, including those associated with **Paṇḍita Sūryagupta**, **Lord Atīśa**, and the **Sādhanasamucchaya** tradition. This is important because it shows that “the Twenty-One Tārās” is not one perfectly fixed list shared identically by all Buddhist traditions. It is better understood as a **family of related enumerative systems**.

The Atīśa tradition is especially useful pedagogically because it shows how the twenty-one forms can be organized by color and function. In the Atīśa system all the Tārās share the same basic appearance and differ primarily in body color, and green—although central in broader Tārā practice—is not actually included in the enumeration of Atīśa’s twenty-one forms. That detail is important because it warns against assuming that the better-known Green Tārā simply sits inside every numbered twenty-one-form system. The historical reality is more complex.

The Twenty-One Tārās matter for more than classification. They convert Tārā from a single devotional figure into a **liturgical universe of specialized emanations**. Some forms pacify disease and fear; others increase merit or prosperity; still others act in more forceful or wrathful ways. By giving each form a specific verse, visual profile, and ritual energy, the tradition creates a modular system that can be adapted to diverse circumstances without abandoning the underlying unity of Tārā.

| Feature of the Twenty-One Tārās | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Twenty-one praise verses | creates a memorable liturgical structure |
| Multiple lineages | shows the system is important but not perfectly standardized |
| Color differentiation | ties forms to ritual activities and functions |
| Shared identity as Tārā | preserves unity across multiplicity |

## 3.4 Why multiplicity matters

The multiplicity of Tārā is not only a ritual convenience; it is a clue to how Buddhist deities grow historically. A single savioress associated with danger-aversion becomes a more elaborate symbolic system once texts, praises, and ritual manuals begin to differentiate her functions. Multiplicity allows Tārā to meet several religious needs at once: she can be a general object of devotion, a long-life deity, a fierce protector, a source of prosperity, or a highly specialized tantric form. This flexibility helps explain why Tārā became so enduring in Tibetan Buddhism and so adaptable across different ritual settings.

Multiplicity also reveals something important about Buddhist theology more broadly. It suggests that sacred figures are not always meant to be singular, psychologically unified “characters” in the modern sense. Instead, they often operate as **clusters of enlightened capacities**. Tārā is a particularly good example because her many forms remain visibly linked to one underlying identity while still developing their own distinctive ritual lives. For students, this helps bridge iconography and doctrine: multiple forms are not arbitrary artistic variations, but expressions of a larger theory of enlightened activity.

Finally, the growth of Tārā’s many forms prepares the ground for later comparative and regional questions. Once Tārā exists as a flexible system rather than only as one figure, she can be adopted differently by Tibet, Nepal, China, Korea, and Japan. Some traditions foreground Green and White Tārā; others preserve specific textual witnesses without a broad public cult. The many forms of Tārā therefore do not only reflect theological richness. They also make possible the varied patterns of reception that the next sections will examine.

# 4 Comparative Perspectives: Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）and other feminine sacred figures

If Section 3 showed how Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）became a system of differentiated forms and functions, this section asks a broader comparative question: **what kind of sacred figure is Tārā when placed alongside other major feminine figures in Buddhist and South Asian religious worlds?** Comparison is useful here not because these figures are interchangeable, but because each comparison clarifies a different aspect of Tārā’s identity. Set beside **Guanyin（觀音／観音【かんのん】）**, Tārā appears as a close analogue in compassion and rescue. Set beside **Prajñāpāramitā（प्रज्ञापारमिता／般若波羅蜜多）**, she appears as a more active and relational form of Buddhist feminine sacrality. Set beside **Durgā（दुर्गा／杜爾迦）**, she appears as a Buddhist protective power that overlaps with, but is not reducible to, the broader South Asian goddess world.

A good comparative method is to avoid flattening all four into “goddesses.” Tārā and Guanyin are close in devotional function, but Guanyin is the East Asian form of **Avalokiteśvara（अवलोकितेश्वर）**, who in China and Japan came to be worshipped in female or gender-ambiguous form. Prajñāpāramitā is not chiefly a rescuer in crisis, but the female personification of the **Perfection of Wisdom**, often called the **Mother of All Buddhas**. Durgā is a major Hindu goddess whose identity is tied to power, warfare, and the defeat of **Mahīṣāsura**. These distinctions matter because they show that feminine sacredness can be organized around very different religious logics: mercy, wisdom, rescue, sovereignty, or demon-slaying force.

| Figure | Religious setting | Basic status | Primary emphasis | Why the comparison matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་） | Mahāyāna / Vajrayāna Buddhism | savioress, enlightened feminine figure | rescue, protection, swift compassionate action | baseline figure for this article |
| Guanyin（觀音／観音【かんのん】） | East Asian Buddhism | form of Avalokiteśvara | compassion, hearing cries, salvation | closest devotional comparison to Tārā |
| Prajñāpāramitā（प्रज्ञापारमिता／般若波羅蜜多） | Mahāyāna Buddhism | personified wisdom | emptiness, wisdom, “Mother of All Buddhas” | closest doctrinal comparison to Tārā |
| Durgā（दुर्गा／杜爾迦） | Hinduism | supreme goddess / warrior-goddess | protection, power, demon-slaying | closest cross-religious goddess comparison |

## 4.1 Tārā and Guanyin（觀音／観音【かんのん】）

Among these comparisons, Tārā and Guanyin are the closest in **lived devotional function**. Both are figures to whom devotees turn in distress, both are associated with compassion and protection, and both are imagined as responding to human suffering in a direct and affectively intimate way. Guanyin is the East Asian form of Avalokiteśvara, worshipped in female or feminized form in China and Japan. That means the comparison is not accidental: both figures belong to the same broad religious field of compassionate salvation, even if they developed differently.

The difference lies in **regional destiny and theological structure**. Guanyin is not originally a separate female deity but a regional reception of Avalokiteśvara, whose gender in East Asia became increasingly feminine or ambiguous. Tārā, by contrast, appears as a more explicitly feminine Buddhist savioress from the outset of her mature cult. This gives Tārā a different kind of theological profile. Guanyin is a feminized bodhisattva of mercy; Tārā is a feminine savioress whose many forms organize ritual activity in especially tantric ways. Both can rescue, but Guanyin is more strongly associated with hearing cries and extending mercy, while Tārā is more strongly associated with **swift intervention** and the differentiation of activity into peaceful, increasing, powerful, and wrathful forms.

This comparison also helps explain the later contrast between Tibet and East Asia. Because Guanyin already filled the compassion-protection role so successfully in China, Korea, and Japan, Tārā never became as central there as she did in Tibetan Buddhism. In other words, Tārā and Guanyin are close enough functionally that one could overshadow the other in a given religious environment. That is one of the clearest examples of how comparison can illuminate reception history.

## 4.2 Tārā and Prajñāpāramitā（प्रज्ञापारमिता／般若波羅蜜多）

If Guanyin is Tārā’s closest comparison in **devotional practice**, Prajñāpāramitā is her closest comparison in **Buddhist feminine sacrality at the doctrinal level**. Prajñāpāramitā is the body of Mahāyāna sūtras and commentaries associated with emptiness and also the female personification of wisdom, sometimes called the **Mother of All Buddhas**. This already indicates a major difference from Tārā. Prajñāpāramitā is not primarily a savioress from worldly danger but the sacralization of transcendental wisdom itself.

That contrast is useful because it clarifies what kind of feminine figure Tārā is. Prajñāpāramitā represents wisdom, emptiness, and the generative matrix of buddhahood; Tārā represents responsiveness, rescue, and practical compassionate activity. One could say, cautiously, that **Prajñāpāramitā is wisdom as ground, while Tārā is compassion as movement**. Tārā is typically approached in prayer, praise, and ritual invocation. Prajñāpāramitā is approached more often through philosophical, scriptural, and contemplative frameworks, even when she is iconographically personified.

At the same time, the two figures share something important: both make room for **feminine sacrality within Buddhism** without reducing it to mere ornament or consorthood. For undergraduate readers, this comparison helps avoid a simplistic view in which Tārā is the only significant Buddhist feminine figure. Instead, Tārā and Prajñāpāramitā show two distinct but related modes of feminine sacred presence: one more devotional and active, the other more metaphysical and doctrinal.

## 4.3 Tārā and Durgā（दुर्गा／杜爾迦）

The comparison between Tārā and Durgā moves outside Buddhism and into the broader South Asian religious world. This is the most historically delicate comparison because it raises the question of cross-religious influence. Durgā is a goddess created by the male Hindu gods to slay the buffalo demon **Mahīṣāsura**. She is therefore not simply a compassionate helper but a **warrior-goddess of sovereign force**.

Tārā and Durgā do share significant features. Both are feminine protective powers. Both can be fierce as well as beneficent. Both are invoked against danger, obstruction, and threatening forces. Both also belong to traditions in which color, iconography, and multiple forms matter. These similarities are real. Yet the comparison becomes misleading if it ignores doctrinal context. Durgā belongs to a Hindu goddess theology in which she is a supreme or quasi-supreme embodiment of **śakti**, divine power. Tārā, by contrast, remains embedded in Buddhist cosmology, bodhisattva logic, mantra practice, and liberation theory. Even when she appears fierce, she is not primarily a demon-slaying warrior in the Durgā mode.

This comparison therefore supports a more careful historiographical conclusion. Tārā may well have formed in a religious world saturated with goddess symbolism and shared tantric imagery, but she should not be reduced to a Buddhist version of Durgā. It is better to say that Tārā emerged in conversation with a broader South Asian goddess environment while becoming a distinctly Buddhist savioress. This is exactly the kind of case in which comparison is useful as long as it avoids collapsing one tradition into another.

## Comparative takeaway

These three comparisons sharpen the article’s central argument. Tārā is like **Guanyin（觀音／観音【かんのん】）** in being a compassionate rescuer, but unlike Guanyin she became most central in Tibetan rather than East Asian Buddhism. She is like **Prajñāpāramitā（प्रज्ञापारमिता／般若波羅蜜多）** in embodying Buddhist feminine sacrality, but unlike Prajñāpāramitā she is more relational, protective, and ritually immediate. She is like **Durgā（दुर्गा／杜爾迦）** in manifesting feminine protective power, but unlike Durgā she remains firmly inside Buddhist soteriology and tantric practice. Comparison therefore does not blur Tārā’s identity; it helps define it more precisely.

# 5 East Asian Reception: Presence without centrality

If Section 4 clarified Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）by comparing her with other feminine sacred figures, this section asks a more regional question: **why did Tārā remain textually present but socially marginal in China, Korea, and Japan?** The answer is not that East Asian Buddhism ignored her. On the contrary, Tārā was translated, canonized, and ritually transmitted in East Asia. Chinese canonical sources preserve texts such as **《佛說聖多羅菩薩經》**, **《金剛頂經多羅菩薩念誦法》**, **《聖多羅菩薩一百八名陀羅尼經》**, and **《讚揚聖德多羅菩薩一百八名經》**, which show that Tārā entered the East Asian Buddhist textual world under names such as **多羅菩薩** and **聖多羅菩薩**. The issue, then, is not absence but **limited public centrality**.

This distinction between **textual presence** and **social centrality** is crucial. A deity may exist in translation, appear in ritual manuals, and even be represented in art without becoming a major public devotional center. In East Asia, this seems to have been Tārā’s fate. She was available in canon and practice, but she did not become a dominant mass savioress on the scale that she later did in Tibetan Buddhism. By contrast, in Tibet and related Himalayan traditions, Tārā became one of the most beloved and frequently invoked deities in Buddhism. Explaining this difference requires attention not just to doctrine, but to **institutions, devotional competition, and local religious adaptation**.

## 5.1 Tārā in China, Korea, and Japan

The first point to establish is that Tārā was genuinely transmitted into East Asia. Chinese sources preserve both scripture-like and ritual-manual forms of her cult. **《佛說聖多羅菩薩經》** shows that Tārā could be framed in a standard sūtra format, beginning with the familiar opening **「如是我聞」**, while **《金剛頂經多羅菩薩念誦法》** shows that she was also received through esoteric recitation and visualization practice. These materials indicate that Tārā entered East Asian Buddhism not merely as a name but as a usable ritual figure.

This East Asian reception, however, was uneven. In **China**, Tārā appears in the canon and in esoteric practice networks, but she never seems to have become a large-scale public cult comparable to **Guanyin（觀音）**. In **Japan**, Tārā was known mainly through esoteric transmission rather than broad popular devotion. In **Korea**, the same broad pattern seems to hold: textual and ritual presence, but much less evidence of a major public cult. This does not mean she was irrelevant. It means that she remained relatively **specialized and secondary** within the larger religious landscape of East Asian Buddhism.

For undergraduates, the key lesson is that Buddhist transmission is not all-or-nothing. A figure can cross linguistic and cultural boundaries successfully at the level of **text and ritual** while remaining minor at the level of **popular religious identity**. Tārā’s East Asian career is a good example of this intermediate level of reception. She was neither absent nor dominant; she was preserved, translated, and ritually available without becoming central.

| Region | Evidence of Tārā’s presence | General outcome |
|---|---|---|
| China | canonical texts, esoteric manuals, transliterated names such as 多羅菩薩 | textually present, but not a major mass public cult |
| Korea | broader East Asian esoteric transmission context | present but apparently limited in public centrality |
| Japan | esoteric Buddhist transmission and ritual knowledge | known, but much less central than 観音【かんのん】 |
| Tibet | widespread liturgy, praise, iconography, popular and monastic devotion | one of the most beloved Buddhist deities |

## 5.2 Why Tārā remained marginal in East Asia

The most important reason is likely **competition from Guanyin（觀音／観音【かんのん】）**. Avalokiteśvara became widely worshipped in China, Korea, and Japan in female or feminized form as Guanyin or Kannon. This matters because Guanyin already occupied the very religious niche that Tārā fills so effectively elsewhere: compassion, rescue, hearing cries, and aid in distress. In a religious environment where such a figure was already deeply established, Tārā had less room to emerge as an independent public savioress.

A second reason is **absorption into Avalokiteśvara-centered frameworks** rather than clear differentiation from them. Chinese catalog evidence preserves a title glossed as **觀自在多羅瑜伽念誦法** for a Tārā ritual text, showing how easily Tārā could be placed within a broader **觀自在** orbit. This kind of absorption matters historically. If a deity is transmitted mainly as part of another deity’s ritual network, it becomes harder for that deity to stand out as an independent public focus of worship. Tārā’s East Asian reception seems often to have worked this way: preserved, but within larger Avalokiteśvara-centered systems.

A third reason is the **institutional profile of East Asian esoteric Buddhism**. Many Tārā materials in Chinese and Japanese contexts belong to ritual manuals, dhāraṇī texts, and specialized esoteric traditions. That type of transmission can preserve a deity very effectively without necessarily making her a major lay devotional figure. By contrast, a deity embedded in broad temple worship, miracle narratives, and cross-sectarian popular devotion has a much better chance of becoming socially central. This helps explain why Tārā survived textually in East Asia while remaining more limited in public religious life.

A fourth reason is **local adaptation**. Some Buddhist figures entering China were not merely translated but deeply reworked to fit Chinese religious culture. Tārā does not seem to have undergone this process as successfully as some other figures did. She remained recognizably part of an imported esoteric repertoire rather than becoming one of the most fully localized public savioresses.

## 5.3 Tārā and Zhunti / Cundī（準提／准提／じゅんてい【準提】）

The comparison with **Zhunti / Cundī（準提／准提）** is especially useful because it highlights **two different destinies for Buddhist goddesses in China**. Both Tārā and Zhunti entered Chinese Buddhism through esoteric and dhāraṇī-based channels. Both are female Buddhist deities with ritual and visual dimensions. Yet their Chinese careers diverged sharply. Tārā remained textually present but socially secondary; Zhunti became much more **durable, adaptable, and localized** in Chinese ritual and devotional life.

This contrast helps clarify Tārā’s East Asian marginality. Tārā entered a Chinese Buddhist world where **Guanyin** already dominated the compassion-protection field. Zhunti, by contrast, seems to have had more room to be reconstructed as a distinct ritual figure. Her cult was especially well suited to mantra recitation, image worship, aspiration, and lay ritual use, all of which made her easier to integrate into Chinese temple and household religious life. Tārā had portability too, but in China that portability did not translate into the same broad social uptake.

The comparison therefore supports a broader argument about Buddhist reception. What matters is not only whether a figure is translated, but whether that figure can be **socially remade** in the new environment. Zhunti was repeatedly rebuilt within Chinese religious life; Tārā, by contrast, remained more strongly marked as a specialized imported figure, often overshadowed or absorbed by stronger local incumbents. This does not diminish her significance. It simply explains why her East Asian career looked so different from her Tibetan one.

| Dimension | Tārā in East Asia | Zhunti / Cundī in China |
|---|---|---|
| Mode of transmission | canonical and esoteric textual transmission | canonical, esoteric, and increasingly localized ritual transmission |
| Relation to major incumbents | overlapped heavily with 觀音／観音【かんのん】 | less directly blocked by a dominant equivalent |
| Public cultic outcome | present but relatively marginal | broader and more durable devotional integration |
| Historical pattern | preservation without large-scale expansion | repeated reconstruction and localization |

## Section takeaway

Tārā’s East Asian reception shows that Buddhist figures can be **successfully transmitted without becoming socially central**. She was preserved in Chinese canonical texts, translated into esoteric ritual worlds, and known across East Asia, but she did not develop into a dominant public savioress. The main reasons seem to be strong competition from **Guanyin（觀音／観音【かんのん】）**, partial absorption into Avalokiteśvara-centered systems, the specialist character of her esoteric transmission, and a weaker degree of successful local remaking than figures such as **Zhunti / Cundī（準提／准提）**. In this sense, Tārā’s East Asian career supports the article’s larger thesis: Buddhist sacred figures are not simply transported across regions; they are **reshaped by local devotional and institutional worlds**.

# 6 Lived Religion: What devotion to Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）does

If Section 5 explained why Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）was received differently across regions, this section asks a more intimate question: **what does devotion to Tārā actually do for practitioners?** In practice, Tārā devotion often functions as a form of **protection, emotional regulation, moral reorientation, and gendered religious imagination**. These functions overlap rather than appearing separately. Tārā is not simply believed in; she is also **used, felt, remembered, visualized, and inhabited**.

One reason this question matters is that Tārā’s importance cannot be explained by doctrine alone. A purely doctrinal description—“a female Buddhist savioress associated with compassion and rescue”—does not explain why she became one of the most beloved deities in Tibetan Buddhism or why her rituals remain compelling in daily life. To answer that, we have to look at how ritual and symbol meet human vulnerability. Tārā’s cult is especially strong wherever practitioners face danger, illness, uncertainty, or emotional instability and seek a form of help that is immediate but still morally and religiously meaningful.

| Function of Tārā devotion | What it does in lived practice |
|---|---|
| Protective | frames danger as answerable through ritual, prayer, and presence |
| Affective | helps regulate fear and distress through repetition and trust |
| Ethical | redirects crisis toward discipline, merit, and right conduct |
| Gendered-symbolic | offers an image of awakened feminine power and care |
| Transformative | supports identification with enlightened qualities through visualization and practice |

## 6.1 Protection and emotional regulation

At the most immediate level, devotion to Tārā gives practitioners a sense that **help is near**. The Tārā corpus repeatedly emphasizes swift response, danger-aversion, and practical rescue. The *Tārā Tantra* itself is framed around the **four activities**—peaceful, increasing, powerful, and wrathful—which already shows that Tārā is approached not only as an object of reverence but as a source of intervention in concrete situations.

The *Eight Dangers* tradition helps clarify the emotional logic of this devotion. A practitioner confronted by fear, illness, danger, or uncertainty does not simply endure those conditions passively. Through prayer, recitation, or visualization, such experiences are placed inside a meaningful religious pattern: danger becomes something that can be named, addressed, and ritually answered. This is one reason Tārā devotion can be described as a form of **emotional regulation**. The term is modern, but it fits the sources: fear is not denied, but transformed into a structured devotional response.

This does not mean that Tārā devotion is reducible to psychology. The practitioner is not merely “self-soothing.” Within the tradition, Tārā is real, responsive, and effective. But from an analytical perspective, the practice clearly also organizes emotion. It gives fear an object, a language, a rhythm, and a hoped-for response. That combination of **ritual repetition and trusted sacred presence** is one reason why Tārā remains so powerful in lived religion.

## 6.2 Gender and feminine awakening

Tārā is also important because she offers a particularly visible image of **feminine awakening** within Buddhism. In many religious traditions, powerful female figures are either maternal but passive, or powerful but frightening. Tārā complicates this binary. She can be nurturing, swift, fierce, protective, and enlightened all at once. This gives her a distinctive place in Buddhist religious imagination. She is not merely an adjunct to a male deity, but often a primary focus of devotion in her own right.

For practitioners, this can matter deeply. A female sacred figure can make certain possibilities imaginable that are otherwise less visible in institutional Buddhism. Tārā may function as a powerful counter-image to assumptions that spiritual authority is predominantly male. In that sense, devotion to Tārā can have a **symbolic and aspirational gender function**: she makes enlightened femininity ritually present.

At the same time, this symbolic function should not be romanticized. A central caution in gender studies of religion is that the presence of a powerful goddess or female deity does **not automatically produce social equality for women**. Tārā may be loved, praised, and ritually central while monastic and institutional structures remain male-dominated. This tension is one of the most important analytical points in studying her. Tārā can be empowering as a symbol and spiritually meaningful in lived devotion, yet still coexist with unequal social realities.

## 6.3 Ritual identification and transformation

A third major function of Tārā devotion is that it can be **transformative**, not just petitionary. In some forms of Buddhist devotion, the believer mainly asks for help from an external sacred figure. Tārā practice can certainly work that way. But in tantric settings it goes further. The practitioner may visualize Tārā in great detail, recite her mantra, and eventually identify with her form and qualities. The *Tārā Tantra* belongs to exactly this kind of religious world, where devotion, visualization, mantra, and transformation are intertwined.

This matters psychologically because it changes the structure of dependence. The practitioner is not only asking Tārā to act from outside; the practitioner is also learning to internalize and embody Tārā’s enlightened qualities. In modern analytical terms, this can be described as a movement from **attachment to identification**. First, Tārā is the one who helps. Then, through practice, Tārā becomes the image through which one trains compassion, courage, steadiness, and awakened action. This makes Tārā devotion more than crisis management. It becomes a discipline of reshaping the self.

That is also why Tārā devotion holds together such different registers of religion. It can be simple prayer, daily recitation, temple liturgy, monastic devotion, tantric visualization, or a highly personal source of reassurance. The same deity can operate across these levels because the underlying religious logic is flexible: Tārā is both someone to whom one turns and someone whose qualities one seeks to cultivate.

## Section takeaway

What devotion to Tārā does for practitioners can therefore be summarized in four linked ways. It offers **protection** by framing danger as answerable. It provides **emotional stability** by turning fear into structured ritual action. It offers a **gendered sacred image** in which awakened femininity becomes visible and compelling. And in tantric settings, it becomes **transformative**, allowing the practitioner not only to seek help from Tārā but also to become more like her through visualization and practice. The limitation, however, is equally important: symbolic feminine power does not automatically erase social inequality. Tārā’s lived power lies precisely in this combination of comfort, ritual efficacy, aspiration, and unresolved tension.

# 7 Why Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）matters as a case study in Buddhism

If Section 6 showed what devotion to Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）does in lived practice, this final body section steps back and asks a broader question: **why is Tārā such a useful case study for understanding Buddhism itself?** The answer is that Tārā brings together many of the major dimensions of Buddhist history in one figure. Through Tārā, one can study the formation of deities in India, the growth of Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna ritual systems, the circulation of scriptures and dhāraṇī, the interaction of text and image, the uneven reception of Buddhism across regions, and the role of feminine sacred figures in lived religion. Tārā is therefore not only an interesting deity; she is a compact window into the larger processes by which Buddhism changes over time.

One reason Tārā works so well as a case study is that she is both **continuous and variable**. Across the traditions discussed in this article, she remains recognizably linked to compassion, rescue, protection, and liberating activity. Yet the form that these ideas take changes sharply by region and genre. In Indian materials, she emerges through protective and dhāraṇī-centered networks. In Tibetan Buddhism, she becomes one of the most beloved liturgical and devotional figures, especially through the Twenty-One Tārās and the four activities. In East Asia, she remains canonically present but much less socially central than figures such as 觀音／観音【かんのん】. This combination of stability and variation makes her especially valuable for students trying to understand how Buddhist traditions transmit figures without preserving them in identical ways.

## 7.1 A figure shaped by movement across regions

Tārā’s history is, above all, a history of **movement**. She likely crystallized in early medieval India within networks of protective ritual, Avalokiteśvara devotion, and wider tantric-goddess exchange, and from there her cult expanded outward into the Himalayan world, Sri Lanka, and East Asia. That movement matters because it shows that Buddhist figures are not fixed at the moment of origin. As they travel, they are translated, reinterpreted, emphasized differently, or absorbed into preexisting religious structures. Tārā’s movement from India to Tibet and East Asia is therefore not a simple story of diffusion; it is a story of **transformation through transmission**.

This point is especially clear when Tibet and East Asia are placed side by side. In Tibet, Tārā became central. In China, Korea, and Japan, she was transmitted but usually remained secondary, in part because Guanyin（觀音）or Kannon（観音【かんのん】）already occupied the compassion-protection niche and in part because Tārā’s reception there often remained tied to esoteric textual and ritual channels. Comparing these outcomes makes an important methodological point for Buddhist studies: **successful transmission does not guarantee equal prominence everywhere**. Regional religious worlds actively sort, reshape, and rank what they receive.

This is also why Tārā helps students avoid a common mistake in the study of religion, namely the assumption that traditions spread as complete packages. Buddhism did not move across Asia in a single uniform form, and neither did Buddhist deities. Tārā’s different regional fortunes make this visible in a particularly concrete way. A figure can be central in one region, peripheral in another, and partially absorbed into another cult elsewhere, all while remaining recognizably the “same” figure at some level.

## 7.2 Text, image, and practice together

A second reason Tārā matters is that she shows how **texts, images, and practices** work together in Buddhism. The Tārā corpus is not limited to one kind of source. It includes scriptures such as *Tārā Who Protects from the Eight Dangers*, liturgical materials such as *Praise to Tārā with Twenty-One Verses of Homage*, dhāraṇī texts, ritual manuals, and Chinese canonical witnesses such as **《佛說聖多羅菩薩經》** and **《金剛頂經多羅菩薩念誦法》**. These texts do not simply describe belief; they actively create religious possibilities by giving practitioners words to recite, forms to imagine, and benefits to expect.

Images are equally important. Art-historical evidence shows Tārā appearing in specific iconographic forms, including early associations with Avalokiteśvara and later elaborations into Green Tārā, White Tārā, and larger twenty-one-form systems. These images are not just illustrations of texts. They are part of the same religious system, helping to stabilize and multiply Tārā’s identity. In Buddhism more broadly, text and image often work together to create devotion; Tārā is a particularly clear example because her visual plurality is so closely linked to liturgical and ritual differentiation.

Practice completes the triangle. Tārā is recited, praised, visualized, invoked, and sometimes ritually identified with. This means that Tārā is not best understood at the level of doctrine alone. She becomes fully visible only when one looks at how textual formulations, artistic representations, and concrete ritual actions reinforce one another. For undergraduate readers, this is one of the main lessons of the case study: Buddhism is not just a body of ideas preserved in books. It is a lived tradition in which text, image, and embodied practice continually generate one another.

| Image | iconography multiplies functions without dissolving identity |
| Ritual | prayer, recitation, and visualization make doctrine lived |

| Gender | feminine sacred power can be central symbolically yet uneven socially |

## 7.3 Revisiting the thesis

The article’s thesis has been that Tārā is best understood not as a single static Buddhist goddess, but as a **historically evolving savioress whose meaning changed across texts, rituals, and regions**. By this point, that claim should be clearer. Tārā’s early Indian formation was networked rather than singular. Her textual traditions stabilized her while also multiplying her forms. Her comparison with 觀音, Prajñāpāramitā（प्रज्ञापारमिता／般若波羅蜜多）, and Durgā（दुर्गा／杜爾迦）showed that she occupies a distinctive place at the intersection of compassion, protection, wisdom, and feminine sacred power. Her East Asian reception demonstrated that transmission does not necessarily lead to centrality. And her lived practice showed that devotion to her works psychologically, ritually, and symbolically in ways that doctrinal summaries alone cannot explain.

This makes Tārā a strong concluding case for the study of Buddhism because she resists reduction. She cannot be understood only as a text, only as a goddess, only as a tantric figure, or only as a symbol of femininity. She is all of these, but in changing combinations depending on context. That complexity is precisely why she matters. To study Tārā is to see Buddhism not as a frozen system, but as a tradition that continually reworks its figures through translation, ritual, comparison, and regional life.

In that sense, Tārā is a particularly effective undergraduate case study because she teaches two lessons at once. First, Buddhist traditions create continuity through names, texts, and inherited liturgies. Second, they create difference through local reception, institutional structures, and changing devotional needs. Tārā’s history shows that both processes happen at the same time. She remains Tārā, yet what Tārā means is never entirely fixed.

## Section takeaway

Tārā matters as a case study because she brings together the major themes of this article in one figure: historical formation, scriptural expansion, iconographic multiplicity, cross-regional reception, feminine sacred power, and lived ritual efficacy. She allows students to see how Buddhism produces continuity without uniformity and religious identity without rigidity. In that sense, Tārā is not just a topic within Buddhist studies. She is a model for how Buddhist traditions themselves work.

# Conclusion

## 8.1 Main findings

This article has argued that Tārā（तारा／སྒྲོལ་མ་）is best understood not as a single static Buddhist goddess, but as a **historically evolving savioress** whose meaning changed across texts, rituals, and regions. That argument began with her early formation in India, where Tārā appears to have crystallized within a religious environment shaped by **protective ritual, Avalokiteśvara（अवलोकितेश्वर／觀自在／觀音／観音【かんのん】）devotion, and wider tantric-goddess exchange**. From the start, then, Tārā was not simply an isolated figure. She emerged through networks of practice, image, and scripture that made rescue, compassion, and ritual efficacy central to her identity.

The article then showed how Tārā’s cult was stabilized and expanded through a cluster of texts rather than a single authoritative scripture. Texts such as *Tārā Who Protects from the Eight Dangers*, *Praise to Tārā with Twenty-One Verses of Homage*, and shorter dhāraṇī materials gave Tārā a recognizable textual body. These materials did not merely preserve devotion; they actively shaped it. They made Tārā portable, repeatable, and ritually available across communities. In that sense, textuality was not secondary to Tārā’s development. It was one of the main mechanisms by which she became a major Buddhist figure.

A further finding was that Tārā’s many forms are not signs of incoherence, but of **ritual and symbolic flexibility**. Green Tārā, White Tārā, and the broader systems of colored and enumerated Tārās show how Buddhist traditions use multiplicity to organize divine action. Tārā remains one figure, yet she can appear as healer, protector, rescuer, increaser, magnetizer, or fierce subduer of obstacles. This plurality helps explain her lasting religious appeal, especially in Tibetan Buddhism, where liturgical and visual systems allowed her to become one of the most beloved deities in the tradition.

Comparison with other feminine sacred figures clarified what is distinctive about Tārā. Compared with **Guanyin（觀音／観音【かんのん】）**, Tārā emerges as a close devotional analogue but with a more explicitly tantric and multiform profile. Compared with **Prajñāpāramitā（प्रज्ञापारमिता／般若波羅蜜多）**, she appears less as personified wisdom and more as active, relational, and practical compassion. Compared with **Durgā（दुर्गा／杜爾迦）**, she shares protective feminine power but remains firmly embedded in Buddhist soteriology rather than Hindu warrior-goddess theology. These comparisons showed that Tārā belongs to a wider world of feminine sacred figures while still retaining a specifically Buddhist identity.

One of the article’s most important findings concerned **regional reception**. Tārā was certainly transmitted into East Asia and preserved in Chinese canonical and esoteric materials, yet she remained relatively marginal in China, Korea, and Japan compared with her role in Tibet. This uneven reception supports the article’s central thesis: religious figures are not merely transported from one region to another; they are **ranked, absorbed, localized, or sidelined** by the devotional and institutional worlds into which they move. In East Asia, the strong presence of **Guanyin（觀音／観音【かんのん】）**, the specialist character of esoteric transmission, and the limited social remaking of Tārā all contributed to her secondary place.

Finally, the article argued that Tārā matters not only as a historical or textual figure, but as a figure of **lived religion**. Devotion to Tārā helps practitioners respond to fear, illness, danger, and uncertainty by giving those experiences ritual form. It also provides an important image of feminine awakened power, even if symbolic centrality does not automatically translate into social equality for women in Buddhist institutions. Tārā therefore occupies a particularly rich intersection of ritual efficacy, emotional consolation, gendered symbolism, and transformative aspiration.

|---|---|
| Early formation | Tārā emerged through networks of protection, compassion, and tantric exchange |
| Texts | A cluster of scriptures and dhāraṇī made her cult stable and portable |
| Forms | Multiplicity expresses differentiated enlightened activity, not confusion |
| Comparison | Tārā is best understood through contrast with Guanyin, Prajñāpāramitā, and Durgā |
| Reception | Transmission across Asia led to very uneven regional outcomes |
| Lived practice | Tārā functions protectively, emotionally, ritually, and gender-symbolically |

## 8.2 Final reflection

Tārā is a valuable subject for undergraduate study because she demonstrates something fundamental about Buddhism: **Buddhist traditions change by preserving and transforming at the same time**. The same figure can move across languages, scriptures, ritual systems, and regions while remaining recognizable yet never entirely identical to herself. Tārā’s history is therefore not just the story of one deity. It is the story of how Buddhism works as a transregional religious tradition.

She also helps correct simplistic assumptions about religion. She shows that deities are not just theological ideas but also ritual presences, emotional resources, visual forms, and historical products. She shows that feminine sacred power in Buddhism is real and influential, yet not automatically socially liberating. And she shows that canonical presence does not guarantee popular centrality. All of these lessons make Tārā a particularly strong case study for understanding religion as something lived, transmitted, negotiated, and remade.

In the end, Tārā matters because she holds together many levels of Buddhist life at once: **scripture and practice, image and devotion, gender and authority, continuity and change**. To follow Tārā across India, Tibet, and East Asia is to see Buddhism not as a fixed inheritance, but as a dynamic tradition that continually reimagines its sacred figures in response to new communities, needs, and worlds.
