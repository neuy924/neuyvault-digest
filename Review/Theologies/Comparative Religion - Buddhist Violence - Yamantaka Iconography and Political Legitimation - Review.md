## Abstract

This review examines how Buddhist traditions negotiate the tension between a strong anti-killing ethic and the presence of wrathful imagery, doctrinal exception, and violent political history. It argues that the central analytical problem is not violent Buddhist iconography in itself. Wrathful forms such as Yamāntaka are better understood first as ritual and doctrinal languages of transformation, in which death, fear, and obstruction are confronted through symbolic force rather than straightforwardly endorsed as literal violence. The more difficult problem emerges when symbolic violence and ethical exception are translated into public authority.

The article therefore brings three literatures into conversation: scholarship on wrathful iconography and ritual semiotics; work on Buddhist ethics, especially the anti-killing baseline, upāya, and compassionate violence; and political-historical studies of Buddhist nationalism, militarism, and state formation. It argues that Buddhist violence becomes historically intelligible not simply when traditions depict wrath or preserve exceptional doctrines, but when those symbolic and doctrinal resources are mobilized to authorize protection, sovereignty, threatened identity, and coercive order.

A comparative reading of Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Japan, and Tibet yields a provisional typology of Buddhist violence: majoritarian nationalist protection, institutional complicity with sovereign militarism, clerical-state coercive order, and religious repression coupled with resistant sacralization. The review concludes that “Buddhist violence” is best treated not as a single contradiction or essence, but as a family of political formations produced through different mechanisms of legitimation.

## 1. Introduction: Buddhist non-harm and the problem of violence

Buddhism is still widely presented, in both public discourse and some older scholarly habits, as a religious tradition defined above all by non-harm, renunciation, and compassion. That baseline is not invented: Buddhist ethics does place unusual weight on restraint, and scholarship on Indian Buddhist ethics continues to treat abstention from killing as a central normative commitment. At the same time, that picture is incomplete. Buddhist traditions also preserve a dense archive of wrathful imagery, ritual technologies of subjugation, doctrinal discussions of exceptional action, and historical entanglements with warfare, statecraft, punishment, empire, and majoritarian politics. The problem is therefore not whether Buddhism is “really” peaceful or “secretly” violent. The better question is how a tradition with a strong anti-killing norm has also generated symbolic, doctrinal, and institutional forms that can be connected to violence without collapsing into simple contradiction.

This review argues that the central analytical problem is not wrathful iconography by itself. Violent-looking Buddhist imagery is often best read, within its ritual and doctrinal settings, as a controlled symbolic language for confronting death, fear, impermanence, and obstructive mental states rather than as a straightforward endorsement of cruelty. The more difficult problem emerges later, at the point where symbolic force and doctrinal exception are translated into public authority. Put differently, Buddhist violence becomes historically intelligible not simply when traditions depict wrath, but when religious symbols, ethical exceptions, and institutional claims are mobilized to authorize protection, punishment, exclusion, or war.

This article therefore proceeds from ritual semiotics to political theology. It begins from wrathful iconography, using Yamāntaka as an entry point, not because Yamāntaka explains all Buddhist violence, but because wrathful form offers a concentrated site where non-harm, terror, transformation, and religious authority are held in tension. From there, the review turns to the anti-killing baseline in Buddhist ethics, then to Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna exception discourses around compassionate violence, and finally to the political histories of Sri Lanka, Japan, Tibet, and Myanmar. The governing claim is that the decisive issue is not visual violence alone, but the mechanism by which symbolic and doctrinal exception acquire collective legitimacy.

### 1.1 The historiographic problem

The historiography has often been distorted in two opposite ways. On one side is the apologetic tendency to treat violence in Buddhist contexts as accidental, external, or evidence of decline from “true Buddhism.” On the other is the revisionist temptation to overcorrect by presenting Buddhist violence as proof that Buddhism is no different from any other religious or political formation. Neither move is analytically satisfying. There is no single definitive account of Buddhism and violence as a general topic, but there is now a substantial body of case-based scholarship that makes denial untenable while also resisting crude generalization.

A second historiographic difficulty is categorical. “Buddhist violence” is not one thing. The political forms at issue differ sharply across regions and periods: majoritarian ethnocracy in Sri Lanka, anti-Muslim religious nationalism in Myanmar, institutional complicity with imperial militarism in Japan, and a more complex combination of clerical-state formation, sectarian conflict, and later state repression in Tibet. These distinctions matter because without them “Buddhist violence” becomes a vague moral label rather than a usable comparative category.

A third problem is methodological. Discussions of Buddhist violence often jump too quickly from text or image to event. Wrathful deities, skull imagery, and ritual subjugation are treated either as harmless esoterica or as transparent evidence of latent violence. Both readings are too direct. Violent or grotesque elements in Himalayan Buddhist art operate as symbolic tools within a learned visual language. That does not make them politically innocent. It does mean, however, that the route from iconography to violence is mediated rather than automatic. A serious account needs an intermediate level of analysis: legitimation.

### 1.2 Review aims and structure

The aim of this review is to synthesize three literatures that are too often kept apart: first, scholarship on wrathful iconography and ritual semiotics; second, Buddhist ethical literature on non-harm, killing, and compassionate exception; and third, political-historical work on Buddhist nationalism, militarism, and religious legitimation. The article does not attempt a comprehensive history of violence in all Buddhist traditions. Nor does it offer a full taxonomy of Buddhist ethics. Its narrower purpose is to show how these literatures illuminate one another when read through the problem of translation: how symbolic violence, doctrinal exception, and institutional authority become connected.

The review’s central claim is that wrathful imagery is not, in itself, the decisive scandal. Buddhist traditions can and do interpret wrathful figures as ritualized forms of transformed wisdom, fierce compassion, or pedagogical shock. The deeper problem appears when symbolic force is redescribed as political necessity, when elite doctrinal exceptions become collective mandates, or when threatened religious community is made to ground projects of sovereignty and exclusion. This is where a political-theological lens becomes useful: not to import an alien framework, but to identify the passage by which theological-symbolic material becomes public authority.

The article is organized in six further sections. Section 2 examines wrathful iconography as ritual and doctrinal language, with Yamāntaka as the opening case. Section 3 reconstructs the anti-killing baseline in Buddhist ethics. Section 4 reviews Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna discussions of compassionate violence and exception. Section 5 develops the problem of legitimation and the political translation of religious exception. Section 6 applies that framework comparatively to Sri Lanka, Japan, Tibet, and Myanmar. Section 7 concludes by proposing a provisional typology of Buddhist violence and identifying three gaps for further research: lived reception of wrathful imagery, the doctrine-to-institution pipeline, and stronger comparative models.

## 2. Wrathful iconography as ritual and doctrinal language

Wrathful Buddhist imagery is often treated, especially in non-specialist discourse, as either an embarrassment to be explained away or a hidden truth that exposes Buddhism as no less violent than other traditions. Both readings are crude. Current museum and art-historical scholarship instead treats wrathful iconography as a learned visual language whose frightening features—fangs, flames, skulls, severed heads, trampling figures, flayed skins, and proliferating limbs—operate within ritual and doctrinal systems rather than as transparent endorsements of literal aggression. The question is therefore not whether these images look violent; they plainly do. The question is what kind of work that visual violence performs within Buddhist worlds.

At the most general level, wrathful form condenses several functions at once. It marks the deity as operating beyond ordinary human or divine decorum; it materializes a confrontation with death, decay, and fear; and it presents awakening not only as serenity but also as force capable of destroying obstacles. The visual grammar is thus not incidental ornament but a semiotic field in which terror, protection, transformation, and authority are tightly linked.

This matters methodologically because iconography sits between doctrine and practice. Wrathful images are not merely illustrations of texts, nor are they reducible to aesthetic style. They function as mnemonic condensations of cosmology, meditation, ritual hierarchy, and soteriology. In tantric contexts especially, visual form is tied to deity yoga, mandalic space, ritual implements, and controlled visualization. The image, then, is part of a ritual technology rather than a free-standing picture of sacralized violence.

### 2.1 Yamāntaka as an entry point

Yamāntaka, more specifically Vajrabhairava, offers an unusually concentrated example of wrathful semiotics. In standard forms he appears with nine faces, thirty-four hands, and sixteen legs, with the principal face that of a buffalo and an upper face identified with Mañjuśrī. The buffalo head is not an arbitrary monstrous flourish: it links the deity to the domain of Yama, lord of death, while the upper Mañjuśrī head identifies the figure’s deeper identity as wisdom rather than demonic force. Even at the level of composition, the image makes a claim: death is confronted and subjugated not by brute power alone but by awakened knowledge.

The deity’s multiplied limbs and faces amplify this claim. In iconographic terms, multiplicity signifies more than supernatural excess. The many heads expand modes of perception; the many arms display a repertoire of implements and capacities; the many legs communicate unstoppable dominance over obstacles. What is represented is not mere ferocity but an overdetermined, totalized agency adequate to the destruction of ignorance and death-bound delusion.

The subsidiary elements intensify the same logic. Vajrabhairava commonly bears a curved knife and skull cup, sometimes an outstretched elephant hide, and a necklace of freshly severed heads. Read together, these motifs organize a visual discourse of ego-destruction, mortality-awareness, and transformational violence: the head is severed, the skin is stripped, the body becomes a sign of impermanence, and the implements of destruction are re-coded as instruments of liberation.

Equally important is the charnel-ground setting in which such forms often appear. Charnel symbolism relocates the practitioner from ordinary social space into an environment where impermanence is stripped of euphemism. Yamāntaka therefore serves as an especially useful analytic entry point because his iconography brings together the three basic poles of wrathful Buddhist art: confrontation with death, destruction of ignorance, and the authority of enlightened force.

### 2.2 The functions of wrath

Wrath in this iconographic register should not be confused with ordinary anger. Wrathful or forceful forms are not depictions of unpurified hatred, but symbolic representations of fierce compassion, pristine awareness, or forceful wisdom. The visual aggression matters precisely because it is meant to be read as transformed affect. In other words, the image does not abolish violence; it displaces it from the plane of ordinary emotion to the plane of ritualized, doctrinally authorized force.

One major function of wrath is pedagogical shock. The imagery interrupts pious expectations that religion should always appear mild, consoling, or aesthetically orderly. Flaming hair, exposed fangs, skull crowns, and trampling bodies produce a deliberate estrangement effect. They compel the viewer or practitioner to confront fear, disgust, mortality, and aversion rather than evade them. In this sense, wrathful iconography works as a visual discipline against denial. It stages the truth that saṃsāra is not merely disappointing but terrifying, unstable, and saturated with death.

A second function is ritual transformation. Within tantric settings, wrathful form is not only observed; it is visualized, inhabited, and used. The wrathful body is not only an object of interpretation but a template for subject-formation. The practitioner is trained to identify with a form that has already conquered terror, already metabolized death, and already transformed destructive force into awakened method. Wrath, in this register, is less a statement about others than a disciplined reconfiguration of self-experience.

A third function is protective and apotropaic. Wrathful deities guard thresholds, protector chapels, and ritual space; they also stand for the force required to repel interference and subdue hostile powers. Frightening appearance is itself part of efficacy, and trampling figures signify overcome obstacles. Even where Yamāntaka is not simply a protector deity in the narrower sense, he participates in the same broader logic of terrifying form as defensive power. Wrath thereby becomes legible as a visual technology of boundary-making, one that marks sacred space as protected precisely through a display of overwhelming force.

The analytical payoff is that wrathful iconography should be read neither as harmless decoration nor as straightforward proof of latent militancy. It is better understood as a ritual-doctrinal language of transformed violence: violence displaced into symbol, discipline, visualization, and sacral power. That language is not politically innocent, but its political significance is indirect. The crucial issue is not that Buddhism contains frightening images. It is that symbolic force can later be redescribed as institutional or collective mandate.

## 3. The anti-killing baseline in Buddhist ethics

Any account of Buddhist violence that begins from wrathful imagery alone will misstate the tradition’s ethical center of gravity. Across Buddhist traditions, the prohibition on killing is not marginal. It sits close to the core of both lay and monastic moral formation, and modern scholarship continues to treat abstention from taking life as one of the clearest common ethical commitments running across Buddhist worlds.

This baseline matters for two reasons. First, it blocks the lazy inference that wrathful imagery reveals a hidden pro-violence core. Second, it clarifies what later exception-discourses are actually exceptional to. If Buddhism had no robust anti-killing norm, then discussions of compassionate violence, political protection, or forceful subjugation would not generate real doctrinal tension. They would be ordinary extensions of a permissive ethic. The fact that they do generate tension is itself evidence that the anti-killing baseline is real.

### 3.1 The centrality of ahiṃsā and the first precept

The most visible expression of this baseline is the first precept, usually rendered as refraining from taking life. In Buddhist ethics, this is not merely a social rule against homicide in the narrow legal sense. It is a discipline of non-harm that extends across sentient life and is bound up with compassion, restraint, and karmic causation. The broader Indic concept of ahiṃsā—non-harm or non-injury—helps name this orientation, but in Buddhist usage the concern is not simply passive harmlessness. It is the deliberate refusal to cultivate or enact lethal intention.

Buddhist moral reasoning strongly urges the minimization of harm. In early Buddhist terms, there is no obvious route by which killing in war becomes wholesome. This does not mean Buddhist societies never went to war; they clearly did. It means that the ethical grammar of the tradition does not easily redescribe killing as straightforward moral good.

This emphasis on intention is crucial. Buddhist ethics is not exhausted by rule-following; it is a discipline of mind. The seriousness of killing lies not only in the external result but in the mental act that wills the death of a sentient being. To kill is not merely to violate a rule but to crystallize hatred, greed, fear, or delusion into action.

The anti-killing baseline is therefore stronger than a superficial reading might suggest. It is embedded in lay morality, monastic discipline, compassion for sentient beings, karmic theory, and the broader soteriological project of reducing suffering. Even when Buddhist traditions disagree about hard cases, they do so against this normative background.

### 3.2 Why the contradiction is only partial

If the anti-killing baseline is real, why do wrathful imagery, ritual subjugation, and later exception-discourses not simply collapse into contradiction? The answer is that Buddhist traditions distinguish between ordinary violence rooted in affliction and symbolic, ritual, or forceful forms that are not automatically equated with hatred. This distinction does not eliminate the problem, but it explains why the tradition can preserve terrifying images and even discuss forceful action without immediately abandoning its normative commitment to non-harm.

At the ethical level, the decisive distinction is usually between killing as unwholesome intention and other forms of force or symbolic violence that do not necessarily instantiate the same mental state. This is why wrathful iconography can be interpreted, from within tantric and wider Mahāyāna frameworks, as confronting ignorance, fear, or obstruction rather than expressing ordinary rage. The contradiction is therefore only partial: Buddhism does condemn killing and hatred, but it does not reduce all frightening or forceful forms to those categories.

That distinction, however, should not be romanticized. It is analytically useful because it explains the internal logic of Buddhist ethical discourse. It is also unstable, because once the tradition permits a category of “force without hatred” or “exception without moral corruption,” it opens the door to contested claims about who may act, under what authority, and with what karmic consequences. In other words, the anti-killing baseline remains normatively central, but it is not absolute in every later Buddhist discourse.

## 4. Exception and compassionate violence

If Section 3 established the anti-killing baseline of Buddhist ethics, this section addresses the pressure placed upon that baseline by doctrines of exception. The problem is not marginal. Once Buddhist ethics becomes strongly intention-sensitive, and once compassion is elevated as a dominant moral value, a difficult question emerges: can violence ever be justified if performed without hatred and for the sake of preventing greater suffering? Modern scholarship has shown that the answer, within parts of Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna literature, is not simply “no.” The literature does not uniformly suspend the prohibition on killing, but it does open conceptual space for a narrow category of compassionate violence, usually reserved for highly realized beings and framed as extraordinary rather than ordinary conduct.

The importance of this literature is twofold. First, it demonstrates that Buddhist ethics cannot be reduced to an uncomplicated pacifism. Second, it shows that the real issue is not whether Buddhist traditions contain exceptions—they do—but how those exceptions are formulated, constrained, and later expanded. The transition from anti-killing norm to compassionate exception is where the ethical problem becomes unstable. At that point, questions of realization, authority, karmic consequence, and political abuse become unavoidable.

### 4.1 Mahāyāna exception structures

The most important conceptual mechanism here is upāya-kauśalya, skillful means. In broad terms, upāya allows that teachings and actions may be adapted to circumstance in order to benefit beings. In ethical literature, this can generate a strong paternalist logic: what is normally prohibited may, under rare conditions, be permitted if it is the only effective means of preventing catastrophic harm or advancing liberation.

The classic narrative is the ship’s captain story from the Upāyakauśalya Sūtra. A bodhisattva-captain learns that a robber intends to murder five hundred merchants. He kills the robber, not from hatred, but to save the merchants and also to spare the would-be killer from the terrible karmic consequences of mass murder. This story matters because it does more than recognize tragic necessity. It constructs an argument in which killing may be understood as an act of compassion under very special conditions.

Such allowances are not isolated anomalies confined to fringe texts. Compassionate violence appears across major Mahāyāna scriptures and among major thinkers. This means that the discourse of exception cannot be pushed to the margins as a late corruption or merely tantric distortion. It is woven into major strands of Mahāyāna thought, even if always under conditions of restriction and idealization.

At the same time, Mahāyāna exception theory is not a blank permission structure. It is usually tied to demanding conditions: extraordinary compassion, non-hatred, advanced realization, and insight into karmic consequences. The exceptional agent is not the ordinary believer or ruler but the bodhisattva whose knowledge and motivation are presumed to exceed normal human limits. This is ethically significant because the doctrine survives only by radically narrowing who may qualify.

### 4.2 Vajrayāna intensification and ritualization

Vajrayāna does not invent the logic of compassionate violence, but it intensifies and ritualizes it. Where Mahāyāna texts often present exceptional violence narratively or philosophically, tantra embeds forceful compassion in deity imagery, ritual technologies, and transgressive symbolic forms.

This intensification takes several forms. First, wrathful deities such as Yamāntaka present enlightened activity in terrifying form, making subjugation and destruction central visual tropes. Second, tantric ritual can involve symbolic violence against obstacles, hostile forces, or obstructive beings, often within controlled esoteric settings. Third, tantric discourse can align transgressive action with nondual insight, suggesting that ordinary ethical categories are conventional and must sometimes be surpassed for the sake of liberation.

Yet the move from Mahāyāna exception to tantric intensification is not merely quantitative. Tantra changes the register of the debate. The issue is no longer only whether a bodhisattva may kill in an emergency; it is also whether terrifying and transgressive forms can themselves be soteriologically productive. Wrath is ritualized, embodied, and visualized. Violence becomes symbolically productive before it becomes ethically actionable.

Still, the same formal restrictions remain in place. Tantric sources and their defenders do not usually claim that ordinary untrained actors may commit violence with spiritual impunity. As in Mahāyāna exception discourse, the decisive qualifications are realization, compassion, initiation, and doctrinal competence. The very extremity of tantric materials therefore reveals a paradox: the more radical the exception, the more strongly it depends on a claim to rare and difficult authority.

### 4.3 Theoretical problems: epistemic authority, moral elitism, and abuse

The exception literature solves one problem only by generating several others. The first is epistemic authority. To justify killing compassionately in the strong Mahāyāna or Vajrayāna sense, an agent would need extraordinary knowledge: not only of immediate consequences, but of long-term karmic effects, the intentions of all parties, and the absence of less harmful alternatives. This means that the doctrine is stable only if it is reserved for agents whose authority is effectively beyond ordinary verification.

The second problem is moral elitism. If spiritually advanced beings may do what ordinary people may not, then Buddhist ethics becomes asymmetrical. The same act is condemned in one case and potentially celebrated in another, depending on who performs it and under what claimed realization. Once that asymmetry appears, the tradition becomes vulnerable to sanctified exceptionalism.

The third problem is karmic ambiguity. Some accounts imply that compassionate killing remains a grave moral act, but one that a bodhisattva accepts for the sake of others. Others treat compassionate violence as potentially auspicious and devoid of negative karmic consequence when undertaken under proper conditions. These are not minor differences. The first model preserves tragedy; the second suggests that enlightened intention can neutralize or even reverse the karmic valence of lethal action.

The fourth and most politically consequential problem is institutional abuse. Once rare exceptions are conceptualized, they can be translated outward. The move from “a highly realized bodhisattva may exceptionally act” to “our monks, rulers, army, or nation act for a higher good” is not automatic, but it is structurally available. This is the critical bridge to political theology. The danger does not lie only in abstract doctrine; it lies in how exception-talk authorizes institutions to claim sacred necessity, threat, or protective mandate.

## 5. Political theology and the problem of legitimation

If Sections 2–4 established that Buddhism contains a real anti-killing norm, a symbolic language of wrath, and a constrained discourse of compassionate exception, the next question is how these materials become politically operative. The key issue is legitimation. Violent imagery does not produce political violence by itself, and doctrinal exception does not automatically become public policy. Between symbol and event lies a mediating field in which monks, rulers, parties, militaries, and publics redescribe force as protection, necessity, or moral obligation.

A political-theological lens is useful here because it directs attention to the passage by which religious concepts acquire collective force. In Buddhist settings, that passage rarely takes the form of a simple command to kill. It more often works through softer but more consequential categories: defending the sāsana, protecting sacred territory, preserving moral order, guarding the majority community, or authorizing rulers as custodians of a threatened religious world. The issue, then, is not raw theology alone, but the institutional and political conditions under which Buddhist authority becomes usable for coercive ends.

### 5.1 From doctrinal exception to collective mandate

At the doctrinal level, compassionate-violence arguments are generally framed as rare exceptions for highly realized agents. Politically, however, exceptions are unstable. Once a tradition concedes that force may under some conditions be exercised for a higher good, later actors can attempt to scale that logic upward. The move is structurally simple even if historically variable: what is first attributed to the bodhisattva, adept, or protector is later redescribed as the responsibility of monk, ruler, army, or nation. This is the crucial translation mechanism. A tightly restricted ethical exception becomes a broader public grammar of necessity.

This translation depends on collective subject formation. Publics must come to imagine themselves not simply as believers, but as bearers of a sacred inheritance under threat. Once that happens, protection can be moralized and coercion sacralized. Doctrine, symbol, and institutional status can be reorganized into a collective mandate when a majority community imagines itself as vulnerable, besieged, or providentially charged with defense.

A second step in the translation process is moral distancing. Public violence is often legitimized not as aggression but as defense, purification, restoration, or prevention. This is where the earlier symbolic repertoire matters. If wrath can already be understood as force without hatred, and if protection of the Dharma can already be cast as a religious good, then political actors inherit a language with which coercion may be reframed as regrettable but righteous. The point is not that Yamāntaka causes nationalism. It is that a tradition that can distinguish violent appearance from immoral intention already possesses a conceptual wedge that later political reasoning may exploit.

### 5.2 Sacred protection, sovereignty, and threatened identity

The most common public form of legitimation in Buddhist politics is the language of protection. Protection is flexible. It may mean protection of monasteries, relics, sacred geography, women, demographic majority, national culture, or the state itself. Because it is flexible, it can bind ethical restraint to coercive politics without appearing to abandon compassion.

This is where political theology becomes sharper than a simple “religion and violence” frame. The core problem is not merely that religion influences politics. It is that religious categories help define who belongs, what must be preserved, and what counts as an existential threat. In Buddhist-majority contexts, that can produce a fusion of majority ethnicity, religious truth, and territorial destiny. When that fusion occurs, the state no longer appears as one actor among others. It appears as custodian of a sacred civilizational order.

The same logic also helps explain why very different cases can still be compared. Japan’s wartime Buddhist complicity did not look like Sri Lankan or Myanmar-style grassroots majoritarian agitation, yet the legitimating structure was analogous in one key respect: Buddhist institutions and idioms were folded into a larger sovereign project and helped sacralize obedience, sacrifice, and violence in the name of a higher order. The decisive question is not whether Buddhism contains violent images or even whether it contains ethical exceptions. The more important question is how these are institutionally translated into public authority.

## 6. Comparative regional histories

The argument of the previous section was that wrathful imagery and doctrinal exception do not become politically consequential on their own. They require institutional translation. Section 6 tests that claim across four regional histories: Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Japan, and Tibet. The point is not to force these cases into a single narrative of “Buddhist violence,” since their causal structures differ sharply. Rather, the aim is to show that Buddhist symbols, institutions, and ethical vocabularies become politically potent under specific conditions: when religion is fused with majority identity, when the state seeks moral legitimation, or when sacred community is narrated as threatened and therefore in need of extraordinary protection.

### 6.1 Sri Lanka and Myanmar: majoritarian Buddhist nationalism

Sri Lanka and Myanmar are the clearest cases in this review of Buddhist majoritarian nationalism. In both, Buddhist identity has been tied to majority ethnicity and national belonging strongly enough that defense of Buddhism can be redescribed as defense of the nation itself. In Sri Lanka, recent scholarship characterizes the postcolonial order as a form of Sinhalese Buddhist ethnocracy, arguing that Buddhist majoritarianism became embedded in state structure, electoral politics, and debates over sovereignty and minority belonging. That framing helps explain why violence cannot be understood merely as sporadic monk extremism. The deeper issue is a political formation in which Buddhism functions as a legitimating grammar of majority entitlement.

The Sri Lankan case should not be flattened into a simple “religious war” model. The civil war involved ethnicity, territory, language, constitutional design, and state violence, not Buddhism alone. Yet the scholarship increasingly argues that Buddhist primacy helped define the terms within which pluralism was narrowed and coercive centralization became morally intelligible. The postwar shift toward anti-Muslim agitation reinforces this point. Once Buddhist-majoritarian protection becomes a normalized political idiom, the target of exclusion can change while the legitimating structure remains.

Myanmar presents a parallel but not identical pattern. Scholarship on Myanmar’s Theravāda context describes the rise of a phenomenon widely labelled Buddhist nationalism, especially in connection with anti-Muslim mobilization and the Rohingya crisis. The crucial point is that Buddhist nationalism in Myanmar has not been only a matter of a few radical monks. It has involved a wider ecosystem in which monastic activism, Burman-majoritarian identity, demographic anxiety, and military interests became mutually reinforcing.

The Rohingya case shows the full force of the legitimation problem. Citizenship law, military strategy, frontier governance, and race-making were all central, but Buddhist nationalist rhetoric helped cast Muslims, and especially the Rohingya, as demographic and civilizational threats. That rhetoric matters because it transformed coercion from a merely strategic act into a morally intelligible act of defense. In both Sri Lanka and Myanmar, then, the decisive mechanism is not doctrinal permission in the abstract. It is the fusion of majority religion, state belonging, and threatened identity into a public theology of protection.

### 6.2 Japan: Buddhist institutional complicity with imperial militarism

Japan differs from Sri Lanka and Myanmar in a crucial respect. The main issue is not primarily majoritarian violence against an internal religious minority. It is institutional Buddhist complicity with imperial militarism. The literature associated with Brian Victoria, while debated in some details, shifted the field by documenting the extent to which Zen and other Buddhist institutions aligned themselves with Japanese nationalism, empire, and war. Reviews and subsequent scholarship generally accept the broad claim that major Buddhist institutions were not merely passive under wartime pressure; many actively collaborated, adapted doctrine, and furnished moral rhetoric compatible with military service and imperial sacrifice.

This case is analytically important because it shows a different pathway from Buddhist discourse to violence. Here the link is less through threatened-majority populism than through state absorption and ideological harmonization. Zen idioms of egolessness, immediacy, fearlessness, and disciplined action could be repurposed within a militarized national project. That did not mean Zen doctrine naturally leads to war. It meant Buddhist institutions proved capable of reinterpreting their own resources in forms congenial to the state’s demand for obedience, sacrifice, and moral purification through battle. The political-theological issue here is not mob protection of religion, but the sacralization of imperial duty.

The Japanese case therefore sharpens the general thesis of this article. Violence need not be legitimated through the explicit language of compassionate killing or wrathful deity symbolism. It can also be legitimated through institutional accommodation to a sovereign order that defines war as morally necessary and invites religious traditions to provide its ethical atmosphere. Japan is the clearest case in this review of Buddhist violence as institutional complicity with an expansionist state, rather than as grassroots religious majoritarianism.

### 6.3 Tibet: clerical-state formation, sectarian conflict, and modern repression

Tibet needs more careful periodization than the other three cases because “Tibetan Buddhist violence” can refer to at least three distinct historical formations that should not be collapsed into one another: premodern sectarian and regional conflict, Ganden Phodrang clerical-state consolidation under the Dalai Lamas, and modern PRC domination, repression, and Tibetan resistance. Review scholarship on Tibet under the Dalai Lamas is useful precisely because it rejects the sentimental image of Tibet as a uniformly peaceful Buddhist polity and emphasizes that conflict, coercion, and political struggle were structurally present in Tibetan history.

The first layer is premodern sectarian and regional conflict. Tibetan Buddhist history included rivalry among schools, conflicts over patronage, alliances with regional rulers, and the use of coercion in struggles over authority. By the end of the seventeenth century the Geluk tradition had assumed religio-political dominance in Central Tibet under the Dalai Lamas, indicating that monasteries were not merely spiritual sites but major political and economic actors. That does not mean Tibet was uniquely violent. It means Buddhist institutions were embedded in competition over land, taxation, labor, patronage, and governance.

The second layer is clerical-state consolidation, especially under the Ganden Phodrang. Here the relevant issue is not random monastic militancy but the emergence of a polity in which religious and political authority were fused. Buddhist rule did not suspend ordinary problems of governance; it had to manage conflict, impose hierarchy, and preserve dominance. This is important for the article’s broader argument because it shows that even in a paradigmatically Buddhist state, violence and coercion need not appear as explicit repudiations of Buddhism. They can appear as instruments of order within a sacralized polity.

The third layer is the PRC-Tibet conflict in the modern era, which has a radically different structure. Here the dominant large-scale violence problem is not a Buddhist majority subordinating minorities, but a non-Buddhist party-state ruling over Tibetan regions through securitization, religious regulation, demographic engineering, and campaigns of political integration. Religion is at the center of both resistance and repression: monasteries and Buddhist identity become not merely cultural survivals, but focal sites of state suspicion and Tibetan mobilization.

This modern layer makes Tibet unlike Sri Lanka, Myanmar, or wartime Japan. In those cases, Buddhist institutions were significantly implicated in public legitimation of coercive order. In modern Tibet, Buddhist institutions are often themselves the objects of state control, even while Tibetan Buddhism also provides symbolic resources for resistance. That does not make Tibetan Buddhism politically innocent in every respect. There are histories of armed Tibetan resistance, and in the post-1959 period Tibet has also seen self-immolation and other forms of radical protest. But analytically the Tibetan case should not be forced into the same template as Buddhist majoritarian nationalism.

### Mini-table: four case types

| Case | Dominant form | Main mechanism of legitimation | Core analytical caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sri Lanka** | Majoritarian ethnocratic nationalism | Buddhist primacy fused with majority ethnicity and state belonging | Do not reduce civil war history to religion alone; religion works through constitutional and majoritarian structures. |
| **Myanmar** | Exclusionary Buddhist nationalism | Threatened-religion rhetoric plus monastic activism and military-nationalist synergy | Do not isolate monks from wider military, legal, and demographic regimes. |
| **Japan** | Institutional complicity with imperial militarism | Doctrinal adaptation to obedience, sacrifice, and state war aims | Do not confuse institutional collaboration with a single timeless Zen essence. |
| **Tibet** | Premodern clerical-state conflict; modern repression and resistance | In premodern periods, sacralized polity and sectarian-state competition; in modern period, PRC securitization of Buddhist identity | Do not collapse Dalai Lama-era governance, sectarian conflict, and PRC-era repression into one undifferentiated “Tibetan violence” story. |

Taken together, the four cases support a more differentiated typology than the blanket phrase “Buddhist violence” usually permits. Sri Lanka and Myanmar are best read through the category of majoritarian nationalist protection. Japan is best read through institutional complicity with sovereign militarism. Tibet combines premodern clerical-state conflict with modern repression and contested resistance. What links them is not a single Buddhist doctrine of violence, but the recurring conversion of Buddhist authority into political legitimacy under conditions of threat, sovereignty, and institutional entanglement.

## 7. Conclusion: toward a typology of Buddhist violence

The argument of this review has been that the central problem is not violent imagery in Buddhism taken in isolation. Wrathful iconography, as Section 2 argued, is better understood first as a ritual and doctrinal language of transformation: a semiotic repertoire through which fear, death, obstruction, and ego are confronted, visualized, and in some tantric contexts ritually inhabited. Nor is the decisive issue simply that Buddhist ethics contains exceptions. Section 3 established that the anti-killing baseline remains a real and durable normative center, while Section 4 showed that doctrines of compassionate violence emerge only as constrained and unstable exceptions to that baseline. The critical question, then, is how symbolic force and doctrinal exception become historically actionable. The answer developed here is political legitimation. Buddhist violence becomes intelligible when religious authority is translated into public claims about protection, sovereignty, threatened identity, and necessary coercion.

This argument yields a narrower and more useful conclusion than either apologetic or sensationalist accounts allow. It is not persuasive to deny that Buddhist institutions, monks, symbols, and doctrines have participated in violent political orders. The historical record in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Japan, and Tibet makes that denial untenable, albeit in very different ways. But it is equally unhelpful to infer from those histories that Buddhism is simply another violence-permissive tradition in which iconography and doctrine transparently dictate political action. The cases reviewed here show mediation everywhere: through state formation, constitutional primacy, empire, clerical administration, repression, nationalism, and threat narratives. The phrase “Buddhist violence” is analytically serviceable only if it refers not to one essence, but to a family of political formations in which Buddhist authority is mobilized under distinct historical conditions.

### 7.1 Provisional typology

A provisional typology follows from the comparative discussion. First is majoritarian nationalist protection, seen most clearly in Sri Lanka and Myanmar, where Buddhism is fused with majority ethnicity and state identity, and coercion is narrated as defense of a sacred national community. Second is institutional complicity with sovereign militarism, exemplified by wartime Japan, where Buddhist institutions adapt themselves to an expansionist state and help furnish a language of sacrifice and moral necessity. Third is clerical-state coercive order, visible in premodern Tibetan governance, where Buddhist institutions are not merely moral commentators but governing actors implicated in hierarchy, conflict management, and the use of force. Fourth is religious repression and resistant sacralization, most visible in modern Tibet, where Buddhist institutions are objects of state control even as Buddhist identity becomes a medium of collective resistance. These are not exhaustive categories, but they are more precise than the undifferentiated moralism of much public discussion.

The value of this typology is not that it solves every causal problem. It is that it forces analysis to specify the relation among doctrine, institution, and political form. The same Buddhist tradition may look quite different when operating as majority religion, minority religion, imperial collaborator, clerical state, or repressed community. Once that is recognized, the search for a single Buddhist stance on violence becomes less useful than the analysis of translation mechanisms: how symbols, ethics, and authority are reassembled in different regimes of power.

### 7.2 Research gaps

Three research gaps emerge clearly from this review. The first concerns the lived reception of wrathful imagery. Scholarship has become much better at describing iconographic symbolism, but much less clear on how practitioners, monks, ritual specialists, and lay observers actually experience, interpret, and deploy wrathful figures in concrete settings. A buffalo-headed Yamāntaka in a retreat manual, a monastery chapel, a museum catalogue, and a nationalist public culture may not function in the same way at all. Without more reception-focused work, the link between iconography and politics remains under-theorized.

The second gap concerns the doctrine-to-institution pipeline. Buddhist studies has substantial work on compassionate violence as a philosophical problem and substantial work on Buddhist nationalism and war as historical problems, but the institutional mechanics connecting the two remain insufficiently developed. How do elite discourses of upāya, wrath, or protection become public pedagogies, legal preferences, security narratives, or electoral programs? Legitimation is the key concept here, but the comparative institutional history of that translation still needs much sharper analysis.

The third gap is the need for a stronger comparative political typology. The category “Buddhist violence” remains too coarse unless scholars distinguish among ethnocratic majoritarianism, imperial collaboration, clerical-state formation, repression of Buddhist populations, sectarian conflict, and resistant sacralization. Tibet alone demonstrates why this matters: without periodization, the case becomes analytically unusable. More rigorous typology would make cross-regional comparison less moralizing and more explanatory.

The broader implication is that the study of Buddhist violence should move away from the question of whether Buddhism is “really peaceful” and toward the question of how traditions of non-harm nevertheless generate symbolic, ethical, and institutional resources that can be translated into coercive politics. That question is harder, but it is also the one that does justice to both Buddhist doctrine and Buddhist history.
