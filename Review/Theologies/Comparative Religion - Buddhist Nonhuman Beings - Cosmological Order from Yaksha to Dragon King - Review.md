## Abstract

This review article examines the role of nonhuman beings in Buddhist cosmology, with particular attention to the transformation of Indic categories in Chinese, Korean, and Japanese Buddhism. Beings such as **夜叉** (*yàchā*; Skt. *yakṣa*), **羅剎** (*luóchà*; Skt. *rākṣasa*), **龍** (*lóng*; Skt. *nāga*), **乾闥婆** (*qiántàpó*; Skt. *gandharva*), and **鳩槃荼** (*Jiūpántú*; Skt. *Kumbhāṇḍa*) are often treated as mythological decoration, but historically they helped Buddhism organize danger, protection, sacred space, ritual authority, and the relation between local cults and universal religious claims. The article argues that Buddhism commonly handled older spirit and divine categories through absorption rather than erasure. Nonhuman beings were named, ranked, subordinated, translated, visualized, and often redeployed as protectors of the Dharma. This process is especially visible in structures such as **四天王** (*Sì Tiānwáng*), **天龍八部** (*Tiānlóng Bābù*), **須彌山** (*Xūmíshān*) cosmography, **曼荼羅供養** (*Màntuóluó Gòngyǎng*), and esoteric wrathful forms such as **金剛夜叉明王** (*Jīngāng Yèchā Míngwáng*). Moving from Indian Buddhist cosmology to East Asian reception, the article shows how translation, temple architecture, visual art, and ritual practice transformed inherited beings into new religious forms. The central claim is that Buddhism became durable not only by teaching liberation, but by reorganizing the invisible world into a governable, ritualized, and visually compelling sacred order.

# 1. Introduction: Why Nonhuman Beings Matter in Buddhism

## 1.1 Beyond “mythology”: nonhuman beings as part of Buddhist religious worldmaking

To modern readers, beings such as **夜叉** (*yàchā*; Skt. *yakṣa*), **羅剎** (*luóchà*; Skt. *rākṣasa*), **龍** (*lóng*; Skt. *nāga*), and **乾闥婆** (*qiántàpó*; Skt. *gandharva*) can look like decorative mythology: strange creatures orbiting around what is assumed to be the “real” content of Buddhism—ethics, meditation, and philosophy. That reading is too neat. In historical Buddhist traditions, these beings were not marginal ornaments. They helped structure how Buddhists imagined the cosmos, danger, sacred authority, protection, and the boundary between cultivated religious order and the untamed powers of the world.

This matters because Buddhism did not emerge into an empty spiritual landscape. It developed within a wider South Asian environment already populated by local gods, serpent powers, territorial spirits, dangerous ghosts, and semi-divine beings. Early Buddhism did not simply deny their existence. Instead, it often recognized them, confronted them, classified them, subordinated them, and sometimes recruited them. A being that begins as a threat at the edge of the forest may later reappear as a guardian of a scripture, a retainer of **毘沙門天** (*Píshāméntiān*; Vaiśravaṇa), or a member of the great protective ensemble known in East Asia as **天龍八部** (*Tiānlóng Bābù*).

## 1.2 The central question: absorption, re-ranking, and transformation

The central claim of this article is that Buddhism often dealt with nonhuman beings through **absorption, re-ranking, and transformation**. It did not merely preserve older spirit categories unchanged, nor did it simply discard them as superstition. It reorganized them within a new cosmological and ritual order.

This process can be seen at several levels. At the level of narrative, dangerous beings may confront the Buddha or his followers and then be subdued, converted, or compelled to serve the Dharma. At the level of cosmology, previously unruly beings become assigned to larger systems of governance under the **四天王** (*Sì Tiānwáng*), the Four Great Kings. At the level of ritual, they may become protectors of scriptures, monasteries, devotees, or regions. At the level of iconography, their forms are reshaped into mural figures, guardian statues, temple ensembles, and eventually wrathful esoteric deities.

## 1.3 Scope and method

This article examines nonhuman beings in Buddhism from India to East Asia, with special attention to how older cosmological categories were transformed in **China**, **Korea**, and **Japan**. Its focus is not every possible nonhuman class, but a cluster especially important for Buddhist cosmology and East Asian reception: **夜叉**, **羅剎**, **龍**, **乾闥婆**, related beings such as **鳩槃荼** (*Jiūpántú*; *Kumbhāṇḍa*), and the larger grouping of **天龍八部**.

Methodologically, the article is comparative and synthetic. It uses textual, cosmological, iconographic, and reception-historical lenses. The argument throughout is straightforward: nonhuman beings are not a trivial fringe of Buddhism. They are one of the clearest ways to see Buddhism as a lived religion—one that orders the cosmos, manages danger, absorbs local powers, and makes the strange inhabitable.

| Term | Transliteration | Broad role in Buddhist imagination |
|---|---|---|
| 夜叉 | *yàchā* / *yakṣa* | Ambivalent fierce beings; territorial, dangerous, but often recruitable |
| 羅剎 | *luóchà* / *rākṣasa* | More strongly demonic, predatory, and fear-marked beings |
| 龍 | *lóng* / *nāga* | Serpent/dragon powers tied to water, kingship, treasure, and protection |
| 乾闥婆 | *qiántàpó* / *gandharva* | Music- and fragrance-associated heavenly beings |
| 天龍八部 | *Tiānlóng Bābù* | East Asian classification of eight major nonhuman classes |

# 2. The Major Nonhuman Classes in Buddhist Cosmology

## 2.1 夜叉 *yàchā*; Skt. *yakṣa*: ambivalent local powers and recruitable guardians

Among the major nonhuman classes in Buddhist cosmology, **夜叉** are perhaps the most revealing because they are so unstable. They can be frightening, violent, territorial, and associated with wilderness or local sacred places, yet they are also among the beings most easily drawn into Buddhist protection. This doubleness is not accidental. It reflects the fact that **yakṣas** seem to come from an older Indic religious environment of local powers—spirits of groves, mountains, treasure, crossroads, and dangerous margins—rather than from a neatly bounded Buddhist invention.

In Buddhist literature, **夜叉** often appear at the edge of order. They may threaten monks, test the Buddha, or embody the fear attached to places outside ordinary human control. Yet they are also unusually available for conversion. A hostile **yakṣa** can become a guardian; a fierce local being can become a protector of a sutra, a temple, a region, or a deity such as **毘沙門天** (*Píshāméntiān*; Vaiśravaṇa). This recruitability makes them central to Buddhist religious history.

## 2.2 羅剎 *luóchà*, 龍 *lóng*, and 乾闥婆 *qiántàpó*

If **夜叉** are the most ambivalent, **羅剎** are usually the most overtly threatening. In Buddhist sources, **rākṣasas** are more strongly marked as predatory, violent, and demonic. Their default profile is darker than that of **yakṣas**. They can still be subordinated or converted, especially in Mahāyāna and East Asian contexts, but the category tends to preserve a sharper aura of danger.

**龍**, by contrast, occupy a very different symbolic ecology. The Indic **nāga** is a serpent power associated with water, weather, kingship, treasure, subterranean realms, and dangerous potency. When Buddhism moved east, however, **nāga** underwent one of the most dramatic transformations in the whole tradition. Translated as **龍**, it entered the Chinese, Korean, and Japanese dragon field. This was not a simple lexical substitution. It was a merger.

**乾闥婆** stand apart from both the demonic and the serpentine. They are linked above all to music, fragrance, celebration, and aesthetic attendance. If **夜叉** belong to the grammar of threat and guardianship, and **龍** to the grammar of elemental and royal power, then **乾闥婆** belong to the grammar of sacred atmosphere.

| Class | Native script | Transliteration | Typical associations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yakṣa | 夜叉 | *yàchā* | Ambivalent local power, guardianship, fierceness |
| Rākṣasa | 羅剎 | *luóchà* | Predation, violence, demonic threat |
| Nāga | 龍 | *lóng* | Water, kingship, treasure, serpent/dragon power |
| Gandharva | 乾闥婆 | *qiántàpó* | Music, fragrance, celestial attendance |

## 2.3 From difference to system: why Buddhist texts list these beings together

Given how different these classes are, why do Buddhist texts so often list them together? The answer is not that they are interchangeable. It is that Buddhism repeatedly gathers them into a larger **nonhuman social order**. When sutras describe assemblies of **天、龍、夜叉、乾闥婆、阿修羅、迦樓羅、緊那羅、摩睺羅伽**, the point is not biological precision. The point is universality and hierarchy.

The East Asian category **天龍八部** makes this particularly clear. The eight are not a “species chart.” They are a coalition of re-ranked beings, drawn from older Indic cosmology and reorganized under Buddhist authority. The list dramatizes the cosmic reach of the Dharma, displays Buddhism’s ability to encompass dangerous or alien beings, and turns a messy mythic environment into something more legible and governable.

# 3. Buddhist Cosmology as a Structured World

## 3.1 須彌山 *Xūmíshān*; Skt. *Sumeru / Meru* and the four-continent universe

Buddhist cosmology is not an accidental heap of heavens, demons, and rebirth realms. It is a structured world, and one of its most important organizing images is **須彌山** (*Xūmíshān*), the cosmic mountain at the center of the world-system. Around it lies a patterned universe of seas, mountains, heavens, hells, and inhabited continents.

The classic inhabited world is arranged as **四大洲** (*Sì Dàzhōu*), the Four Great Continents, placed around **須彌山** in the four cardinal directions. These are not continents in the modern geological sense. They are cosmographic regions: moral-geographical zones inhabited by humans under different conditions of lifespan, abundance, and karmic environment.

| Direction | Chinese name | Transliteration | Sanskrit |
|---|---|---|---|
| East | 弗婆提 / 毘提訶洲 | *Fúpótí / Pítíhēzhōu* | *Pūrvavideha* |
| South | 閻浮提 / 贍部洲 | *Yánfútí / Shànbùzhōu* | *Jambudvīpa* |
| West | 瞿耶尼 / 瞿陀尼 | *Qúyēní / Qútuóní* | *Aparagodānīya* |
| North | 鬱單越 / 北俱盧洲 | *Yùdānyuè / Běi Jùlúzhōu* | *Uttarakuru* |

## 3.2 閻浮提 *Yánfútí*; Skt. *Jambudvīpa* and the human world

Of the four continents, **閻浮提** is by far the most important in Buddhist literature. It is the southern continent, but more significantly it is the continent of ordinary human life as imagined by Buddhist tradition. When Buddhist texts speak in a morally urgent way about suffering, karma, practice, decline, kingship, or the possibility of awakening, they are often speaking from the perspective of **閻浮提**.

This gives **閻浮提** a paradoxical status. It is not the most pleasant of the continents. In many cosmographic systems it is shorter-lived, harsher, and more unstable than some of the others. Yet it is also the spiritually decisive one. It is the place where Buddhas appear, where moral struggle is acute enough to matter, and where liberation can be sought under conditions of real pressure.

## 3.3 四大洲 and 八小洲: shape, lifespan, and moral ecology

The four continents are not merely placed differently; they are also described as having different shapes, human lifespans, and life conditions. In Abhidharma-style cosmography, this produces a kind of moral ecology: the continents are distinct environments for human existence rather than interchangeable territories.

| Continent | Shape | Lifespan | General condition |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 閻浮提 (*Jambudvīpa*) | Triangular | 100 years | Mixed, harsh, spiritually urgent |
| 毘提訶洲 (*Pūrvavideha*) | Semicircular | 250 years | More stable, less pressured |
| 瞿耶尼 (*Aparagodānīya*) | Circular | 500 years | More abundant and ease-filled |
| 北俱盧洲 (*Uttarakuru*) | Square | 1,000 years | Highly abundant, quasi-utopian |

The system also includes **八小洲** (*Bā Xiǎozhōu*), the eight subcontinents, two accompanying each great continent. These complete the world-map and make it more than a simple four-point diagram.

## 3.4 Cosmography as more than geography

It would be a mistake to treat this cosmology as only an obsolete map. Even in premodern Buddhist settings, **須彌山** cosmography did more than locate continents. It organized moral imagination, ritual practice, sacred directionality, and the placement of human life within a much larger order of beings.

This is why Buddhist cosmography can be called a **structured world** rather than merely a mythic picture. It links space with ethics, environment with karmic possibility, and geography with governance. In **曼荼羅供養** (*Màntuóluó Gòngyǎng*), practitioners offer not an abstract “world,” but a highly articulated cosmos: **須彌山**, **四大洲**, **八小洲**, **日月**, and further elements depending on the system.

# 4. The 四天王 *Sì Tiānwáng* and the Governance of the Nonhuman World

## 4.1 Who the 四天王 are

The **四天王** (*Sì Tiānwáng*), often translated as the Four Great Kings or Four Heavenly Kings, are among the most important governing figures in Buddhist cosmology. They are not Buddhas, and they are not merely decorative guardian gods stationed at temple gates for visual intimidation. Their deeper function is cosmological and administrative. They rule the four quarters of the world around **須彌山**, protect the Dharma, supervise classes of nonhuman beings, and stand at the hinge between higher heavenly order and the unruly populations of the lower cosmos.

| Direction | Chinese name | Transliteration | Sanskrit |
|---|---|---|---|
| East | 持國天王 | *Chíguó Tiānwáng* | *Dhṛtarāṣṭra* |
| South | 增長天王 | *Zēngzhǎng Tiānwáng* | *Virūḍhaka* |
| West | 廣目天王 | *Guǎngmù Tiānwáng* | *Virūpākṣa* |
| North | 多聞天王 / 毘沙門天 | *Duōwén Tiānwáng / Píshāméntiān* | *Vaiśravaṇa* |

## 4.2 Direction, continent, and retinue

The **四天王** matter because Buddhist cosmology is directional. Each Heavenly King governs not only a cardinal direction, but also a corresponding human world-sector and a set of subordinate beings. This turns cosmography into jurisdiction.

| King | Direction | Associated human region | Typical retinue or governed class |
|---|---|---|---|
| 持國天王 | East | 東勝身洲 / 毘提訶洲 | 乾闥婆 and related beings |
| 增長天王 | South | 閻浮提 | 鳩槃荼 and related spirits |
| 廣目天王 | West | 西牛貨洲 / 瞿耶尼 | 龍 |
| 多聞天王 / 毘沙門天 | North | 北俱盧洲 | 夜叉, 羅剎 |

## 4.3 增長天王 and 鳩槃荼 as a case study in cosmic administration

The relation between **增長天王** and **鳩槃荼** is a particularly useful case because it reveals how Buddhist cosmology handles awkward, low-status, and grotesque beings. **鳩槃荼** are not glamorous. Traditional explanations describe them as bizarre ghost-like beings, sometimes glossed as **甕形鬼** (*wèngxíng guǐ*), “jar-shaped spirits,” or **冬瓜鬼** (*dōngguā guǐ*), “winter-melon spirits,” with imagery often tied to swollen belly or lower-body grotesquerie.

But Buddhist cosmology does not leave them as clutter. It assigns them to **南方增長天王**. The southern quarter, associated with **閻浮提**, the human world, falls under **增長天王**, and **鳩槃荼** belong to his jurisdiction. This shows that even highly grotesque beings are incorporated into ordered Buddhist cosmology.

## 4.4 Buddhism as cosmic political theology

The **四天王** reveal something larger than their own iconography. They show that Buddhism often operates as a kind of **cosmic political theology**. Space, population, morality, danger, and protection are arranged in a ranked order under legitimate authority.

The **四天王** are therefore one of Buddhism’s great technologies for managing alterity. They do not erase strange beings. They assign them sectors, chains of command, and duties. They stand where cosmology becomes order.

# 5. From India to East Asia: Translation and Transformation

## 5.1 Translation as reinterpretation

When Buddhism moved from India into East Asia, nonhuman beings were not simply carried across intact like labeled specimens in a doctrinal suitcase. They were **translated**, and translation in this context meant much more than substituting one word for another. It involved phonetic transcription, semantic approximation, commentary, visual reimagining, and eventual integration into new religious ecologies.

Some terms remained relatively foreign through transliteration. **Yakṣa** became **夜叉** (*yàchā*) or sometimes **藥叉** (*yàochā*). **Rākṣasa** became **羅剎** (*luóchà*). But other terms were translated into existing East Asian symbolic fields. The most important example is **nāga**, rendered as **龍** (*lóng*). An Indic serpent-king entered a Chinese-language world in which dragons already carried enormous associations with water, rain, sovereignty, auspiciousness, and cosmic force.

## 5.2 China: classification, commentary, and ritual order

China was the first major East Asian environment in which these Indic beings were systematically reworked. Chinese Buddhism was especially skilled at turning fluid nonhuman categories into classifiable religious populations. Through sutra translation, lexicography, commentary, and ritual manuals, Chinese Buddhism stabilized names, grouped beings into regularized sets, and placed them within larger structures such as **四天王**, **天龍八部**, and the protective retinues of specific cults.

At the same time, China did not leave these beings at the level of textual exotica. **夜叉** became connected with **毘沙門天**, **藥師** devotion, and protective cults such as the **十二藥叉大將**. **龍王** entered Buddhist rainmaking, water ritual, and moral kingship discourse. China was the great machine of systematization.

## 5.3 Korea: guardian order and temple-space integration

Korean Buddhism inherited these categories through the Sinitic Buddhist tradition, but the Korean reception often placed particular emphasis on **guardian order**, **sacred architecture**, and the integration of nonhuman beings into temple space. Imported classes such as **야차** (*yacha*; 夜叉), **나찰** (*nachal*; 羅剎), **용** (*yong*; 龍), and **가루라** (*garura*; 迦樓羅) frequently appear less as unstable mythic beings and more as components of a protective religious environment.

The **팔부중** (*Palbujung*; 八部衆) appear in relation to pagodas, grottoes, temple paintings, and guardian programs. The emphasis often falls on protection, order, and placement rather than on elaborating each being’s full mythic biography.

## 5.4 Japan: sculptural individualization and iconographic intensification

Japanese Buddhism inherited the same broad cosmological categories, but Japan often transformed them through a different artistic and devotional logic. If China excelled in classification and Korea in guardian regularization, Japan was especially powerful in **iconographic intensification** and **sculptural individualization**.

This is clearest in the treatment of ensembles such as **八部衆** (*Hachibushū*) and in the development of wrathful and protective imagery. Japanese sculpture, especially in the Nara period, often turned inherited nonhuman categories into strongly individuated presences. The famous **興福寺八部衆** (*Kōfukuji Hachibushū*) shows how beings inherited from Indian cosmology could become sculptural personalities with expressive faces, bodily tension, and almost psychological individuality.

| Region | Dominant transformation pattern | Characteristic result |
|---|---|---|
| China | Classification, commentary, ritual ordering | Imported beings become doctrinally legible and systematized |
| Korea | Guardian integration, temple-space deployment | Beings become part of protective sacred architecture |
| Japan | Sculptural individualization, iconographic intensification | Beings become highly personalized and visually powerful |

# 6. 天龍八部 *Tiānlóng Bābù*: the Buddhist Coalition of Nonhuman Protectors

## 6.1 What 天龍八部 / 八部眾 are

The term **天龍八部** (*Tiānlóng Bābù*), also called **八部眾** (*Bābùzhòng*), refers to a standard East Asian Buddhist grouping of eight classes of nonhuman beings who attend the Buddha, hear the Dharma, and frequently function as protectors.

| Chinese | Transliteration | Sanskrit / Indic equivalent | Broad profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| 天 | *tiān* | *deva* | Heavenly beings |
| 龍 | *lóng* | *nāga* | Serpent/dragon powers |
| 夜叉 | *yàchā* | *yakṣa* | Fierce, ambivalent spirits |
| 乾闥婆 | *qiántàpó* | *gandharva* | Musical celestial beings |
| 阿修羅 | *āxiūluó* | *asura* | Conflict-marked anti-gods |
| 迦樓羅 | *jiālóuluó* | *garuḍa* | Great bird-beings |
| 緊那羅 | *jǐnnàluó* | *kiṃnara* | Semi-human musical beings |
| 摩睺羅伽 | *móhóuluóqié* | *mahoraga* | Serpentine or hybrid underworld beings |

This list should not be mistaken for a tidy zoological taxonomy. **天龍八部** are not “eight species” in the modern classificatory sense. They are better understood as a **Buddhist coalition** of nonhuman populations drawn from older Indic religious environments and reorganized into a Dharma-centered order.

## 6.2 Why the eight matter in sutras and assemblies

In Buddhist sutras, the repeated appearance of formulas such as **天、龍、夜叉、乾闥婆、阿修羅、迦樓羅、緊那羅、摩睺羅伽** serves an important literary and theological purpose. These lists dramatize the scale of the Buddha’s significance. The audience for the Dharma is not limited to monks, nuns, kings, and laypeople. It includes gods, dragon-kings, fierce spirits, celestial musicians, bird-beings, anti-gods, and other nonhuman classes.

## 6.3 The East Asian afterlife of the eight classes

In East Asia, **天龍八部** developed an afterlife far richer than a simple textual list. The category became doctrinal, visual, ritual, and architectural all at once. China preserved and stabilized the set through translation, commentary, and scriptural repetition. Korea integrated the **八部眾** into temple-space, grotto programs, guardian imagery, and the visual logic of sacred protection. Japan went even further in some cases, turning the inherited classes into highly individualized sculptural presences, as in the famous **興福寺八部衆**.

## 6.4 Why this category matters for the larger argument

The importance of **天龍八部** lies in the fact that it makes visible a core habit of Buddhist religious formation: **incorporation without flattening**. Buddhism does not simply say that all nonhuman beings are illusions and therefore irrelevant. Nor does it preserve every older spirit category in its raw inherited form. Instead, it gathers them into an ordered structure and gives them a place in relation to the Buddha, the Dharma, and the protection of sacred space.

# 7. Wrath, Protection, and Esoteric Transformation

## 7.1 From spirit to protector

One of the most important developments in Buddhist religious history is the movement from dangerous nonhuman being to formal protector. Beings such as **夜叉**, **羅剎**, **龍**, and related classes often begin in Buddhist literature as powers at the edge of order: fierce, unpredictable, hungry, territorial, or otherwise marked by alterity. Yet over time, many of them are not merely defeated. They are enlisted.

This distinction matters. A religion that only destroys hostile beings presents itself as oppositional. A religion that converts and redeploys them presents itself as sovereign. Buddhism often prefers the second strategy. Hostile beings may hear the Dharma, submit to the Buddha, swear vows, accept command, or become guardians of places, relics, monastic communities, sutras, or specific deities.

## 7.2 金剛夜叉明王 and the logic of esoteric absorption

This process reaches a new intensity in **密教** (*Mìjiào*; Esoteric Buddhism), where the old language of dangerous beings can be elevated into the titles and forms of major wrathful deities. The clearest example for the present discussion is **金剛夜叉明王** (*Jīngāng Yèchā Míngwáng*; Jpn. *Kongō Yasha Myōō*). Here the term **夜叉** has not disappeared. It is preserved in the name of a **明王** (*Míngwáng*; Wisdom King), a high-order wrathful deity within esoteric Buddhist ritual systems.

| Term | Transliteration | Basic force |
|---|---|---|
| 金剛 | *jīngāng* | Vajra, indestructible force, esoteric power |
| 夜叉 | *yàchā* | Yakṣa-like ferocity, dangerous nonhuman force |
| 明王 | *míngwáng* | Wrathful Wisdom King, enlightened protector |

## 7.3 Wrathful deities as transformed nonhuman force

The emergence of wrathful deities in East Asian esoteric Buddhism can be understood, in part, as the further transformation of older nonhuman categories. This does not mean that every **明王** is secretly just a promoted demon. It means something subtler: the iconographic and semantic grammar of wrathful deities often draws upon older traditions of dangerous power already associated with beings like **夜叉**, **羅剎**, serpent powers, guardian spirits, and other liminal forces.

| Stage | Type of being | Religious role |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Local spirit / dangerous nonhuman | Threat, liminality, locality |
| 2 | Subdued or converted being | Attendant, vow-holder, protector |
| 3 | Formal guardian in Buddhist order | **護法**, retainer, directional protector |
| 4 | Wrathful esoteric deity | Enlightened coercive power |

Wrathful deities preserve the force of the dangerous while removing its theological instability. The monster is not deleted; it is disciplined, enthroned, and made to defend the Dharma.

# 8. Art and Iconography: Visualizing the Buddhist Nonhuman World

## 8.1 敦煌 *Dūnhuáng*: mural cosmology and hybrid nonhuman assemblies

If Buddhist texts classify nonhuman beings, **敦煌** visualizes them as a crowd. In the cave-mural environment of **莫高窟** (*Mògāokū*), beings such as **龍**, **夜叉**, **乾闥婆**, **迦樓羅**, and related classes do not usually appear as isolated textbook specimens. They appear as parts of a larger cosmic scene: attending sermons, filling skies, framing sacred presences, supporting visual hierarchies, and helping turn the cave wall into a mapped universe.

This matters because **敦煌** was a Silk Road contact zone. The visual language there carries Indian, Central Asian, and increasingly Sinitic elements at once. As a result, nonhuman beings often preserve traces of their older Indic identities while also drifting toward East Asian visual conventions.

## 8.2 Korean temple art: guardian regularization

In Korean Buddhist art, the same nonhuman classes are often treated with greater formal discipline. If **敦煌** excels at mural abundance and cosmological crowding, Korean temple art often excels at **guardian regularization**. The beings are still diverse, but their diversity is increasingly subordinated to the protective logic of temple space, grotto design, pagoda relief, and ritual architecture.

This Korean tendency is particularly important because it shows how cosmology can be compressed into architectural clarity. The viewer does not need a full doctrinal explanation every time. The temple image already teaches the lesson: sacred space is not undefended.

## 8.3 Japanese sculpture: from category to personality

Japanese Buddhism inherited the same broad cosmological categories, but Japanese sculpture often pushed them toward a different kind of visual life. If Korean temple art tends toward guardian-system clarity, Japanese sculpture often turns inherited classes into **individualized presences**. The best-known case is the sculptural tradition of **八部衆** (*Hachibushū*), especially in Nara-period contexts such as **興福寺** (*Kōfukuji*).

Here the nonhuman being is no longer only a category within a list. It becomes a body, a face, a stance, a mood. Japanese sculptural practice is especially powerful at turning cosmological roles into something like personhood.

## 8.4 龍, 夜叉, and 迦樓羅 as three visual trajectories

| Category | Indic background | East Asian visual trajectory | Dominant effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 龍 | *nāga* | Fusion with East Asian dragon imagery | Naturalization and expansion |
| 夜叉 | *yakṣa* | Militarization and guardian assimilation | Controlled ferocity |
| 迦樓羅 | *garuḍa* | Hybrid retention with selective anthropomorphization | Mythic dramaticity |

**龍** are the clearest case of naturalization. **夜叉** move toward guardianhood and martial imagery. **迦樓羅** preserve perhaps the strongest sense of visible mythic hybridity. These three cases clarify the regional differences discussed above: at **敦煌**, they remain embedded in mural cosmology; in Korean art, they are drawn toward guardian function; in Japanese sculpture, they become specific and visually memorable presences.

# 9. Ritualizing the Cosmos: Maṇḍala Offering and Kālacakra

## 9.1 曼荼羅供養 and the offering of the whole universe

One of the most revealing features of Buddhist cosmology is that it does not remain a map on the page. It becomes something that can be ritually handled, assembled, visualized, and offered. This is especially clear in **曼荼羅供養** (*Màntuóluó Gòngyǎng*; maṇḍala offering), where the practitioner symbolically offers the entire structured universe to the Buddhas and bodhisattvas.

| Ritual element | Chinese | Transliteration | Cosmological role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central mountain | 須彌山 | *Xūmíshān* | Axis of the world |
| Four continents | 四大洲 | *Sì Dàzhōu* | Main human world-regions |
| Eight subcontinents | 八小洲 | *Bā Xiǎozhōu* | Supporting world-completion |
| Sun and moon | 日月 | *Rìyuè* | Cosmic luminaries |
| Additional treasures and emblems | 寶物 / 寶幢 / 寶蓋 | *bǎowù / bǎozhuàng / bǎogài* | Ritual enrichment of the cosmos |

## 9.2 From external map to ritual world

The movement from cosmography to maṇḍala offering shows that Buddhist thought does not treat space as inert background. The world-map is also a ritual world. A practitioner who offers **須彌山** and the continents is not acting as a geographer; the practitioner is ritually totalizing the field of experience. The map becomes an offering because it already functions as a complete symbolic universe.

This also clarifies why the earlier cosmological structures matter so much. Without **四大洲**, **八小洲**, directional order, and central axis, the maṇḍala offering would lose its logic of totality. The ritual depends on a cosmos that is already imaginally organized.

## 9.3 Kālacakra and the reconfiguration of the cosmos

The **Kālacakra** system introduces a major shift in the relation between cosmology and ritual. Older Abhidharma-style cosmography presents a structured external universe: Meru, continents, heavens, hells, cycles of formation and destruction. **Kālacakra**, by contrast, reworks cosmology into a more explicitly correlated system linking **outer cosmos**, **inner body**, and **tantric path**.

| Cosmological mode | Older Abhidharma universe | Kālacakra universe |
|---|---|---|
| Main emphasis | Structured external cosmos | Outer-inner correspondence |
| Core function | Classification and world-mapping | Transformation through correlation |
| Key relation | Meru-centered geography | Cosmos, body, time, and tantra linked |
| Ritual effect | Offer the world | Offer and transform a world-body system |

## 9.4 Why this matters for Buddhist thought

The shift from Abhidharma-style maṇḍala offering to **Kālacakra**-inflected ritual logic reveals two different but related Buddhist uses of cosmology. In the earlier pattern, the universe is structured, inhabited, and ritually offerable. In the later tantric pattern, the universe is still structured and offerable, but it is also homologous with the practitioner’s own body and path. The cosmos becomes internalized.

The world-map turns inward without ceasing to be cosmic. A world that begins as structured space becomes ritual object, then symbolic totality, then body-correlated transformative field.

# 10. Conclusion: Buddhism and the Management of Alterity

## 10.1 The central pattern: absorption rather than erasure

A consistent pattern has emerged across this study: Buddhism very often deals with nonhuman beings through **absorption rather than erasure**. Beings such as **夜叉**, **羅剎**, **龍**, **乾闥婆**, and **鳩槃荼** do not disappear when Buddhism asserts doctrinal authority. They are not simply denounced as false leftovers from a superseded religious past. Instead, they are named, ranked, distributed, translated, ritualized, and repeatedly placed inside Buddhist order.

This pattern matters because it shows that Buddhism, historically, is not only a philosophy of liberation or a psychology of suffering. It is also a religion that had to contend with a crowded sacred environment already full of local powers, dangerous beings, inherited mythic classes, and regionally meaningful spirits. Its solution was often neither pure rejection nor passive acceptance. It was **reorganization**.

## 10.2 East Asia as a zone of creative transformation

The move from India to East Asia did not preserve this world unchanged. It transformed it. China, Korea, and Japan did not merely receive an Indic cosmology and display it faithfully. Each region subjected inherited categories to new linguistic, visual, ritual, and spatial logics.

| Region | Main mode of transformation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| China | Translation, lexicography, classification | Imported beings become doctrinally legible |
| Korea | Temple integration, guardian ordering | Nonhuman beings become spatial protectors |
| Japan | Sculptural individuation, iconographic intensification | Cosmological classes become memorable presences |

These differences are not secondary local color. They are part of the argument. East Asia matters because it reveals that Buddhist cosmology is portable precisely because it is transformable. A being can keep its name and lose its old body. It can keep its force and gain a new office.

## 10.3 Nonhuman beings as a key to understanding Buddhism

Nonhuman beings are a key to understanding Buddhism because they reveal the tradition at several levels at once. They reveal **cosmology**, because they populate the world around **須彌山**, the **四大洲**, and the directional domains of the **四天王**. They reveal **ritual**, because they appear in **曼荼羅供養**, protective liturgies, healing cults, and esoteric transformations. They reveal **iconography**, because they are painted in **敦煌**, integrated into Korean temple art, and sculpted into highly individualized figures in Japan. They reveal **religious politics**, because they show how Buddhism asserts sovereignty over beings that predate it, resist it, or lend it force.

Most importantly, they reveal Buddhism as a tradition concerned with what might be called the **management of alterity**. Alterity here means not simply difference, but charged difference: beings that are dangerous, hybrid, nonhuman, local, excessive, liminal, or difficult to classify. Buddhism does not solve the problem of alterity by pretending it does not exist. It solves it by organizing it. The result is a cosmos that is never fully domesticated but is repeatedly made governable.

The broader lesson is plain. Buddhism became durable not only because it taught liberation, but because it learned how to inhabit complexity. It could speak to monks and kings, but also to dragons, yakṣas, demonesses, bird-beings, guardian spirits, and the ritual imagination of entire societies. That capacity for ordered inclusiveness—never complete, never simple, often strange—is one of the reasons Buddhist worlds have proven so resilient across time and place.
