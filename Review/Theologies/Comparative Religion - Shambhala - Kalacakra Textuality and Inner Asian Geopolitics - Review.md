# Abstract

This review argues that **Shambhala** (Sanskrit: **Śambhala**, शम्भल; Tibetan: **Bde ’byung**, བདེ་འབྱུང་) should not be treated as a timeless or unitary object. Its earliest recoverable form belongs to the **Kālacakra** (कालचक्र; དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་) corpus of late Indian Buddhism, where it functions simultaneously as sacred geography, royal archive, and eschatological reserve. The article first reconstructs Shambhala within the Kālacakra textual tradition, especially through the surviving **Laghu-Kālacakra** and **Vimalaprabhā** (विमलप्रभा; དྲི་མེད་འོད་), and then examines the anti-*mleccha* (म्लेच्छ) passages as a historically specific Buddhist response to political pressure and Islamic expansion. It argues that these passages are distinctive not because Buddhist literature lacked polemic elsewhere, but because they escalate sectarian competition into apocalyptic civilizational struggle.

The review then situates Shambhala comparatively within Buddhist rhetoric toward **Brahmins** (ब्राह्मण), **Jains** (जैन), **tīrthikas** (तीर्थिक), and **Śaiva** (शैव) rivals, showing that the Kālacakra material marks a shift from doctrinal refutation to crisis eschatology. From there, it traces the modern detachment of Shambhala from its Buddhist textual matrix through **Theosophy**, **Nicholas Roerich**, and Soviet and Japanese occult-geopolitical appropriations, while distinguishing it from the separate genealogies of **Agartha** and **Shangri-La**. The article further compares Qing imperial uses of Tibetan Buddhism with Pan-Mongol and Pan-Buddhist deployments of Shambhala, arguing that multiple Inner Asian polities competed within a shared sacred-political field even as they differed in institutional depth and temporal orientation.

Finally, the review examines popular, devotional, and vernacular afterlives of Shambhala among ordinary Buddhists through mass Kālacakra initiations, imagined pilgrimage, crisis prophecy, and ethical-symbolic interpretations. It concludes that Shambhala is best understood as a **late tantric kingdom that became a historically mobile sign of hidden legitimacy, future order, and moral renewal**. Its later portability does not erase its specific Kālacakra origin; rather, the density of that original construction helps explain why the symbol proved so adaptable across religious, political, and modern cultural worlds.

本文進一步將 Shambhala 放入更廣泛的佛教論戰語境中，比較其對 **Brahmins**（ब्राह्मण）、**Jains**（जैन）、**tīrthikas**（तीर्थिक）與 **Śaiva**（शैव）對手的修辭模式，指出時輪文獻代表了一種從教義駁斥轉向危機末世論的變化。其後，本文追溯 Shambhala 如何透過 **神智學（Theosophy）**、**Nicholas Roerich**，以及蘇聯與日本的神祕地緣政治挪用，而逐步脫離其佛教文本母體；同時也區分它與 **Agartha**、**Shangri-La** 各自不同的譜系。文章並比較清帝國利用藏傳佛教進行整合的方式，與泛蒙古、泛佛教政治對 Shambhala 的動員，指出多個內亞政體其實都在同一套神聖—政治象徵場域中競逐，只是它們在制度深度與時間取向上並不相同。

最後，本文考察 Shambhala 在一般佛教徒之間的流行、信仰與通俗後世生命，包括大型 **Kālacakra** 灌頂、想像性朝聖、危機預言，以及作為倫理—象徵地平線的現代詮釋。本文的結論是：Shambhala 最適合被理解為一個**晚期密教王國，後來演變為具有歷史流動性的隱秘合法性、未來秩序與道德更新之符號**。它後來的高度可移動性，並沒有抹去其特定的時輪教法起源；相反地，正是那個原初構造的高度密集性，解釋了它為何能夠在宗教、政治與現代文化世界中如此靈活地被重新使用。

# Review Article
## 1. Introduction: Why Shambhala Is Not One Thing

### 1.1 Definitional problem

**Shambhala** (Sanskrit: **Śambhala**, शम्भल; Tibetan: **Bde ’byung**, བདེ་འབྱུང་; Chinese: **香巴拉** / **時輪淨土**) is often treated in modern discourse as a generic “hidden kingdom,” but that is already a distortion. In the Buddhist archive, Shambhala is tied above all to the **Kālacakra** (Sanskrit: **Kālacakra**, कालचक्र; Tibetan: **Dus kyi ’khor lo**, དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་; Chinese: **時輪**) tradition, one of the latest major Buddhist tantric formations to emerge in India. Standard reference works place the Kālacakra tradition in late Indian Buddhism, around the 10th to early 11th century, and identify it as a final phase of Indian tantric Buddhism before its long afterlife in Tibet.

That textual anchoring matters because “Shambhala” has since been pulled into several unrelated genealogies. In Buddhist sources, it is a **sacred kingdom**, a **lineage-preserving polity**, and an **eschatological horizon**. In modern occultism, it becomes a universalized occult center. In popular modern culture, it is often collapsed into the same mental folder as **Agartha** or **Shangri-La**, even though those emerge from different historical and literary traditions. The first task of this article is therefore negative: to refuse the lazy equivalence that treats all hidden Asian kingdoms as versions of the same myth.

A useful starting distinction is the following:

| Term | Native form | Primary genealogy | Basic function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shambhala** | Śambhala (शम्भल), Bde ’byung (བདེ་འབྱུང་) | Buddhist Kālacakra tradition | Sacred kingdom, tantric custody, future restoration |
| **Kālacakra** | Kālacakra (कालचक्र), Dus kyi ’khor lo (དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་) | Late Indian Buddhist tantra | Doctrinal, ritual, cosmological, and soteriological system |
| **Kalāpa** | Kalāpa (कलाप) | Kālacakra sacred geography | Capital or central city of Shambhala in the tradition |

The table is elementary, but necessary, because most subsequent confusion begins with the failure to distinguish the **kingdom**, the **tantric corpus**, and the **sacred capital**.

### 1.2 Method and scope

This review treats Shambhala not as a timeless religious essence but as a **historically mobile symbol with a recoverable textual core**. The method is therefore layered. First, the article begins with the **Indian and Tibetan textual record**, especially the Kālacakra corpus and the **Vimalaprabhā** (Sanskrit: **Vimalaprabhā**, विमलप्रभा; Tibetan: **Dri med ’od**, དྲི་མེད་འོད་), since these are the main surviving sources through which Shambhala becomes legible. The Vimalaprabhā is regularly identified as the most authoritative early commentary in the tradition, and the surviving Kālacakra materials are usually understood as an abridged remnant of a larger remembered textual formation.

Second, the article reads Shambhala comparatively across **three linked but non-identical domains**: textual mythology, political imagination, and lived devotion. That means the discussion does not stop with the question “What did the Kālacakra texts say?” It also asks how Shambhala was used in Buddhist polemic, how it was transformed by modern occult and imperial projects, and how it survives among ordinary Buddhists as a devotional horizon. This approach is necessary because Shambhala’s history is not one of simple continuity. It is a history of repeated **refunctions**: from tantric sacred geography to apocalyptic polemic, from courtly-imperial symbol to popular religious aspiration.

Third, the scope of this article is deliberately **Buddhist-first but not Buddhist-only**. The analysis begins from Buddhist textual specificity and then follows the term outward into Inner Asian imperial politics, modern esotericism, and vernacular religious practice. That sequencing is important. Too much modern writing starts with Roerich, Theosophy, or New Age fantasy and works backward; the result is an inflated modern afterlife that obscures the narrower but more historically solid Kālacakra matrix from which Shambhala actually emerges.

### 1.3 Core thesis

The central claim of this article is that **Shambhala is best understood not as a single idea, but as a sequence of historical transformations anchored in a specific tantric source tradition**. In its earliest recoverable Buddhist form, Shambhala is a royal sacred geography within the Kālacakra system: a place where the teaching is preserved, ordered, and projected toward a future restoration. Britannica’s summary of the Kālacakra as a late, syncretic, astrologically elaborate tantric system is useful here, because it reminds us that Shambhala belongs to a textual world where cosmology, kingship, ritual, and historical anxiety are tightly interwoven.

From that starting point, however, Shambhala becomes something more mobile. It becomes, at different moments, an **apocalyptic engine** in anti-mleccha polemic, a **symbolic resource** in Inner Asian contests over Buddhist sovereignty, an **occultized hidden center** in modern esoteric universalism, and a **devotional horizon** for lay Buddhist publics encountering it through Kālacakra initiation, prophecy, and moral imagination. The argument of the review is not that these later forms are identical, nor that they are all equally faithful to the earliest textual strata. It is that they form a connected historical chain in which a specific Buddhist kingdom is progressively detached from its original doctrinal setting and made to carry larger claims about hidden truth, sacred rule, future victory, and civilizational renewal.

Put more sharply: Shambhala begins as a **late Buddhist tantric kingdom** and ends up as a **floating signifier of legitimacy and salvation**. The scholarly task is to reconstruct that movement without collapsing its stages into one another. A review article that does not preserve those distinctions will either romanticize the tradition or flatten it into modern fantasy. Both are analytical failures.

## 2. Shambhala in the Kālacakra Corpus

### 2.1 Late Indian tantric origins

Shambhala (Sanskrit: **Śambhala**, शम्भल; Tibetan: **Bde ’byung**, བདེ་འབྱུང་) enters Buddhist literature through the **Kālacakra** (कालचक्र; དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་) tradition, one of the latest major tantric formations to emerge in India. Standard reference works place the Kālacakra school in the **10th to early 11th century**, and characterize it as a late, elaborate, and in some respects highly syncretic phase of Indian Vajrayāna. This matters because it means Shambhala is not an ancient pan-Buddhist location inherited continuously from early canonical Buddhism. It is, instead, a product of **late tantric Buddhism**, already shaped by a world in which cosmology, kingship, ritual, and historical stress were being tied together with unusual intensity.

The tradition’s own account presents the Buddha as teaching the Kālacakra to **King Sucandra** (also Suchandra; Sanskrit: **Sucandra**, सुचन्द्र) of Shambhala. From the standpoint of internal tradition, this gives Shambhala a foundational role: it is not merely where the teaching later travels, but where it is **first received, curated, and ordered**. From the standpoint of historical scholarship, however, this should be read as a **textual self-legitimation narrative** rather than straightforward historiography. The importance of the story is not whether a historian can verify it, but that the Kālacakra corpus uses Shambhala to authorize itself as a total and protected revelation.

A second point follows from this. Because the Kālacakra tradition is late, Shambhala arrives already as a **dense composite symbol**: it is at once a place, a lineage, a political model, and an eschatological reserve. It is not introduced gradually over centuries across multiple Buddhist genres. It appears in a tradition that is already system-building, already concerned with total cosmological correlation, and already oriented toward the preservation of truth in a declining age. That compressed entry into the archive helps explain why Shambhala becomes so durable later: it begins life as a **high-load symbolic node**, not as a minor narrative detail.

### 2.2 The core textual archive

The extant textual core for reconstructing Shambhala is the **Kālacakra Tantra** itself—more precisely the surviving **shorter or abridged form**, often referred to as the **Laghu-Kālacakra** (लघुकालचक्र)—together with its authoritative commentary, the **Vimalaprabhā** (विमलप्रभा; Tibetan: **Dri med ’od**, དྲི་མེད་འོད་, “Stainless Light”). Tsadra’s reference materials describe the Vimalaprabhā as the key commentary expounding the root tantra, and note that the Kālacakra likely entered Indian Mahāyāna in the 10th century. Modern summaries also consistently identify the Vimalaprabhā as the most important commentary and the basis for subsequent Kālacakra exegesis in Tibet.

This archive is already marked by absence. The tradition remembers a larger, earlier root tantra no longer extant in full, while the surviving Kālacakra materials represent an abridged and transmitted form. That means the textual history of Shambhala must be reconstructed from **what survives**, not from a fully recoverable original corpus. The result is a common problem in tantric textual history: one must distinguish between the tradition’s memory of a larger revelation and the historically accessible layers preserved in manuscript, translation, and commentary. In practice, Shambhala is known to scholarship above all through this surviving **tantra-commentary pair**.

The role of attributed authorship is also significant. The Vimalaprabhā is traditionally linked to **Puṇḍarīka** (पुण्डरीक; Tibetan: **Pad ma dkar po**, པདྨ་དཀར་པོ་), presented as a king of Shambhala. This means that commentary itself is folded back into the sacred kingdom: Shambhala does not merely transmit the teaching; it also **interprets and stabilizes** it. In literary-historical terms, this creates a closed circuit of authority. Revelation originates in Shambhala, is preserved in Shambhala, and is authoritatively commented upon from within Shambhala’s royal lineage.

A concise map of the core archive is useful here:

| Text / term | Native script | Role in the corpus | Relevance to Shambhala |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kālacakra Tantra** | कालचक्र / དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་ | Root tantric system | Introduces Shambhala as sacred kingdom and transmission site |
| **Laghu-Kālacakra** | लघुकालचक्र | Surviving abridged form | Main extant textual basis for reconstruction |
| **Vimalaprabhā** | विमलप्रभा / དྲི་མེད་འོད་ | Foundational commentary | Interprets and codifies Shambhala through a royal-commentarial frame |
| **Puṇḍarīka** | पुण्डरीक / པདྨ་དཀར་པོ་ | Attributed commentator-king | Embeds textual authority in Shambhala’s own lineage |

The point of the table is simple: Shambhala is not merely a narrative setting in the Kālacakra corpus. It is built into the **very structure of textual authority**.

### 2.3 Kingship, custody, and sacred geography

Within the Kālacakra corpus, Shambhala is represented as a **royal Buddhist polity** centered on **Kalāpa** (कलाप), its capital. It is ruled by a sequence of kings who preserve the teaching, govern according to dharma, and prepare the ground for future restoration. Modern scholarly discussion of the **twenty-five Kulika/Kalki kings** of Shambhala confirms that dynastic sequence is integral to the tradition, not a later decorative addition. The kingdom is therefore textualized as both a place and a line of transmission: sacred geography and sacred genealogy reinforce one another.

This is where Shambhala becomes more than a Buddhist “pure land” in the loose popular sense. It is not primarily a postmortem paradise. It is a **functioning royal order** with social structure, lineage continuity, and doctrinal custodianship. The Kālacakra corpus imagines truth as something that must be politically housed. That is a striking feature. Instead of picturing liberation only as monastic discipline or meditative realization, the texts embed the preservation of ultimate teaching in a righteous kingdom governed by enlightened or quasi-enlightened rulers. Shambhala is thus a model of **sacralized polity**, where sovereignty and revelation are mutually sustaining.

The kings most often foregrounded in the tradition—such as **Mañjuśrīkīrti** (मञ्जुश्रीकीर्ति) and **Puṇḍarīka**—do conceptual work beyond mere genealogy. They stand for codification, purification, and ordering. In traditional narrative, these rulers condense and interpret the teaching so it can survive in a degenerate age. In analytical terms, they personify the transition from revelation to canon, and from canon to protected lineage. That is why the kingdom matters: Shambhala is a way of imagining how a final, comprehensive tantra remains intact when the surrounding world is unstable.

### 2.4 Eschatology and future restoration

The Shambhala of the Kālacakra corpus is not only archival. It is also **future-facing**. The same kingdom that preserves the teaching is destined to become the source of a final restoration, led by a later ruler often identified in modern summaries as **Raudra Kalkin / Rudra Cakrin** (रुद्रचक्रिन्). Encyclopedic treatments of Shambhala note the prophecy that a future king will emerge from Shambhala with a vast army to defeat forces of decline and usher in a renewed age. Even when modern summaries are schematic, the essential structure is clear: Shambhala is both **storehouse and reserve force**, both memory and future intervention.

This eschatological dimension should not be read as detachable from the rest of the system. In Kālacakra thought, outer history, cosmic cycles, polity, and inner yogic processes are correlated. Britannica’s description of the Kālacakra as an astrologically oriented and divergent tantric school is useful because it points to precisely this wider architecture: time itself is systematized, and Shambhala is inserted into that architecture as the place where righteous time can be preserved and reactivated. Eschatology here is not merely a final battle fantasy. It is part of a larger tantric logic in which decay, concealment, revelation, and restoration are structurally linked.

That said, one should not over-spiritualize the material too early. Later interpreters often emphasize symbolic or inner readings, and those are real, but in the textual presentation Shambhala is still given dynasties, cities, kings, and future military agency. The most accurate formulation is that the Kālacakra corpus deliberately maintains a **double register**: Shambhala is both a sacred kingdom in outer narrative space and a node in a larger tantric hermeneutic where external geography mirrors internal realization. Any reading that keeps only one side of that structure will misread the corpus.

The upshot of Section 2 is straightforward. In the Kālacakra corpus, Shambhala is a **late Buddhist tantric sacred kingdom** defined by four linked functions: it is the place where the teaching is first received, the archive in which it is preserved, the royal order through which it is interpreted, and the eschatological source from which it will return. Everything later—anti-mleccha polemic, occult universalization, imperial appropriation, vernacular devotion—depends on this original compression of **geography, lineage, sovereignty, and futurity** into one symbol.

## 3. Polemic, Islam, and the Problem of the *Mleccha* (म्लेच्छ)

### 3.1 Historical pressure and textual response

The anti-*mleccha* (म्लेच्छ; Tibetan often rendered through equivalents for “barbarian” or foreign enemy rather than a single fixed loan form) passages in the **Kālacakra** (कालचक्र; དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་) corpus are best read as a **late Buddhist textual response to new political pressure**, not as a timeless statement of Buddhist doctrine. Standard reference works place the Kālacakra tradition in the **10th to early 11th century**, that is, in the final phase of Indian tantric Buddhism, precisely when Muslim political expansion had become a major fact in regions relevant to Buddhist institutions. Britannica explicitly situates the Kālacakra as arising in India in the 10th century and as belonging to the final phase of Indian Tantric Buddhism “just prior to the Muslim invasion,” while Oxford Bibliographies likewise describes it as one of the latest Buddhist tantric traditions of North India.

This timing is not incidental. John Newman’s study of Islam in the Kālacakra argues that the corpus is exceptional because Sanskrit Buddhist literature otherwise says very little about Islamic beliefs and practices. The Kālacakra materials, by contrast, treat the *mleccha* not merely as generic outsiders but as a historically recognizable adversary, often identifiable with Muslims and sometimes with **Tājiks / Tāyin** in the text’s refracted vocabulary. The result is a form of Buddhist writing shaped by real contact, partial information, and crisis mentality.

The basic interpretive point, then, is narrow but important: the Kālacakra corpus does not invent its enemy ex nihilo, yet neither does it describe Islam neutrally. It transforms a historically encountered religious-political presence into an apocalyptic category of anti-dharma. In that sense, the *mleccha* passages belong to the same moment as the rest of the Kālacakra system: a late tantric attempt to preserve and totalize Buddhist order under perceived conditions of civilizational threat.

### 3.2 Recognizable Islam, distorted Islam

The hostility of the Kālacakra texts should not obscure the fact that they preserve **recognizable information about Islam**. Newman’s analysis notes references to **Allah / al-Raḥmān**, **Muḥammad**, **Mecca**, **Baghdad**, ritual washing, and prayer multiple times daily. These are not random fantasies. They indicate that the authors or transmitters of the relevant passages had access—directly or indirectly—to some concrete knowledge of Muslim practices and sacred history.

At the same time, the texts subject that knowledge to aggressive polemical distortion. Newman summarizes the Kālacakra presentation of Islam as a religion of violence, one that consecrates slaughter and serves a merciless god of death. He also notes obvious inaccuracies and hostile inventions, including the charge that Muslims consume blood, despite the fact that blood consumption is prohibited in Islamic law. This combination of partial accuracy and defamatory embellishment is analytically important. It shows that the texts are not performing ethnography. They are engaged in **boundary construction**: taking observed practices, reclassifying them under Buddhist demonological and sacrificial categories, and producing a morally legible enemy.

One of the most revealing features of the corpus is that its depiction of Islam is not wholly flat. Newman points out that the Kālacakra materials can register Muslim rejection of caste distinctions, presenting the *mlecchas* as living “as a single group” rather than according to multiple inherited caste divisions. That does not make the presentation sympathetic overall, but it does show that the text’s polemic is assembled from a mixture of observation, selective recognition, and hostile recoding. The enemy is not a pure fantasy; it is a distorted mirror of a real interlocutor.

A concise comparison clarifies the structure:

| Feature | In the Kālacakra presentation | Analytical assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Ritual washing / repeated prayer | Recognized with some accuracy | Evidence of real contact or informed transmission |
| Allah, Muḥammad, Mecca, Baghdad | Refracted but identifiable | Partial knowledge of Islamic sacred vocabulary |
| Violence / sacrificial cruelty | Strongly emphasized | Polemical recoding of political and ritual difference |
| Eating blood, demonic god, savage conduct | Asserted polemically | Distortion, slander, and civilizational disgust-marking |
| Rejection of caste | Acknowledged | Selective recognition of a real social contrast |

The point is not to rescue the text as “surprisingly fair,” because it is not. The point is to show that its hostility is built from **recognition plus recoding**, not from ignorance alone.

### 3.3 Apocalypse, war, and inner allegory

The most difficult material is the **eschatological war narrative** tied to Shambhala (शम्भल; བདེ་འབྱུང་). In the better-known prophetic frame, a future ruler of Shambhala—usually rendered in modern scholarship as **Raudra Kalkin / Rudra Cakrin** (रुद्रचक्रिन्)—will defeat the *mleccha* forces and restore Buddhist order. This is not a marginal embellishment. It is part of the way the Kālacakra system imagines the survival and eventual vindication of dharma in a dark age. Modern summaries of the Kālacakra and Shambhala traditions consistently preserve this feature, even when simplifying the details.

That said, the war material should not be read in only one register. Newman emphasizes that Kālacakra tradition also reads the battle in **microcosmic and yogic terms**: the enemy can be interpreted as the afflictions (*kleśa*, क्लेश), and the victory of Shambhala can be understood as an inner triumph of disciplined realization over ignorance and violence. This inner reading is not a modern embarrassment strategy imposed from outside; it belongs to the broader Kālacakra habit of correlating outer cosmos, polity, and body.

But the existence of an inner allegory does **not** cancel the outer rhetoric. This point matters because modern apologetic readings often move too fast from prophecy to symbolism, as if allegory acquits the text of polemical violence. It does not. The exoteric narrative still imagines a final purification of the world through the defeat of a demonized religious enemy. The inner reading complicates the text; it does not neutralize it. A critical reading has to preserve both levels at once: **outer apocalyptic hostility and inner tantric hermeneutics**.

### 3.4 Why these passages matter

The *mleccha* passages matter for three reasons. First, they are among the rare cases where Sanskrit Buddhist literature addresses Islam directly enough to leave a reconstructable textual record. Second, they show how a late Buddhist tradition processed political shock: not by simple reportage, but by translating a new religious-political rival into inherited South Asian categories of barbarism, sacrifice, and end-time struggle. Third, they reveal that Kālacakra Shambhala is not just a hidden kingdom of wisdom; it is also a **polemical and eschatological engine**.

The conclusion of this section should therefore be stated precisely. The Kālacakra corpus does not give us reliable information about Islam as such. It gives us reliable information about **how a late Buddhist tantric community imagined Islam as threat**. That distinction is foundational for the rest of the article, because it explains why Shambhala can later function not only as sacred geography, but also as a reservoir of future violence, restoration, and civilizational reversal.

## 4. Shambhala and Buddhist Rhetoric toward Religious Others

### 4.1 Brahmins (ब्राह्मण), birth, and ethical inversion

One of the most durable features of Buddhist polemic against **Brahmins** (Sanskrit: **brāhmaṇa**, ब्राह्मण; Pali: **brāhmaṇa**; Chinese often rendered **婆羅門**; Tibetan often translated descriptively rather than as a fixed loan) is that it tends to work through **ethical inversion rather than eschatological annihilation**. In early Buddhist discourse, the central move is not “destroy the Brahmin enemy,” but “redefine who counts as a true Brahmin.” The **Vāseṭṭha Sutta** (Pali: **Vāseṭṭha-sutta**) states explicitly that one is not a brahmin “by birth” but by one’s actions. That is sharp social critique, aimed at lineage ideology and ritual prestige, but it remains a contest over **status, truth, and moral authority**, not a prophecy of civilizational war.

This matters for the present comparison because the anti-*mleccha* (म्लेच्छ) rhetoric in the **Kālacakra** (कालचक्र; Tibetan: **Dus kyi ’khor lo**, དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་) corpus is much more than another instance of anti-Brahmin refutation. Early Buddhist anti-Brahmin discourse typically says: your claims of inherited purity are false, your sacrificial order is morally compromised, and our path defines spiritual superiority more correctly. Even when severe, the rhetoric is still fundamentally **argumentative and classificatory**. The opponent is an erring rival within a shared Indic field, not an apocalyptic anti-world.

That older anti-Brahmin polemical habit does, however, survive inside the Kālacakra material. John Newman notes that the **Vimalaprabhā** (विमलप्रभा; Tibetan: **Dri med ’od**, དྲི་མེད་འོད་) compares Vedic sacrificial violence with *mleccha* practice and can even suggest that Brahmins who persist in such rites degenerate toward barbarism. This is analytically important: the Kālacakra does not discard earlier Buddhist anti-Brahmin criticism. It **absorbs** it, then places it inside a broader end-time framework where sacrificial violence, foreign threat, and the decline of dharma become structurally linked.

### 4.2 Jains (जैन), tīrthikas (तीर्थिक), and philosophical rivals

When Buddhist texts engage **Jains** (जैन), **tīrthikas** (तीर्थिक, “ford-makers,” often used polemically for non-Buddhist religious rivals), and other philosophical opponents, the dominant mode is usually **doxographic and soteriological**. The enemy is someone with the wrong account of karma, liberation, selfhood, ritual efficacy, or asceticism. In such cases the rhetoric can be dismissive, sarcastic, or systematically refutational, but its basic form is still recognizably **debate-centered**. The rival is a mistaken thinker or practitioner, not yet a cosmic invader. That pattern is consistent with broader scholarship on Buddhist interreligious argument, which treats Buddhist encounters with Brahmins, Jains, and other śramaṇa rivals as part of a long history of philosophical competition and sectarian system-building.

This is precisely why the Kālacakra corpus marks a shift. Newman points out that the tradition can be relatively tolerant of **non-violent forms of tīrthika practice**, even while rejecting them as inferior. That comparative tolerance throws the anti-*mleccha* passages into sharper relief. The text distinguishes between ordinary non-Buddhist rivals—who can still be situated within a manageable plural field of error—and *mlecchas*, who are cast as a qualitatively different type of threat: a community whose religion is represented as intrinsically violent and whose victory would mean the destruction of the dharma-bearing order itself.

The difference, then, is not simply that some non-Buddhists were criticized more harshly than others. It is that the **register** changes. With Jains, Brahmins, and generic tīrthikas, Buddhism typically contests **truth-claims**. With the Kālacakra’s *mleccha*, Buddhism contests **historical survival**. Once the issue becomes survival rather than doctrinal correctness, the rhetoric can thicken into prophecy, militarization, and civilizational reversal. That is the escalation that has to be kept in view.

### 4.3 Śaivas (शैव), tantric rivals, and the rhetoric of subjugation

The comparison becomes more complicated with **Śaiva** (शैव) and other tantric rivals, because later Buddhist tantric literature is already comfortable with forms of violent imagery, subjugation, and ritual aggression. David Gray’s work on tantric Buddhist ritual argues that Buddhist tantras contain both **rhetorical violence** and depictions or prescriptions of **ritual violence**, sometimes justified through appeals to spiritually advanced awareness or non-dual gnosis (**advayajñāna**, अद्वयज्ञान). This is an important corrective to romantic claims that Buddhism only became “violent” when confronting Islam. Tantric Buddhism already possessed languages of coercion, wrath, and symbolic destruction.

But even here, one should resist flattening the comparison. Much tantric violence is aimed at **subduing demons, hostile forces, ritual enemies, or sectarian rivals** within a shared ritual universe. Its logic is often initiatory, symbolic, or liturgically performative. The rhetoric may be extravagant and threatening, but it does not necessarily construct the opponent as a foreign civilizational anti-order in the same way that the anti-*mleccha* passages do. Gray’s analysis is useful precisely because it distinguishes between **hyperbolic rhetorical violence** and more concrete violent ritual prescriptions; that distinction helps us see that not all Buddhist violence-talk is of the same kind.

So while Buddhist tantric rhetoric toward Śaivas and related competitors may involve domination, humiliation, or ritual defeat, it usually remains inside a **shared Indic-tantric grammar of competition**. The Kālacakra’s anti-*mleccha* rhetoric goes beyond that by projecting a final confrontation between dharmic order and an enemy imagined as both religiously alien and historically catastrophic. The difference is not absolute, but it is real. Śaiva rivalry may be sharp sectarian contest; the *mleccha* passages move toward **millenarian civilizational struggle**.

A compact comparison makes the distinction clearer:

| Opponent category | Native term(s) | Dominant Buddhist rhetorical mode | Typical stakes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brahmins | brāhmaṇa (ब्राह्मण) | Ethical inversion, critique of birth and sacrifice | Moral authority, social classification |
| Jains / tīrthikas | jaina (जैन), tīrthika (तीर्थिक) | Debate, refutation, wrong-view critique | Correct doctrine, liberation-path |
| Śaiva / tantric rivals | śaiva (शैव) and others | Subjugation, wrath, ritualized violence | Sectarian supremacy, ritual power |
| *Mleccha* in Kālacakra | mleccha (म्लेच्छ) | Apocalyptic demonization, prophetic war | Survival of dharma, civilizational order |

The table oversimplifies at the edges, but the broad pattern holds: the rhetoric toward the *mleccha* is distinguished less by the mere existence of hostility than by the **scale and eschatological charge** of that hostility.

### 4.4 Why the anti-*mleccha* passages are distinctive

The anti-*mleccha* passages in the Kālacakra are distinctive because they **escalate familiar Buddhist polemical habits into a new register**. Buddhism had long criticized sacrifice, inherited purity, false asceticism, and rival systems of liberation. Tantric Buddhism had also developed idioms of wrath, subjugation, and ritual violence. What is unusual in the Kālacakra corpus is the way these strands are braided together under the pressure of a new historical enemy. Anti-sacrificial critique, sectarian exclusion, and tantric violence-talk are all still present, but they are now oriented toward a vision of end-time conflict in which the opponent is represented as a force capable of extinguishing Buddhist order itself.

This is why the Kālacakra material should neither be sanitized nor exceptionalized too crudely. It is not an alien intrusion into otherwise peaceful Buddhism; the tradition already possessed polemical and violent resources. But it is also not “just more of the same.” The move from critique of **wrong view** to fear of **historical elimination** changes the texture of the discourse. Once the preservation of dharma is imagined as requiring a future war led by Shambhala (शम्भल; བདེ་འབྱུང་), Buddhist rhetoric becomes not merely sectarian but **apocalyptic**.

The analytical payoff of this comparison is straightforward. It allows us to see Shambhala not only as sacred geography, but as the point where Buddhist discourse about kingship, preservation, and righteous violence converges. Without that convergence, the later political and modern afterlives of Shambhala would be harder to explain. The kingdom matters because it is not simply hidden wisdom; it is also the imagined site from which threatened order may return in decisive force.

## 5. From Shambhala (शम्भल / བདེ་འབྱུང་) to Agartha (Agarttha) and Shangri-La

### 5.1 Theosophical universalization

By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, **Shambhala** (Sanskrit: **Śambhala**, शम्भल; Tibetan: **Bde ’byung**, བདེ་འབྱུང་) had begun to slip out of its original **Kālacakra** (कालचक्र; Tibetan: **Dus kyi ’khor lo**, དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་) frame and enter a new comparative-occult marketplace. That transition did not happen because Buddhist texts themselves equated Shambhala with a generic hidden paradise. On the contrary, the Kālacakra archive presents Shambhala as a specific sacred kingdom tied to **Suchandra** (सुचन्द्र), the **Kalki / Kulika** rulers, and the preservation of the tantra. The Dalai Lama’s own explanatory material still preserves that traditional structure: the Buddha teaches the tantra to King Suchandra; Suchandra returns to Shambhala and composes a vast commentary; and the teaching is transmitted through the kings of Shambhala before re-entering India.

What changed in modern esotericism was not the textual core, but the **frame of interpretation**. Once Western occult movements became interested in Tibet, Inner Asia, and “hidden masters,” Shambhala could be detached from its doctrinal specificity and reimagined as a **universal occult center**. In that new frame, it no longer functioned primarily as a Buddhist royal polity preserving a late tantra. It became instead a node in a speculative map of hidden wisdom, invisible adepts, primordial tradition, and planetary supervision. This was the decisive conceptual shift: Shambhala moved from **sacred geography internal to a Buddhist system** to **portable esoteric symbol**. The significance of that move is historical rather than merely semantic. Once Shambhala was treated as a universal secret center, it became easy to merge it with adjacent myths that had no real genealogical dependence on the Kālacakra corpus.

The distinction may be stated bluntly. In Buddhist textual history, Shambhala is a **particular kingdom** within a particular tantric cosmology. In modern occult reception, “Shamballa” or “Shambhala” becomes a **generalized hidden source of wisdom**. The latter is not simply the former in translation. It is a **reinterpretive abstraction** produced by a different intellectual world.

### 5.2 Agartha (Agarttha) and the occult underground

The clearest neighboring myth in that modern esoteric world is **Agartha / Agarttha**. Unlike Shambhala, Agartha does not emerge from Buddhist tantra. It is a modern occult construction strongly associated with the French esoteric revival and especially **Alexandre Saint-Yves d’Alveydre**. A review from the Theosophical Society in America notes that Saint-Yves was among the earliest influential sources for the myth of a hidden kingdom in Central Asia and that in **1886** he published *Mission de l’Inde*, presenting **Agarttha** as a secret, subterranean realm flourishing somewhere in Central Asia. The same source describes Agarttha as a hidden kingdom tied to Saint-Yves’s doctrine of **Synarchy**, a unified sacred-secular order.

This genealogy matters because Agartha is not, at origin, a Buddhist hidden land. It is an **occult-political myth** with subterranean and hollow-earth tendencies, embedded in late nineteenth-century French esotericism rather than in Sanskrit or Tibetan Buddhist literature. Saint-Yves’s Agarttha is explicitly shaped by his broader theory of ideal government, primordial wisdom, and civilizational reform. That already makes it structurally different from Kālacakra Shambhala, whose primary functions are tantric custody, royal legitimation, and future restoration of dharma.

Yet the two myths quickly became entangled. The same Theosophical review openly notes that “probably most” esotericists identified Agarttha with Tibetan **Shambhala**. That sentence is extremely revealing. It shows that the conflation was not inherited from Buddhist sources; it was **produced by esoteric comparison**, within a milieu predisposed to assume that all Asian hidden kingdoms were fragments of one primordial secret tradition. Once that assumption took hold, Shambhala and Agartha could be treated as alternate names for the same concealed center, despite their very different textual pedigrees.

A concise contrast helps:

| Term | Native / historical form | Earliest relevant context | Basic structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shambhala** | Śambhala (शम्भल), Bde ’byung (བདེ་འབྱུང་) | Buddhist Kālacakra corpus | Sacred kingdom, royal custodianship, eschatological restoration |
| **Agartha / Agarttha** | Modern occult spelling cluster | Saint-Yves d’Alveydre, French occult revival | Hidden or subterranean kingdom, synarchic sacred polity, occult center |

The table is not just classificatory. It makes clear that Agartha is **parallel**, not ancestral, to the modern occultized Shambhala. The two intersect historically in modern esotericism, not in Buddhist scripture.

### 5.3 Shangri-La and literary utopia

If Agartha represents the occult underground version of the hidden kingdom, **Shangri-La** represents its twentieth-century literary softening. Britannica defines Shangri-La as the **fictional utopian lamasery** located in Tibet’s Kunlun Mountains in **James Hilton’s 1933 novel** *Lost Horizon*. In the novel, Shangri-La is a remote sanctuary free from war and crime, marked by extreme longevity, serenity, and preservation of cultural treasures. Britannica also notes that the term entered modern lexicon as a synonym for “utopia.”

This is a very different kind of object from both Shambhala and Agartha. Shangri-La is not an occult doctrinal center and not a Buddhist tantric kingdom with dynastic succession. It is a **modern literary utopia**, built for readers of interwar English fiction. Its structure is recognizably modern: isolated valley, timeless beauty, anti-modern refuge, selective preservation of civilization, and atmosphere of spiritualized remoteness. In other words, Shangri-La is what happens when the hidden kingdom motif is translated into a form suitable for liberal-imperial fantasy, travel romance, and modern escapist longing.

Britannica also states that some readers have thought the Tibetan Buddhist myth of **Shambhala** informed Hilton’s description. That is plausible at the level of broad inspiration: both involve a hidden mountain realm preserving wisdom until the world is ready for renewal. But that is very far from identity. Shangri-La is not simply Shambhala under another name. It lacks the doctrinal density of the Kālacakra tradition, the royal succession of the **Kalkis** (कल्कि), and the explicit apocalyptic role of a final dharmic victory. What it preserves is not Buddhist tantra, but the **aesthetic shell** of remote spiritual refuge.

### 5.4 The category error of modern conflation

The modern habit of grouping **Shambhala**, **Agartha**, and **Shangri-La** together is therefore understandable but analytically sloppy. The three belong to **different genealogies**, even though they overlap in imagery.

Shambhala belongs first to the **late Indian Buddhist tantric world**: a sacred kingdom of kingship, commentary, preservation, and future restoration. Agartha belongs to the **modern occult revival**, especially the French esoteric and later Theosophical orbit, where hidden kingdoms become sites of primordial wisdom, secret hierarchy, and subterranean sovereignty. Shangri-La belongs to **twentieth-century fiction**, where the hidden kingdom motif is aestheticized into a utopian refuge for modern readers disenchanted with violence and modernity.

A fuller comparison makes the distinction explicit:

| Dimension | Shambhala (शम्भल / བདེ་འབྱུང་) | Agartha / Agarttha | Shangri-La |
|---|---|---|---|
| Historical origin | Buddhist Kālacakra tradition | French occult revival / modern esotericism | James Hilton’s 1933 fiction |
| Ontological type | Sacred kingdom within tantric cosmology | Hidden or subterranean occult realm | Fictional utopian lamasery |
| Primary function | Preserve tantra and enable future restoration | House primordial wisdom and ideal sacred order | Offer literary refuge and anti-modern paradise |
| Relation to kingship | Dynastic rulers, especially Kalki kings | Esoteric sovereignty, “Lord of the World” motifs | Minimal or softened political structure |
| Relation to apocalypse | Strong, explicit | Variable, often occult-hieratic | Weak; mostly utopian and preservational |

The key analytical claim is that the conflation of these terms is itself a historical phenomenon. It is not simply a mistake to be corrected; it is evidence of how a specifically Buddhist sacred kingdom was **translated, abstracted, and repackaged** for modern esoteric and literary consumption. That modern blur is part of the story. But it should not be mistaken for the original archive.

The implication for the rest of this article is straightforward. Once Shambhala is detached from the Kālacakra frame and placed alongside Agartha and Shangri-La, it becomes available for new uses: occult geopolitics, civilizational fantasy, spiritual tourism, and modern ideological projection. Those later developments depend on precisely the slippage mapped in this section. The next step, therefore, is to examine the figures and movements—especially **Roerich** and related esoteric-political formations—that turned that slippage into a fully mobile modern myth.

## 6. Roerich, Occult Geopolitics, and the Modern Myth of Hidden Sovereignty

### 6.1 Roerich (Рерих), Agni Yoga (Агни Йога), and the bridge from doctrine to myth

If **Theosophy** made Shambhala (शम्भल / བདེ་འབྱུང་) portable, **Nicholas Roerich** (Николай Рерих) made it vivid, mobile, and politically suggestive. Britannica identifies Roerich as a Russian artist, set designer, writer, and “popular mystic,” and notes that after emigrating to the United States in 1920 he and Helena Roerich founded the **Agni Yoga Society** (Агни Йога), a movement combining elements of Eastern thought with Western moral-philosophical concerns. That description is basic but sufficient for the present argument: Roerich was not a Buddhist commentator in the line of the **Kālacakra** (कालचक्र / དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་) scholastics. He was a modern esoteric synthesizer working in a transnational spiritual marketplace.

That distinction matters because Roerich’s importance lies precisely in **detachment without indifference**. He did not ignore Tibetan Buddhist materials; he repurposed them. In his hands, Shambhala ceased to function primarily as a late tantric sacred kingdom tied to **Suchandra** (सुचन्द्र) and the **Kalki** (कल्कि) kings, and instead became a symbol of hidden authority, civilizational rescue, and approaching transformation. Roerich therefore stands at the hinge between two worlds: on one side, the Buddhist archive; on the other, a modern universalist myth in which Inner Asia becomes the theater of concealed wisdom and geopolitical destiny. This is the crucial bridge. It is not that Roerich faithfully transmitted the Kālacakra tradition. It is that he made Shambhala newly available to audiences who would never read the **Vimalaprabhā** (विमलप्रभा / དྲི་མེད་འོད་).

Roerich’s role should therefore be framed as **translation through exaggeration**. He did not invent Shambhala, but he amplified its visual, civilizational, and messianic potential in ways that loosened it further from Buddhist doctrinal control. Once that happened, Shambhala could circulate not merely as sacred geography but as a modern myth of hidden sovereignty.

### 6.2 Expeditionary theater and the political potency of Shambhala

Roerich’s significance was not confined to books and paintings. It took expeditionary form. James Boyd’s study of Roerich’s **1934–35 Inner Mongolian expedition** states directly that the “legendary kingdom of Shambhala had a complex political potency” for North and Inner Asian Buddhist-influenced cultures and that both **Russians and Japanese** sought to exploit that potency during the 1920s and 1930s. This is the key move from symbolic to geopolitical history. Shambhala was not merely admired as a mystical idea; it was recognized as a usable political language.

Boyd’s article is especially valuable because it refuses both naïve romanticism and crude conspiracy. It does not reduce Roerich to a mere charlatan or spy, but neither does it portray him as a neutral seeker of wisdom. Rather, Roerich appears as a figure operating in a zone where scholarship, mysticism, expedition, soft diplomacy, and political fantasy overlapped. Boyd notes that Roerich had convinced some Soviet circles he could help assemble lamas interested in a Buddhist commonwealth and that he had earlier presented himself as capable of mediating among Inner Asian religious elites. Whether or not every such ambition was realistic is secondary. What matters is that Shambhala here functioned as a **politically resonant code** through which competing actors imagined regional influence.

This expeditionary context also sharpened the myth itself. A hidden kingdom sought through travel, rumor, and strategic passage across Inner Asia is no longer simply a doctrinal object. It becomes part of a theater of **semi-belief**: sufficiently sacred to attract devotion, sufficiently vague to enable projection, and sufficiently prestigious to justify political maneuver. That is one reason Roerich matters more than many lesser occultists. He fused image, movement, and geopolitical suggestion into a single modern Shambhala imaginary.

### 6.3 Soviet occult appropriation and the search for usable hidden knowledge

If Roerich dramatized Shambhala, certain Soviet circles attempted to **instrumentalize** it. Study Buddhism’s overview of non-ethnic Russian Buddhism reports that within the **OGPU** milieu, **Gleb Bokii** (Глеб Бокий) and **Aleksandr Barchenko** (Александр Барченко) organized a **Kālacakra study circle**, planned an expedition to Central Asia and Tibet, and sought the so-called “wisdom of Shambhala.” The same source notes that these figures were arrested and executed in 1937. Even if one treats some later retellings cautiously, the broad point is secure: there were Soviet attempts to treat Shambhala not simply as exotic lore, but as a possible repository of **usable power, hidden science, or ideological leverage**.

This is a significant mutation. In the Buddhist archive, Shambhala preserves tantra and dharma. In Soviet occult-political imagination, Shambhala becomes something like a **technology reserve**—a source of secret knowledge that might be appropriated for state or revolutionary purposes. That is a thoroughly modern transformation. It does not require real belief in the Kālacakra system as such. It only requires the conviction that ancient Asian traditions may conceal capacities—psychological, spiritual, social, or even quasi-scientific—that modern states can exploit.

The Soviet case also shows that occult geopolitics is not merely a right-wing or reactionary phenomenon. Shambhala could be appropriated by a formally atheist revolutionary state apparatus precisely because the symbol had become detached from doctrinal orthodoxy. Once abstracted into “hidden wisdom,” it could be folded into projects that were entirely alien to its original Buddhist setting. That is the deeper importance of the Soviet episode: it reveals how thoroughly Shambhala had become **ideologically transferable** by the early twentieth century.

### 6.4 The mobility of the symbol: from sacred kingdom to floating center

By the interwar period, Shambhala had become a **floating signifier of hidden sovereignty**. Russia could be cast as its northern political counterpart; Japanese actors could exploit its prestige in Inner Asia; Soviet occultists could imagine it as a source of secret power; Roerich could treat it as the key to a renewed Eurasian civilizational order. Study Buddhism’s survey of Russian and Japanese schemes in Tibet records that **Agvan Dorjiev** (Агван Доржиев) argued that **Russia was the Northern Kingdom of Shambhala** and that later Japanese propaganda in Mongolia and Inner Asia could claim that **Japan was Shambhala**. Boyd independently notes that Inner Mongolian rebels at one stage hoped for deliverance from the mythical legions of Shambhala, “believed by this point to be Japan.” These examples matter because they show that by this stage Shambhala’s function was no longer doctrinal consistency but **legitimating elasticity**.

A compact mapping clarifies the shift:

| Historical setting | Actor(s) | What Shambhala becomes | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buddhist Kālacakra corpus | Suchandra, Kalki kings | Sacred kingdom | Custody of tantra and future restoration |
| Roerich / Agni Yoga world | Roerich circle | Hidden civilizational center | Spiritual-geopolitical myth |
| Soviet occult milieu | Bokii, Barchenko, OGPU-linked study circles | Reservoir of secret wisdom | Instrumental hidden knowledge |
| Russian / Japanese Inner Asian schemes | Dorjiev, Japanese propagandists, regional actors | Northern legitimating power | Geopolitical and messianic alignment |

The table shows the key historical transformation. Shambhala does not merely “spread.” It changes **ontological status**. It moves from a kingdom in a tantric system to a modern mythic center capable of legitimating ideologically incompatible projects.

The point of this section, then, is not that Roerich or Soviet occultists “misunderstood” Shambhala in a simple sense. The stronger claim is that they participated in a broader modern process in which sacred geography was transformed into **geopolitical metaphysics**. Once that happened, Shambhala could be made to authorize projects as different as Buddhist revival, Russian imperial aspiration, Japanese expansionism, and Soviet occult experimentation. That is the modern myth of hidden sovereignty: the belief that beyond visible institutions there exists a concealed center from which true authority, wisdom, and renewal may yet emerge.

## 7. Inner Asian Empire and the Competition for Buddhist Legitimacy

### 7.1 Qing (清) imperial integration through Tibetan Buddhism (ནང་བསྟན་)

The **Qing empire** (清朝) did not use Tibetan Buddhism (Tibetan: **Bstan pa / Nang bstan**, བསྟན་པ / ནང་བསྟན་; Chinese: **藏傳佛教**) merely as ornamental court religion. It used it as a serious instrument of **imperial integration**, especially in relation to **Mongolia** (Монгол / 蒙古) and **Tibet** (བོད་ / 西藏). Recent comparative work argues that the Qing pursued frontier incorporation through the **Gelug** (Tibetan: **Dge lugs**, དགེ་ལུགས་; Chinese: **格魯派**) order, but did so with regionally differentiated strategies: heavier administrative intervention in Tibet, more symbolic and patronage-based integration in Mongolia. This is a crucial point because it shows that Qing Buddhist policy was not a loose ideology but a calibrated program of rule.

In Tibet, the Qing court tied Buddhist legitimacy to imperial supervision through institutions, appointments, and the management of reincarnation politics. In Mongolia, by contrast, the court more often worked through stipends, titles, ritual prestige, and the language of imperial protection. The unifying logic was that the emperor could appear as the guarantor of the **“Yellow Teaching”** (Chinese: **黃教**; referring broadly to the Gelug establishment) across Inner Asia. This made Buddhist patronage part of the architecture of sovereignty, not merely a supplement to military control.

This is where older simplistic formulations fail. The Qing did not simply “manipulate religion” from outside. Rather, they entered an already existing Buddhist symbolic field and tried to occupy its center. Johan Elverskog’s work on late imperial Mongolia emphasizes that Qing rule was not experienced only as foreign domination imposed on passive Mongols; it also worked through the production of a political world in which many Mongols came to imagine themselves as subjects of **“Our Great Qing”** within an Inner Asian imperial order partly articulated through Buddhism. That is a stronger and more historically plausible formulation than the blunt propaganda model.

### 7.2 Zunghars (Зүүнгар / 準噶爾) and rival Buddhist-capable sovereignty

The **Zunghars** (also **Dzungars**; Mongolian: **Зүүнгар**, Chinese: **準噶爾**) must be treated not merely as a military rival of the Qing, but as a competing Inner Asian polity operating in the same Buddhist-political arena. Scholarship on the Zunghar conquest of Central Tibet stresses that Zunghar intervention in Tibet was not reducible to raw invasion. It also unfolded in a field where control over Tibetan affairs carried implications of **religious legitimacy**, patronage, and wider Mongol prestige.

This matters because the Qing were not the only power capable of presenting themselves as defenders or reorganizers of Buddhist order. The Zunghars also had access to steppe imperial memory, Mongol political idioms, and Buddhist-capable legitimation. Their rulers may not have systematized sacral monarchy in quite the same polished way as the Qing court at **Chengde** (承德), but they were plainly competing in the same zone of meaning. Once Tibet became a site of intervention, the stakes were not only territorial. They were also about **who could claim authority within the Tibetan-Mongol Buddhist ecumene**.

That is the key comparative adjustment. If one treats the Zunghars as merely secular steppe militarists and the Qing as uniquely sacral-imperial, the field is distorted. The better reading is that both polities understood that Inner Asian sovereignty required more than coercion. It required some degree of credibility within a world where Buddhist institutions, reincarnate hierarchs, and transregional patronage mattered. The Qing eventually built the more durable imperial machine, but the Zunghars remained dangerous precisely because they were not external to that symbolic economy.

### 7.3 Manchu (ᠮᠠᠨᠵᡠ / 滿洲) sacral monarchy and eschatological sensitivity

The **Manchu court** (ᠮᠠᠨᠵᡠ ᡤᡠᡵᡠᠨ / 滿洲朝廷) did not generally express “eschatology” in the same prophetic register as later **Shambhala** (शम्भल / བདེ་འབྱུང་) politics. Its sensitivity was subtler. It lay in an awareness that sovereignty in Inner Asia had to be made **cosmologically legible**. The clearest expression of this is the self-representation of the **Qianlong emperor** (乾隆帝) as **Mañjuśrī-Cakravartin** (मञ्जुश्री-चक्रवर्तिन्), that is, as a world-ruling monarch aligned with **Mañjuśrī** (मञ्जुश्री; Tibetan: **’Jam dpal dbyangs**, འཇམ་དཔལ་དབྱངས་; Chinese: **文殊師利 / 文殊**) and Buddhist universal kingship. Rubin Museum materials on Qing visual culture make this explicit: Qianlong used Buddhist art and ritual representation to project himself as a divine bodhisattva ruler at the center of a Buddhist cosmos.

This did not amount to a Shambhala prophecy. The Qing were not claiming to be the hidden kingdom of future restoration in the Kālacakra sense. But they were operating in a neighboring conceptual field: one in which legitimate rule had to look like **protection of dharma**, **cosmic ordering power**, and **multiethnic sacral universality**. In that sense, the Manchu court possessed what may be called **eschatological sensitivity without millenarianism**. It understood that Buddhist expectations about righteous order mattered politically, and it therefore sought to embody fulfilled order in the present rather than deferred rescue in the future.

That distinction is analytically useful. **Shambhala-messianism** says: true order is hidden and will return. **Qing sacral monarchy** says: true order is already here, centered on the reigning emperor. Both operate with Buddhist symbolic capital, but they orient time differently. One is **restorative-future**; the other is **integrative-present**.

### 7.4 Shared symbolic field, different political forms

The main comparative conclusion is that **Qing**, **Zunghar**, and later **Shambhala-inflected** projects all competed over the same broad symbolic field, but with different political forms and different temporal logics. That field included at least four recurring elements: **Gelug patronage**, **transregional Buddhist legitimacy**, **rulership as protector of dharma**, and the possibility that crisis might require either renewed empire or future restoration.

A concise comparison helps:

| Dimension | Qing (清) | Zunghars (Зүүнгар / 準噶爾) | Shambhala politics (शम्भल / བདེ་འབྱུང་) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Political form | Durable multiethnic empire | Rival Inner Asian military polity | Mobile prophetic or para-imperial myth |
| Buddhist strategy | Institutional patronage, court ritual, imperial sacrality | Competitive intervention in Buddhist sphere | Crisis-legitimation through future rescue |
| Temporal orientation | Present order | Competitive present | Restorative future |
| Sovereign image | Emperor as protector / Mañjuśrī-Cakravartin | Regional Buddhist-capable rulership | Hidden king or northern savior |
| Main strength | Institutional depth | Strategic rivalry and steppe credibility | Symbolic elasticity |

The table should not be read too rigidly. In practice, these forms overlapped. Qing ideology could invoke future protection; Shambhala politics could be attached to actual states; Zunghar power could participate in Buddhist legitimation without a polished imperial theology. But the distinctions remain useful because they clarify why similar symbols produced different political outcomes.

The strongest historical claim that emerges from this comparison is that **Inner Asian empire was not merely territorial rule plus religion as ornament**. It was competition within a **shared sacred-political language**. The Qing eventually dominated because they were better able to translate that language into institutions, patronage networks, architecture, and imperial representation. The Zunghars remained potent because they were still intelligible within the same world. And Shambhala politics later became so portable because, once institutional order fractured, prophecy could travel more quickly than bureaucracy.

## 8. Pan-Mongol and Pan-Buddhist Political Uses of Shambhala

### 8.1 Russia as Shambhala (शम्भल / བདེ་འབྱུང་ / Шамбала)

One of the most consequential political reuses of **Shambhala** (Sanskrit: **Śambhala**, शम्भल; Tibetan: **Bde ’byung**, བདེ་འབྱུང་; Mongolian and Russian forms vary) was the attempt to map it onto the **Russian Empire** (Российская империя). The key figure here is **Agvan Dorzhiev** (Агван Доржиев / Agvan Lobsan Dorzhiev), the Buryat-Mongol monk closely associated with the **Thirteenth Dalai Lama** (ཏཱ་ལའི་བླ་མ་). Sources on Dorzhiev note that by the 1890s he had begun circulating the idea that **Russia was the mythical northern land of Shambhala**, that the **Tsar** (царь) might fulfill an old Buddhist messianic expectation, and that Russia’s position among Mongols and other Inner Asian Buddhists made it a plausible northern protector.

This move was not simply devotional speculation. It was strategic. Dorzhiev was trying to translate a precarious geopolitical situation—Tibet caught between British pressure, Qing claims, and Russian possibilities—into a language of **Buddhist sacral geography**. If Russia could be imagined as the **Northern Shambhala**, then alignment with Russia could appear not as secular opportunism but as a form of prophetic realism. In this sense, Shambhala became a tool of **diplomatic sacralization**: it clothed realpolitik in the vocabulary of messianic protection.

This also marks an important distinction between **Pan-Mongol** and **Pan-Buddhist** uses. The Russia-as-Shambhala idea was not purely ethnic. It did not depend on Mongol unity alone. Rather, it aimed at a broader Buddhist coalition that might include **Buryats**, **Kalmyks**, **Mongols**, Tibetans, and other communities linked by Tibetan Buddhism (ནང་བསྟན་ / 藏傳佛教). The ethnic and the religious overlapped, but they were not identical. Dorzhiev’s move works best if read as **Pan-Buddhist geopolitics with Pan-Mongol implications**.

### 8.2 Red Shambhala (Красная Шамбала) and revolutionary translation

If Dorzhiev tried to align Shambhala with imperial Russia, later revolutionary actors and interpreters pushed the symbol in a different direction: **Red Shambhala** (Красная Шамбала). Andrei Znamenski’s study of Inner Asian prophecy and geopolitics shows how Shambhala language could be drawn into early Soviet and Mongolian revolutionary settings as a way of translating violent political upheaval into a recognizable sacred narrative. In this frame, the coming force from the north was no longer simply a Buddhist protector under the Tsar. It could be re-coded as a revolutionary army or as a historical rupture that still wore the symbolic clothing of Buddhist prophecy.

That reuse matters because it demonstrates the extraordinary **semantic elasticity** of Shambhala. A symbol originally tied to the **Kālacakra** (कालचक्र / དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་) kingdom of righteous future restoration could now be attached to secular-revolutionary violence. This was not because communists had become Buddhist, but because Shambhala had become a **vernacular political medium** in Mongolian and Inner Asian settings. Revolutionary mobilization could borrow Buddhist end-time structure even when its ideological content was radically different.

The phrase “Red Shambhala” therefore captures more than a propaganda slogan. It names a structural process: **revolution translated into prophecy**. Once ordinary believers, monks, local elites, and revolutionary intermediaries all operated in a world where Shambhala signified northern rescue, future victory, and moral reversal, communist advance could be narrated as though it were the fulfillment of an already known sacred pattern. That did not erase contradictions between Buddhism and revolutionary atheism. It simply shows that political mobilization in Inner Asia often required passage through preexisting **religio-symbolic forms**.

### 8.3 Japan (日本 / にほん) and pan-Mongol counter-appropriation

The symbolic field did not belong to Russia or Soviet Mongolia alone. **Japan** (日本) and its associated actors also attempted to appropriate Shambhala for **Pan-Mongol** and anti-Soviet purposes. Study Buddhism’s overview of the Shambhala legend in Mongolian politics states directly that, after Japan’s expansion into Inner Mongolia, Japanese propaganda sought to win Mongol allegiance by claiming that **Japan was Shambhala** and by presenting Japanese power as the vehicle for a **pan-Mongol confederation under Japanese protection**. A related discussion notes the same point in the context of mistaken foreign myths: after the Japanese takeover of Inner Mongolia in 1937, Japan exploited the Shambhala legend for political gain and tried to persuade Mongols that Japan itself was the hidden northern savior.

This is analytically revealing because it shows how little the symbol depended on consistent doctrine once it entered geopolitical circulation. Russia could be Shambhala. Soviet revolution could be Red Shambhala. Japan could also be Shambhala. The constant was not the state making the claim. The constant was the need to appear as the **external force capable of restoring order, protecting Buddhism, and reorganizing Inner Asia**. In that sense, Shambhala became a kind of **floating messianic credential**.

This is also where **Pan-Mongolism** becomes sharper. Japanese uses of Shambhala were more overtly entangled with the dream of uniting Mongol lands or mobilizing Mongol groups across existing boundaries. Here the symbol did not simply authorize a Buddhist protector. It helped imagine a **transborder Mongol political space** under a power that claimed to honor Buddhist expectations. That differs slightly from Dorzhiev’s strategy, where the frame was more explicitly Tibetan-Buddhist and diplomatic. The Japanese appropriation leaned harder into **ethno-geopolitical consolidation**, even while still using Buddhist symbolic capital.

### 8.4 Prophecy as mobile political technology

The broader lesson is that Shambhala functioned in Inner Asia as a form of **mobile political technology**. It could attach itself to very different actors because it condensed several politically useful meanings at once: **northern salvation**, **future victory**, **protection of dharma**, and **restoration after disorder**. Znamenski’s work is especially useful here because it shows that Shambhala was not merely an elite literary trope; it had enough resonance among Mongolian and Inner Asian populations to be repeatedly redeployed by competing forces. Study Buddhism’s material on the “Shambhala Wars” and later Japanese exploitation points in the same direction: prophecy was a real medium of mobilization, not just decorative symbolism.

A concise comparison clarifies the main variants:

| Political use | Principal actor(s) | What “Shambhala” meant | Dominant orientation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Russia as Northern Shambhala | Agvan Dorzhiev, Tibetan-Russian networks | Northern Buddhist protector, imperial patron | Pan-Buddhist diplomatic legitimation |
| Red Shambhala | Soviet/Mongolian revolutionary translation | Prophetic frame for revolutionary rupture | Revolutionary-messianic mobilization |
| Japan as Shambhala | Japanese imperial and Inner Mongolian propagandists | Buddhist savior-power backing Mongol unity | Pan-Mongol counter-appropriation |

The table makes clear that the symbol’s power lay not in doctrinal stability but in **structural reusability**. Each project could claim continuity with Buddhist expectation while redirecting that expectation toward very different imperial or para-imperial ends.

This is why Shambhala should not be reduced either to pure religious fantasy or to cynical propaganda. It was neither. It was a **shared symbolic resource** whose meaning was contested precisely because it was politically alive. Pan-Mongol and Pan-Buddhist projects competed over it because it made large-scale alignment seem morally and cosmologically warranted. When institutions were weak or fractured, prophecy could do work that bureaucracy could not. That is the core reason Shambhala became so politically potent in the Inner Asian borderlands of the late imperial and interwar periods.

## 9. Popular, Devotional, and Vernacular Afterlives

### 9.1 Mass Kālacakra (कालचक्र / དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་ / 時輪) and lay religious publics

If earlier sections followed **Shambhala** (Śambhala, शम्भल / **Bde ’byung**, བདེ་འབྱུང་ / 香巴拉) through elite textuality and geopolitics, its most enduring afterlife among ordinary Buddhists appears in a different register: **mass ritual participation**. The most important vehicle here is the **Kālacakra empowerment/initiation** (अभिषेक / dbang, དབང་), which has long exceeded narrow monastic audiences. The Office of the Dalai Lama presents the Kālacakra as a major initiation cycle, while Namgyal Monastery notes that the **Seventh Dalai Lama** began bestowing Kālacakra initiations to **large communities of ordained and lay practitioners in Tibet** in the eighteenth century, and that later Dalai Lamas continued the practice on a broad scale.

That institutional history matters because it shows how Shambhala entered lay consciousness not mainly through reading the **Vimalaprabhā** (विमलप्रभा / དྲི་མེད་འོད་), but through **attendance, blessing, spectacle, vows, and ritual proximity**. In practice, many participants do not approach Kālacakra as philologists of the late Indian tantric archive. They approach it as an event of merit (**puṇya**, पुण्य / bsod nams, བསོད་ནམས་), protection, collective aspiration, and karmic connection to a sacred future. This is a crucial shift in religious sociology: the doctrinal kingdom becomes a **public ritual horizon**.

Modern mass initiations intensify that public dimension. Reports linked to the 2017 Bodh Gaya cycle describe extraordinarily large attendance, with one source calling it the largest Buddhist gathering since the 1950s and estimating **more than a quarter of a million** participants. Even allowing for some imprecision in public-event counting, the scale is not in doubt: Kālacakra has functioned in the modern period as one of the most important means by which ordinary Buddhists encounter Shambhala indirectly, as a ritual field of collective world-renewal rather than a narrowly esoteric doctrine.

### 9.2 Imagined pilgrimage, guidebooks, and visionary travel

A second vernacular afterlife is **imagined pilgrimage**. Shambhala is unlike **Bodh Gaya** (बोधगया / རྡོ་རྗེ་གདན་ / 菩提伽耶) or **Lhasa** (ལྷ་ས་ / 拉薩), because it is not ordinarily available as a stable empirical destination. Yet Tibetan religious culture has long possessed genres that make inaccessible or hidden places religiously traversable through text, oral instruction, and visionary imagination. BDRC’s overview of **lam yig / gnas yig** (ལམ་ཡིག་ / གནས་ཡིག་), Tibetan pilgrimage and travel-guide literature, emphasizes that such works could guide readers not only to ordinary sacred sites but also to **mystical destinations** and **hidden lands** (**sbas yul**, སྦས་ཡུལ་). The same overview specifically notes a **1775 guide to Shambhala** by the Sixth Panchen Lama, showing that Shambhala was not merely a doctrine to be believed but a place to be **narratively approached**.

This matters because pilgrimage in Tibetan Buddhism is not reducible to physical locomotion. A place may be religiously real even when accessed through **guide literature, visualization, oral lore, dream, and karmic eligibility**. In that setting, Shambhala belongs to a wider class of sacred geographies that are approached by moral and visionary orientation as much as by footsteps. The preservation of Shambhala guidebooks and related travel-writing strongly suggests that ordinary Buddhists could inhabit Shambhala as an **imaginative pilgrimage destination**, not merely as an abstract doctrinal reference.

This also helps explain the resilience of the myth. A sacred kingdom that cannot be straightforwardly verified or falsified by ordinary travel is well suited to vernacular religiosity. It can remain distant without becoming irrelevant. It can be approached through story, aspiration, and ritualized directionality. In that sense, Shambhala’s inaccessibility is not a weakness of the popular cultic imagination; it is one of its enabling conditions.

### 9.3 Vernacular prophecy in times of crisis

A third afterlife appears in **vernacular prophecy**, especially in Mongolian and Inner Asian settings, where Shambhala became a way of narrating upheaval, expectation, and moral reversal. Study Buddhism’s treatment of foreign myths around Shambhala notes that the legend was repeatedly adapted for military and political support, including identifications of **Russia**, **Mongolia**, and **Japan** with Shambhala. That observation is important not merely for elite political history, but for popular religion: such appropriations only work if the symbol already has enough vernacular credibility to mobilize lay audiences, monks, and local intermediaries.

In this register, Shambhala functions less as a stable map location than as a **prophetic north**, a reservoir of future intervention, or a sign that history has not been abandoned to corruption. This is especially plausible in societies where religious and political temporality remain deeply entangled. Under those conditions, war, invasion, reform, or foreign patronage can be read not just as political events but as movements within a sacred-historical drama. Shambhala then becomes a language for interpreting instability: a way to say that disorder is temporary, that hidden forces of righteousness remain in reserve, and that apparent defeat may yet reverse.

This vernacular dimension should not be overstated into a claim that all ordinary Buddhists thought constantly in apocalyptic terms. The evidence is too uneven for that. But it is strong enough to support a narrower conclusion: Shambhala had a sufficiently broad religious profile in Inner Asia to become a **usable idiom of popular expectation** in periods of crisis. That is one reason the symbol travelled so well between courtly, monastic, and political settings.

### 9.4 Shambhala as ethical horizon and world-peace symbol

A fourth and increasingly important afterlife is explicitly **ethical-symbolic**. In many modern Buddhist settings, Shambhala is not foregrounded as a kingdom to be reached or a battlefield to be awaited, but as a symbol of **peace**, **moral order**, and **inner transformation**. Study Buddhism’s “Shambhala: Myths and Reality” argues that, despite the many distortions and foreign myths surrounding the legend, Shambhala has remained a “helpful and useful” symbol in both Indian Buddhist and later contexts. The Dalai Lama’s presentation of Kālacakra likewise frames the initiation as part of a path oriented toward awakening and, in modern public discourse, often toward **world peace**.

This reading is partly hermeneutic domestication and partly genuine continuation. It is domestication because it deemphasizes the harder apocalyptic edges of the anti-*mleccha* (म्लेच्छ) and final-battle material. But it is also continuation because Kālacakra has always linked **outer**, **inner**, and **alternative** levels of meaning. Once that multilevel structure exists, Shambhala can be moralized into an **ethical horizon** without being wholly severed from its earlier religious framework. For many contemporary lay practitioners, this is likely the most accessible Shambhala: not a dynastic kingdom ruled by future **Kalki** (कल्कि) kings, but an image of enlightened society, human solidarity, and inner discipline projected into the world.

A concise schema helps distinguish these vernacular afterlives:

| Vernacular mode | Main medium | What Shambhala becomes | Social function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass initiation | Empowerment, blessing, spectacle | Public sacred horizon | Merit, participation, collective aspiration |
| Imagined pilgrimage | Guidebooks, oral lore, visionary travel | Hidden destination | Orientation, narrative approach, sacred directionality |
| Crisis prophecy | Local political-religious interpretation | Future rescuer / moral reversal | Hope under instability, mobilization, meaning-making |
| Ethical-symbolic reading | Modern teaching, popular exposition | Peaceful moral horizon | Accessibility, interiorization, universal relevance |

The table shows that Shambhala’s popular durability lies in **functional plurality**. It can bless, orient, warn, console, and universalize. That is why it survives not only in monasteries and scholarly texts, but also in broad lay religious publics.

The main analytical conclusion of this section is that among ordinary Buddhists, Shambhala lives less as a tightly delimited doctrine than as a **devotional horizon**. It is encountered through mass Kālacakra ritual, imagined through pilgrimage genres, activated in times of disorder, and softened into a symbol of peace and ethical renewal. This does not erase its more difficult textual history. It shows, rather, how sacred geography becomes durable when it can be inhabited in multiple registers at once.

## 10. Conclusion: From Tantric Kingdom to Floating Signifier

### 10.1 Historical transformations

The central argument of this review has been that **Shambhala** (Sanskrit: **Śambhala**, शम्भल; Tibetan: **Bde ’byung**, བདེ་འབྱུང་; Chinese: **香巴拉**) cannot be treated as one stable thing across time. In its earliest recoverable form, it belongs to the **Kālacakra** (Sanskrit: **Kālacakra**, कालचक्र; Tibetan: **Dus kyi ’khor lo**, དུས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་; Chinese: **時輪**) corpus, one of the latest Buddhist tantric traditions to emerge in North India. In that setting, Shambhala is a **sacred kingdom**, a **royal archive of tantra**, and a **future source of restoration**. The Kālacakra tradition’s own transmitted narrative, as summarized by the Dalai Lama’s teaching materials, places **Suchandra / Sucandra** (सुचन्द्र) in Shambhala as the first royal recipient and commentator of the tantra, while modern reference works situate the tradition historically in the late Indian tantric world of the 10th to early 11th century.

From there, however, Shambhala becomes historically mobile. Inside the Kālacakra itself, it already functions not only as sacred geography but also as a **polemical and eschatological reserve**. The hidden kingdom preserves truth against decline and anchors a future reversal of disorder. In later centuries, that same complex makes Shambhala newly available to other projects: anti-*mleccha* (म्लेच्छ) apocalyptic thought, Inner Asian contests over Buddhist sovereignty, Theosophical universalization, Roerichian civilizational mysticism, and modern lay devotional aspiration. The historical pattern is therefore not one of simple continuity, but of **layered refunctioning**. A specifically Buddhist tantric kingdom becomes, step by step, a much more elastic sign of hidden authority, righteous protection, and future renewal.

The full sequence can be stated schematically:

| Historical stage | Dominant frame | What Shambhala becomes |
|---|---|---|
| Late Indian Kālacakra formation | Tantric sacred geography | Royal Buddhist kingdom preserving the teaching |
| Kālacakra eschatology | Polemical-apocalyptic | Hidden reserve of future restoration |
| Inner Asian imperial and para-imperial politics | Symbolic legitimation | Resource for claims to Buddhist protection and sovereignty |
| Modern occult and literary reception | Universalized hidden center | Floating myth of concealed wisdom and authority |
| Vernacular and lay Buddhist afterlives | Devotional horizon | Ritual, ethical, and aspirational symbol |

The point of the table is not to imply clean discontinuity between stages. It is to show that later meanings accreted around an earlier core that remained historically specific.

### 10.2 Analytical payoff

The main analytical payoff is that Shambhala provides a strong case study in how **sacred geography becomes politically and culturally portable**. In the Kālacakra archive, sacred place, righteous kingship, tantric commentary, and cosmic time are tightly linked. This gives Shambhala unusual symbolic density from the start. It is not merely a hidden land. It is already a **compressed theory of preservation**: truth survives because it is housed, ruled, commented upon, and deferred into the future. That density helps explain why Shambhala later proved so reusable. Once loosened from its doctrinal frame, it could still carry a remarkable amount of meaning.

Seen this way, Shambhala’s modern afterlives are not random corruptions. They are secondary formations built on real structural features of the original symbol. The Kālacakra tradition had already linked **hiddenness**, **authority**, **future vindication**, and **sacred rulership**. Modern occultism, Inner Asian geopolitics, and lay devotional culture each seized on different parts of that package. Theosophical and Roerichian readings exaggerated hidden mastery and civilizational centrality. Inner Asian actors emphasized northern rescue and righteous sovereignty. Modern ritual publics often foreground blessing, peace, and moral aspiration. Each selection distorts something, but each also reveals something about why the symbol endured.

This article has therefore argued against two opposite simplifications. The first is the romantic claim that Shambhala is simply a timeless realm of peace, detachable from textual history and polemical context. The second is the reductionist claim that Shambhala is only propaganda, fantasy, or modern invention. Both fail because they ignore the layered historical life of the symbol. The better account is that Shambhala is a **late Buddhist tantric kingdom that later became a floating signifier**, but one whose later mobility remains partly intelligible because of the structure of the original Kālacakra construction.

### 10.3 Limits and future directions

Several limits remain. First, the intermediary **Tibetan scholastic** and **Mongolian textual** receptions of Shambhala require fuller treatment than this review has given them. The extant Kālacakra materials and modern reception histories are easier to narrate than the many centuries of commentarial domestication, ritual transmission, and regional reinterpretation in between. Second, the **vernacular** life of Shambhala is still imperfectly documented. The evidence for lay and ordinary Buddhist reception is often mediated through monasteries, official religious institutions, travel-guide genres, or modern teaching organizations. Yet those traces are enough to show that Shambhala remained active not only in elite discourse but also in mass ritual and pilgrimage imagination. BDRC’s discussion of Tibetan **lam yig / gnas yig** literature, including a Shambhala guide attributed to the Sixth Panchen Lama, is a reminder that hidden sacred geography could be religiously inhabited through narrative approach as well as direct travel.

Third, future work should sharpen the comparative horizon. Shambhala should be placed more systematically alongside other **hidden lands** and sacred geographies, including Tibetan **sbas yul** (སྦས་ཡུལ་, “hidden lands”), as well as compared more carefully with non-Buddhist or cross-cultural myths of concealed sovereignty. Such work would clarify what is genuinely distinctive about Shambhala and what belongs to wider Eurasian patterns of eschatological geography. Fourth, the political dimension remains open to expansion. The competition among **Qing** (清), **Zunghar** (準噶爾 / Зүүнгар), Russian, Mongolian, and Japanese actors for Buddhist symbolic legitimacy suggests that Shambhala should be studied not only as myth or doctrine, but as part of the broader history of **Inner Asian imperial imagination**.

The most defensible final formulation is therefore this: **Shambhala began as a late tantric sacred kingdom and became a historically mobile sign of hidden legitimacy, future order, and moral renewal.** It is precisely because it was never only a place that it could travel so far. But it is equally because it was once a very specific place in a very specific textual system that its later travels can still be reconstructed with some precision.
