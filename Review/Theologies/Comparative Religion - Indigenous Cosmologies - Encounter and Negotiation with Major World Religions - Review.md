## Abstract (150–200 words)

When Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam expanded beyond their points of origin, they did not enter empty religious landscapes. Every territory they reached was already populated with spirits, ancestors, local gods, and supernatural beings embedded in the fabric of daily life. This article examines how these three major traditions responded to indigenous cosmologies — absorbing, suppressing, displacing, or negotiating with local supernatural beings — and asks why their responses differed so dramatically across time and place. Drawing on religious history, anthropology, political theory, and the emerging field of cognitive science of religion, we argue that no single factor — not theology alone, not politics, not human agency — adequately explains the patterns observed. Instead, a multi-causal framework is required, one that takes seriously both the structural features of religious traditions and the universal cognitive tendencies that make spirit beliefs extraordinarily resistant to suppression. The article concludes by examining what cognitive science reveals about why indigenous cosmologies persist even after centuries of official condemnation, and what this persistence tells us about the deep relationship between human minds and the supernatural.

## Section 2: The Theological Toolkit — What Each Tradition Brought to the Encounter

*Purpose: Establish the intrinsic theological differences that predisposed each tradition toward different encounter strategies, without reducing outcomes to theology alone.*

### 2.1 Buddhism's Open Cosmos: Absorption by Default

- Explain *anattā*, *pratītyasamutpāda*, and the infinity of world-systems as the cosmological architecture that makes absorption structurally easy
- Introduce the conversion template: indigenous being encounters the Dharma, is subdued if hostile, takes refuge, becomes a protector (*dharmapāla*)
- Detailed examples: yakṣas in the *Āṭānāṭiya Sutta*; the Mucalinda nāga sheltering the newly awakened Buddha; Nāgārjuna retrieving sūtras from the nāga realm; wrathful deities (*khro bo*) in Vajrayāna absorbing rākṣasa-adjacent energies
- Key concept: *orthopraxy* — Buddhism's emphasis on correct action rather than correct belief gave it semiotic flexibility; an offering can be reframed without changing its observable form

### 2.2 Christianity's Contested Toolkit: Four Strategies in Tension

- Explain why strict monotheism and ontological dualism made absorption the *hardest* option — every indigenous being required a forced theological choice
- Present the four strategies as responses to a structural problem the theology couldn't cleanly solve:
	- **Demonization:** indigenous beings are real but diabolic (Paul's *daimonia*; Carolingian penitentials)
	- **Euhemerism:** indigenous beings are dead humans wrongly deified (Snorri's Eddic rationalization; Bede)
	- **Sanctoral displacement:** saints inherit the functions and sites of indigenous beings (Brigid, Guadalupe, Winifred)
	- **Baptism of cosmic structure:** intermediate beings are philosophically reframed (Pseudo-Dionysius; the neutral angels hypothesis for Irish fairy folk)
- Explain *orthodoxy* vs. *orthopraxy* distinction: Christianity's insistence on correct belief made semiotic flexibility theologically impossible in ways it was not for Buddhism

### 2.3 Islam's Middle Position: The Jinn Framework and Its Instability

- Introduce the Qur'ānic acknowledgment of jinn (Sūrat al-Jinn, 72; Sūrat al-Raḥmān, 55) as a pre-existing taxonomic slot capable of absorbing indigenous spirit beings
- Explain the crucial asymmetry: the jinn framework acknowledges indigenous beings as real but prohibits their propitiation (*shirk*) — ontological acknowledgment without ritual permission
- Introduce the *tawassul* / *istigātha* boundary and how differently it is drawn by Ṣūfī orders vs. Wahhābī/Salafi interpreters
- Establish the permanent instability this creates: every generation must renegotiate the boundary between licit and illicit engagement with spirit beings

## Section 3: The Political Determinant — Why Theology Alone Doesn't Explain the Outcomes

*Purpose: Demonstrate through historical case studies that political and institutional variables explain more variance than theological predisposition alone; introduce the natural experiment logic.*

### 3.1 Ireland and Saxony: A Natural Experiment in Christianization

- Explain the natural experiment logic: same tradition, same broad theological framework, same Northern European cultural context — but radically different political conditions producing radically different outcomes
- **Ireland:** No Roman administrative infrastructure; monastic ecclesiology embedded in kinship networks; the *filid* (poet-scholars) integrated into monasteries; gradual conversion over generations
	- Result: sanctoral displacement flourishes — Brigid's feast date, attributes, and sacred flame directly absorb the goddess Brigid's; the *dinnseanchas* tradition hagiographically rewrites the mythological landscape; mythological material (Ulster Cycle, Fenian Cycle) preserved in monastic scriptoria
- **Saxony:** Carolingian military conquest; the *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae* (785) imposing Christianity by law with death penalty for apostasy; newly appointed bishops with secular authority answering to Frankish crown; Saxon ritual specialists displaced rather than integrated
	- Result: demonization dominates — the *Indiculus superstitionum et paganiarum* lists condemned practices; the *Heliand* accommodates Germanic literary form but not cosmological content; Burchard of Worms's *Corrector* three centuries later documents the failure of suppression — folk practices persist in underground, fragmented forms
- Identify the master variables: coercive state apparatus; treatment of indigenous learned specialists; speed of conversion; episcopal vs. monastic ecclesiology

### 3.2 Islam's Political Spectrum: Ṣūfī Traders to Jihād States

- Show that the same theological framework produced opposite outcomes depending on political context
- **West African accommodation:** Trans-Saharan trade networks; Muslim merchant communities as *strangers* dependent on indigenous hospitality; Ṣūfī orders (Qādiriyya, Tijāniyya) embedding in local social structures through *zāwiya* welfare functions; bori spirits reclassified as jinn while maintaining Hausa names and ritual protocols
- **Sokoto reversal:** Usman dan Fodio's jihād (1804); bori explicitly targeted as *shirk*; suppression producing the same underground-persistence dynamic as Carolingian Saxony, with the additional effect of politicizing bori as a marker of resistance identity
- **Southeast Asian gradualism:** Maritime trade networks; Javanese *wali songo* remembered as spirit-world masters rather than suppressors; *Islam nusantara* as contemporary theological legitimization of accommodation; Muhammadiyah vs. Nahdlatul Ulama as live replication of the demonization/accommodation debate
- Synthesize: the political variable has *stronger predictive power* than the theological variable — reformist Islam produces outcomes more like Carolingian Christianity than like Ṣūfī Islam despite sharing the same scripture

## Section 4: Human Factors — Who Did the Expanding, and Who Carried the Old Beliefs

*Purpose: Introduce the human-level variables — missionary type, indigenous specialists, gender — that mediate between theological/political structures and on-the-ground outcomes.*

### 4.1 The Missionary Type: Incentive Structures and Accommodation Pressure

- Buddhist monks: structurally dependent on lay community support through daily alms rounds; *vinaya* community survival incentivizes accommodation; personal spiritual development is primary obligation rather than institutional doctrinal conformity
- Christian missionaries: variation across types generates variation in outcomes
	- Wandering charismatics (early period): dependent on hospitality, similar to Buddhist monks
	- Carolingian bishops: accountable to Frankish crown, incentivized to demonstrate orthodoxy
	- Jesuits: answerable to Rome, but with internal debate about accommodation (Ricci in China; the Chinese Rites Controversy and its condemnation by Clement XI in 1715)
	- Protestant home-society missionaries: reporting requirements to funding bodies with specific doctrinal commitments
- Muslim expansion agents: the *merchant* (commercial incentives toward tolerance), the Ṣūfī *shaykh* (charismatic authority dependent on local social embedding), the jihād warrior-state (coercive incentives toward suppression)
- Key insight: the social type of the primary expansion agent is a massively underweighted variable; incentive structures predict accommodation vs. suppression more reliably than stated theological positions

### 4.2 Who Carried the Old Beliefs: Gender, Marginality, and Functional Necessity

- Consistent cross-cultural pattern: women, hunters, blacksmiths, healers, and marginal social groups are disproportionate carriers of indigenous spirit practice
- Structural explanation: indigenous spirit complexes governed domains (fertility, childbirth, agricultural luck, occupational hazard, household illness) that official religious institutions consistently failed to adequately serve — particularly for women excluded from or underserved by male-dominated official religion
- Examples: bori's predominantly female leadership in Hausa society; zār's female-centered practice in Sudan and Ethiopia (Janice Boddy's *Wombs and Alien Spirits*); Irish *bean feasa* maintaining fairy-lore; Malay *bomoh* as socially marginal healing specialist
- The suppression paradox: demonization consistently failed to eliminate these practices precisely because it offered no functional substitute for what the spirit complex provided; the Irish analogy — driving practices underground rather than eliminating them — replicates across all three traditions
- Corollary: where expanding traditions brought efficacious practical resources (Buddhist medicine, Islamic literacy and legal mediation, Christian hospitals and schools), conversion was deeper and more durable than where they brought theological argument alone

## Section 5: Why Spirit Beliefs Never Die — The Cognitive Science of Religion

*Purpose: Introduce CSR as the explanatory framework for the universal persistence of indigenous spirit beliefs; cover Boyer and Whitehouse as the two primary theoretical pillars.*

### 5.1 Pascal Boyer: Why Spirit Beings Have the Shape They Do

- Introduce CSR's foundational wager: religious beliefs are natural outputs of ordinary cognitive mechanisms, not sui generis phenomena requiring special explanation
- Explain the relevant cognitive systems:
	- **Agent detection system:** hair-trigger mechanism tuned to over-detect agents; spontaneously populates ambiguous stimuli with intentional beings
	- **Intuitive ontology:** default inference systems organized around categories (PERSON, ANIMAL, ARTIFACT, NATURAL OBJECT), each generating automatic predictions about behavior
	- **Theory of mind (ToM):** automatically models the intentions, emotions, and knowledge states of detected agents, firing even toward non-sentient objects
- **The Minimally Counterintuitive (MCI) agent concept:** religious beings violate a *small number* of default inferences from one ontological category while preserving the rest
	- Examples: ancestors who know your thoughts (PERSON category; violation: access to hidden mental states; preserved: intentions, moral concern, social relationship); ghosts that pass through walls but remember insults (violation: physical permeability; preserved: memory, emotional responses); sacred mountains that punish trespassers (NATURAL OBJECT with agentive violation)
	- The MCI memory advantage: experimental evidence that minimally counterintuitive items are recalled better than either fully intuitive or maximally counterintuitive ones; optimal violations approximately two to three per concept
- **Why suppression consistently fails:** indigenous spirit beings are MCI-formatted and generated by universal cognitive systems operating below deliberate reasoning; every child born into a culture where these beings circulate will spontaneously regenerate belief regardless of official theology
- **Why displacement outperforms demonization:** demonization reclassifies an MCI being without eliminating the cognitive machinery generating it; displacement redirects that machinery toward a new but equally MCI-formatted being (St. Brigid) who can do equivalent functional work

### 5.2 Harvey Whitehouse: How Ritual Form Shapes Community and Resistance

- Introduce the foundational puzzle: two Papua New Guinean groups from the same cultural background practicing radically different ritual forms and producing radically different social outcomes
- **The Doctrinal Mode:**
	- High frequency, low arousal, repetitive, standardized; exploits *semantic memory* (general knowledge stored independently of specific episodes)
	- Social consequence: large-scale anonymous communities unified by shared doctrinal *content* — you can be in communion with a co-religionist you have never met
	- Cognitive consequence: pressure toward systematization, codification, specialist clergy; orthodoxy and heresy become meaningful distinctions
	- Examples: Catholic Mass, Islamic salāt, Buddhist communal chanting, Pomio Kivung regular offering cycles
- **The Imagistic Mode:**
	- Low frequency, high arousal, physically intense, often involving ordeal or unusual sensory experience; exploits *episodic memory* (vivid autobiographical events with strong emotional signature)
	- Social consequence: small, intensely cohesive groups unified by shared *experience* — the bond is specific to those who were *there*
	- Cognitive consequence: *spontaneous exegetical reflection* (SER) — the irresistible post-initiation search for meaning in an overwhelming experience that resists verbal articulation
	- Examples: male initiation rites in Papua New Guinea and sub-Saharan Africa; Greco-Roman mystery cult initiations; intense Sufi *dhikr*; the ritual intensification Whitehouse witnessed directly
- **Why imagistic complexes resist suppression most strongly:** bori, zār, and spirit-possession ceremonies are paradigmatic imagistic rituals — their community bonds are constituted by shared episodic memory of overwhelming experiences, not by doctrinal propositions; you cannot replace an imagistic community bond with theological argument
- **The scaling prediction:** doctrinal religion can scale to millions of anonymous co-believers; imagistic religion structurally cannot — which explains why world religions are primarily doctrinal in their institutional form, with imagistic practices surviving as intense small communities nested within the larger structure

## Section 6: Big Gods and the Scaling of Cooperation — Why World Religions Won

*Purpose: Introduce Norenzayan's Big Gods argument as the macro-historical synthesis; explain why world religions with moralizing deities had a genuine functional advantage over indigenous traditions at scales of social complexity; connect to secularization.*

### 6.1 The Cooperation Problem and the Big God Solution

- State the puzzle: how did humans whose evolved social psychology was calibrated for groups of ~150 individuals build cooperative societies of millions of strangers?
- Standard mechanisms (kin selection, reciprocal altruism, direct reputation tracking) work for small groups but don't scale — you cannot track the reputation of every individual in a city of a million strangers
- **The Big God hypothesis:** cognitively compelling moralizing supernatural agents — gods who are powerful, omniscient with respect to human behavior, and who care about and enforce human moral norms — provided a cultural technology for extending cooperative monitoring beyond the direct-observation horizon
- Define the key features distinguishing Big Gods from parochial gods: broad situational awareness (the Full Access Agent property extended to cosmic scope); moral concern specifically about inter-human prosocial behavior; capacity and will to punish and reward; universal scope extending to strangers
- **The watching eyes effect:** behavioral economics evidence that even minimal observer cues (stylized eye images) significantly increase prosocial behavior; mechanism is automatic reputation-management cognition operating below deliberate reasoning; a compelling belief in an omniscient moral agent who is *always watching* should produce this effect continuously across all social situations
- Experimental evidence: Norenzayan and Shariff's (2008) religious priming and dictator game results; cross-cultural economic games showing world religion participation predicting more generous offers to strangers than local religious traditions

### 6.2 Big Gods, Parochial Gods, and the Logic of Religious Competition

- **Why parochial gods are not inferior — they are differently calibrated:** the nāga is an effective cooperative technology for the riparian community it serves; it fails as a cooperative technology for an expanding empire coordinating strangers across multiple ecological zones
- The competitive advantage of world religions in contexts of political and economic scaling was not only military or commercial but *functional* — Big God religions were better tools for the cooperative problems that expanding political economies posed
- **Religious prosociality is in-group prosociality:** critical qualification — Big God religions promoted cooperation within the moral community defined by the religion while remaining compatible with exploitation of and violence toward out-groups; the Crusader and the saint share a cognitive system; this explains both the cooperative achievements and the violence of world religion expansion
- **Principle 8 — Big Gods then Big Brother:** once Big God religions had bootstrapped large-scale cooperative institutions (legal systems, impersonal markets, bureaucratic states, formal education), those institutions could substitute for divine monitoring as the operative cooperative technology; secular justice system primes produce equivalent prosocial effects to religious primes in experimental conditions
- **The secularization implication:** secularization is not a universal modernization process but a conditional one dependent on prior development of effective secular cooperative institutions; the Scandinavian case (highly secular yet highly cooperative) as exhibit A; the prediction that societies where Big God beliefs are still doing significant cooperative work will resist secularization regardless of economic development

## Section 7: Synthesis and Conclusion — A Multi-Causal Model

*Purpose: Draw the threads together into a coherent analytical framework; clarify what the comparative analysis reveals; indicate directions for future research.*

### 7.1 Toward a Weighted Multi-Causal Model

- Summarize the explanatory factors and their relative predictive power:
	- **Strongest predictors:** state coercive apparatus (present/absent) + cosmological carrying capacity together explain the majority of variance in dominant encounter strategy
	- **Strong secondary predictors:** missionary type and incentive structure; treatment of indigenous intellectual class; speed of expansion
	- **Significant modifiers:** gender division of religious labor; competitive religious market conditions; geographical embeddedness of indigenous cosmology; functional necessity of spirit complex for survival domains
	- **Structural constants:** cognitive naturalness of MCI spirit-agent beliefs (universal, limits the success of all suppression strategies regardless of other factors); epistemological gap between efficacy and correctness criteria (universal, explains the persistence of folk practice under all three traditions)
- The most important interaction effect: coercive state + low cosmological carrying capacity (Carolingian Christianity, Sokoto Islam) produces the most aggressive and least successful suppression; no coercive state + high carrying capacity (Buddhist Southeast Asia, Ṣūfī West Africa) produces the most thorough absorption; no coercive state + intermediate carrying capacity (early Islamic trade expansion) produces the unstable accommodation pattern characteristic of Islamic frontier religion
- The cognitive baseline as a limiting condition on all strategies: you cannot suppress what the human mind spontaneously generates

### 7.2 What Persists, What Transforms, and What This Tells Us

- The persistence question reframed: indigenous spirit beliefs did not survive because expanding religions were insufficiently powerful or persuasive; they survived because they are outputs of universal cognitive machinery and because they continued doing necessary functional work that official traditions consistently failed to replace
- The transformation dynamic: what changes under religious encounter is not the *existence* of spirit beings but their *classification* — the domovoi becomes a demon, the bori spirit becomes a jinn, the goddess Brigid becomes a saint — while the cognitive machinery generating belief in them, and the functional work they perform, remains largely continuous
- The ecological dimension: indigenous spirit complexes frequently encoded genuine ecological knowledge (taboos on overfishing, prohibitions on deforestation of watershed areas, seasonal agricultural rhythms) with spirit beings as enforcement mechanisms; where expanding traditions preserved spirit complexes through absorption or displacement, they inadvertently preserved ecological management functions; where they suppressed spirit complexes through demonization, they sometimes contributed to environmental degradation
- **Closing reflection:** the encounter between world religions and indigenous cosmologies was never a simple story of the new replacing the old. It was a negotiation — often unequal, frequently coerced, always incomplete — between different ways of inhabiting a world populated with beings that human minds will continue generating regardless of what any theology officially permits. The spirits did not lose. They changed shape.

## Section 1: Introduction — The Problem of the Full Religious Landscape

## 1.1 No Empty Religious Territories

In 637 CE, Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang passed through the kingdoms of Central Asia on his return from India, documenting temples where images of the Buddha shared sacred space with local deity figures whose names he could barely transliterate. A Franciscan friar arriving in the Valley of Mexico in 1524 found that the communities he was charged with converting already maintained elaborate relationships with beings who governed rain, maize, death, and the cardinal directions — beings with their own ritual calendars, their own priesthoods, their own sacred geographies. A Ṣūfī trader settling in 13th-century Mali brought his prayer beads and his Arabic into a landscape already thick with spirits inhabiting specific rivers, termite mounds, and iron-smelting sites, spirits whose goodwill the farmers and hunters around him considered as practically necessary as water.

None of these missionaries, merchants, or monks entered an empty religious landscape. This is the starting observation of the present article, and it is more consequential than it first appears. The standard narrative of religious expansion — Buddhism spreading peacefully eastward, Christianity carrying the Gospel to the nations, Islam following the trade routes outward from Arabia — tends to foreground the expanding tradition and background everything it encountered. But what was encountered had its own internal logic, its own cosmological architecture, its own explanation of why children fell sick and crops failed and rivers flooded. The beings populating these indigenous cosmologies were not waiting to be replaced. They were working.

What happened when the expanding traditions met them is the subject of this article. The outcomes were neither uniform nor predictable from theology alone. Buddhism absorbed the nāga — the serpentine water deity central to South and Southeast Asian religious life — so thoroughly that nāga figures became architectural elements of Buddhist temples, their hoods sheltering images of the Buddha himself. Christianity, encountering the goddess Brigid in Ireland, produced a saint so perfectly calibrated to the goddess's attributes — same feast date, same patronage of poetry and smithcraft and healing, same sacred flame at Kildare — that scholars continue to debate where the goddess ends and the saint begins. Islam, arriving in West Africa through Ṣūfī trade networks, found the *bori* spirit-possession complex — a tradition in which named spirits mounted human hosts and governed domains from hunting to childbirth — and produced not suppression but a centuries-long negotiation in which the spirits were reclassified as jinn, given Islamic names in some accounts, and continued their work largely undisturbed. Yet the same Islamic tradition, arriving in Saxony under Carolingian military force, would have looked unrecognizable to those West African Ṣūfī networks: here, indigenous spirit beings were condemned as demons, their shrines destroyed, their practitioners threatened with death under the *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae* of 785 CE.

The same traditions, radically different outcomes. Explaining this variance is the central problem this article addresses.

## 1.2 Why This Question Matters

The encounter between world religions and indigenous cosmologies is not a closed historical chapter. In contemporary Latin America, debates about the theological status of *Pachamama* — the Andean earth mother whose offerings are made before planting in communities that also celebrate Mass — generated a public controversy within the Catholic Church as recently as 2019, when indigenous figures appeared in Vatican ceremonies during the Amazon Synod and prompted accusations of idolatry from conservative quarters. In Indonesia, Nahdlatul Ulama — the world's largest Islamic organization, with approximately ninety million members — has developed a formal theology of *Islam nusantara* (Archipelago Islam) that explicitly legitimizes the incorporation of local Javanese, Sundanese, and Balinese cultural practice within Islamic observance, while Salafi reform movements operating through social media contest this accommodation in real time. In sub-Saharan Africa, Pentecostal Christianity has revived aggressive demonization of indigenous spirit beings with a technological reach — YouTube channels, WhatsApp networks, radio broadcast — that the Carolingian bishops could never have imagined, and with similarly contested results.

These are live arguments, not museum exhibits. Understanding their historical depth and their structural logic requires a framework capable of holding together several levels of analysis simultaneously: the theological architectures that predisposed each tradition toward particular strategies; the political and institutional conditions that determined which theological tendency was actualized in any given context; the human agents — missionaries, merchants, monks, indigenous specialists, women, healers — whose incentives and social positions shaped outcomes on the ground; and, underlying all of these, the universal features of human cognition that made indigenous spirit beliefs extraordinarily resistant to suppression regardless of what strategy was deployed against them.

This article develops exactly that framework. It proceeds by examining each level of analysis in turn — theological, political, human, cognitive — before assembling them into a weighted multi-causal model in the conclusion. The three primary traditions under examination are Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam. The regional cases drawn upon include Ireland and Saxony as a natural experiment in Christian Christianization strategy; West Africa and Southeast Asia as the primary Islamic cases; and mainland Southeast Asia as the primary Buddhist case, with additional examples drawn from Tibet, Japan, and the Americas throughout. The argument, stated plainly, is this: the spirits survived because the human mind generates them spontaneously, because they performed necessary work that official traditions consistently failed to replace, and because the political conditions required to suppress them were far more demanding than any expanding tradition could sustain indefinitely. What changed, across centuries of encounter, was not whether the spirits existed in the minds and practices of local communities, but what names they were given and whose authority they were said to serve.

## Section 2: The Theological Toolkit — What Each Tradition Brought to the Encounter

## 2.1 Buddhism's Open Cosmos: Absorption by Default

The Buddhist missionary's relative ease in accommodating indigenous spirit beings was not a matter of temperament or strategic calculation. It was a structural consequence of the cosmological architecture Buddhism carried with it. The core doctrinal commitments — *anattā* (the absence of fixed, permanent selfhood), *pratītyasamutpāda* (the dependent origination of all phenomena), and the postulation of infinite world-systems across vast stretches of time — generated a cosmos that was, by design, already populated with beings of every conceivable type, all of them somewhere on the path through *saṃsāra*, all of them in principle reachable by the Dharma. Adding a yakṣa or a nāga to this system cost nothing theologically. The question was never whether such beings existed but only where in the cosmological order they sat and what their relationship to the Buddha's teaching might be.

The practical mechanism this generated was a conversion narrative so flexible it could be applied to virtually any local entity. In the *Āṭānāṭiya Sutta* of the Pāli canon, Vaiśravaṇa — king of the yakṣas, beings associated in pre-Buddhist Indian tradition with both fertility and unpredictable violence — formally presents the Buddha with a protective formula designed to shield monks meditating in the wilderness from yakṣas who had not yet taken refuge in the Dharma. The structure of the passage is revealing: some yakṣas are already Buddhist devotees; others remain dangerous precisely because they have not converted. The dangerous ones are not demons to be exorcised but beings to be reached. The indigenous spirit survives, retains its power and its name, and is repositioned as either a protector of the Dharma or a being whose future conversion remains open.

The nāga tradition illustrates how thoroughly this repositioning could proceed. Serpentine water divinities governing rivers, rainfall, and subterranean wealth, nāgas were central to religious life across South and Southeast Asia long before Buddhism arrived. Rather than displacing them, Buddhist narrative made them foundational. In the *Udāna*, the nāga king Mucalinda shelters the newly awakened Buddha beneath his expanded hood during a storm — the indigenous water deity becomes the first post-awakening protector, his body literally forming the canopy over the Dharma's moment of origin. In Mahāyāna tradition, nāgas become custodians of scripture: Nāgārjuna, the 2nd-century philosopher whose work transformed Buddhist thought, is said to have retrieved the *Prajñāpāramitā* sūtras from the nāga realm beneath the ocean. The beings who might have competed with Buddhist authority became instead its librarians. Across mainland Southeast Asia today, the nāga survives as the carved balustrade of every temple staircase — architecturally enclosing the sacred precinct, domesticated but visually sovereign.

Underlying this absorption capacity was Buddhism's strongly *orthopractical* orientation — its emphasis on correct action rather than correct belief as the primary religious obligation. A ritual offering generates merit through its performance; the precise theological framing the practitioner carries matters less than the act itself. This gave Buddhism semiotic flexibility that proved invaluable at every cultural frontier it crossed: the action of making offerings could be reframed as Buddhist practice without requiring the practitioner to abandon the gesture, the location, or even entirely the being being addressed. The temple was built on the sacred hill; the spirit of the hill became the temple's guardian. The continuity was functional, visible, and theologically untroubling.

## 2.2 Christianity's Contested Toolkit: Four Strategies in Tension

Christianity arrived at every religious frontier carrying a structural problem that Buddhism did not have. Strict monotheism — the absolute ontological distinction between the one God and everything else — meant that every indigenous supernatural being required a forced theological choice. What *was* it, exactly? The answer could not be "a being in the cosmos, somewhere on a path toward liberation," because Christian cosmology provided no such path and no such intermediate address. The being was either an angel (an extremely rare and doctrinally constrained application), a demon (the theologically obvious default, given that non-Christian supernatural power was most naturally attributed to fallen spiritual entities), a euhemerized human (a dead person wrongly elevated to divine status by confused communities), or the functional precursor of a saint (a position that required ignoring, officially, what was actually happening).

That none of these solutions was fully satisfying explains why Christianity produced not one response to indigenous cosmologies but four, operating simultaneously in the same times and places, often in unacknowledged tension with each other.

Demonization was the most theologically aggressive and institutionally prominent response. It had apostolic warrant — Paul's assertion in his first letter to the Corinthians that the nations sacrifice to *daimonia* rather than to God — and patristic elaboration in Tertullian, Justin Martyr, and others who argued that pagan gods were real beings, but malevolent ones. In practice this meant that the Germanic *álfar*, the Slavic *domovoi*, the Baltic *laumes*, and their counterparts across Europe were reclassified as diabolic entities. The Carolingian penitentials prescribed penances for offerings made at trees, springs, and crossroads — not because those places were considered inert but because something was understood to be there, and that something was dangerous. The strategy's fatal weakness was that it offered no functional substitute. It did not make people stop propitiating the *domovoi*; it made them feel guilty about it, or drove the practice underground, where it survived for centuries in exactly the fragmented, undocumented forms that Burchard of Worms was still cataloguing three hundred years after Charlemagne's forced conversion of Saxony.

Euhemerism — the rationalization of mythological beings as deified humans — provided an intellectually respectable alternative available to literate elites who needed to preserve mythological material without endorsing its supernatural claims. Snorri Sturluson's *Prose Edda*, composed around 1220, reads the Norse gods as chieftains from Troy who migrated north and were posthumously deified by credulous Scandinavians. The strategy rendered mythological beings historically real but supernaturally inert — ancestors rather than demons — which made their stories recordable and their traditions preservable. It is substantially why the Eddic material survives at all.

Sanctoral displacement operated below the level of official theology, generated largely by folk communities rather than ecclesiastical decree, and was for precisely this reason the most widespread and durable of the four strategies. The saint inherited the function, the feast date, the sacred topography, and frequently the iconographic attributes of the indigenous being, while hagiographic narrative supplied theological cover. The convergence between St. Brigid of Kildare and the goddess Brigid of Irish tradition is precise across too many independent dimensions — February 1st feast date coinciding with Imbolc, patronage of smithcraft and poetry and healing, the perpetual sacred flame maintained by female religious, the association with dairy fertility — to be plausibly coincidental. No Irish ecclesiastical text acknowledges the substitution. The mechanism was emergent negotiation between monastic communities and their surrounding populations, proceeding without authorization because authorization would have required admitting what was happening.

The philosophical baptism of intermediate being categories — most elaborately in Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite's nine-order angelic hierarchy, which mapped directly onto Neoplatonic frameworks for spiritual intermediaries — provided scholastic theology with resources for acknowledging the existence of non-Christian spirit entities without sanctioning their worship. In Irish theological speculation, the *Aos Sí* were sometimes theorized as neutral angels, beings who had taken neither God's side nor Lucifer's in the primordial rebellion and who inhabited the liminal spaces — fairy mounds, twilight hours, threshold calendar days — that their ontological ambiguity demanded. The solution was philosophically creative and pastorally limited: it circulated in learned manuscript culture while the fairy mounds continued receiving their offerings regardless.

## 2.3 Islam's Middle Position: The Jinn Framework and Its Instability

Islam entered its encounters with indigenous spirit cosmologies carrying a resource that Christianity lacked and Buddhism did not need: a Qur'ānically authorized taxonomic slot for non-human spirit beings. The seventy-second sūra narrates a group of jinn who overhear Qur'ānic recitation and convert, returning to their community as Muslim missionaries. The fifty-fifth sūra addresses itself jointly to humans and jinn. Solomon's command over jinn appears in the *Sīra* literature as a divine gift. Non-human spirit beings capable of receiving and responding to revelation were not a theological problem Islam had to solve at its frontiers — they were already acknowledged in scripture.

This gave Islamic expansion a genuine absorptive capacity. Indigenous spirit beings encountered in West Africa, Southeast Asia, Central Asia, and the Persian world could be reclassified as jinn — Muslim or otherwise — without requiring their elimination from the cosmological landscape. The Hausa *iskoki*, the Malay *orang bunian*, the Persian *peri* all found addresses within the jinn taxonomy, retaining their names, their personality traits, and their functional domains while acquiring an Islamic cosmological identity.

The absorptive capacity came, however, with a structural constraint that has no parallel in Buddhism. Jinn are real, but their propitiation is *shirk* — the association of partners with God, among the gravest sins in Islamic theology. The ontological acknowledgment and the ritual prohibition pull in precisely opposite directions, creating what might be called a permanently unstable equilibrium: the beings are acknowledged to exist but condemned to a cosmological limbo where they cannot be legitimately engaged through the ritual means that make engagement practically meaningful. Every generation of Islamic communities living alongside indigenous spirit traditions must renegotiate this boundary, and where that boundary is drawn depends less on scripture — which is fixed — than on which Islamic interpretive tradition holds local authority. The Ṣūfī orders, whose own cosmological vocabulary already included hierarchies of spiritual beings and whose shrine culture provided functionally licit sites for petitionary practice, drew the boundary permissively. Wahhābī and Salafi interpreters drew it with maximal strictness. The same jinn framework, read through different jurisprudential lenses, produced outcomes ranging from quiet accommodation to violent suppression — a range almost as wide as Christianity's four strategies, generated from a single theological instrument.

## Section 3: The Political Determinant — Why Theology Alone Doesn't Explain the Outcomes

## 3.1 Ireland and Saxony: A Natural Experiment in Christianization

In the history of religious expansion, controlled comparisons are rare. Religious traditions spread across different cultures, different centuries, different ecological and economic contexts, making it difficult to isolate which variables are doing the explanatory work. The Christianization of Ireland and Saxony offers an unusual opportunity: two Northern European cultures, both converted within the same broad theological framework, both leaving substantial documentary records, but varying sharply on precisely the political and institutional dimensions that most plausibly drove strategic differences. The comparison does not deliver the clean results of a laboratory experiment, but it comes closer than most historical cases to one.

Christianity arrived in Ireland in the fifth century — Palladius sent by Rome in 431, Patrick active in the decades following — into a political landscape of fragmented petty kingdoms (*tuatha*) with no Roman administrative infrastructure, no urban episcopal network of the Mediterranean type, and a learned class whose social prestige was inseparable from the mythological material that an aggressive Christianity might otherwise have condemned. The Irish *filid* — poet-scholars whose functions encompassed genealogy, law, political satire, and praise-poetry — were not peripheral figures. A king's claim to territory was partly validated by *dinnseanchas*, the place-lore tradition that tied his lineage to the mythological history of the landscape itself. To attack the Tuatha Dé Danann or the *sídhe* with the theological force applied in Carolingian Europe would have been to attack the foundations of aristocratic legitimacy. The Irish Church could not afford this, and — crucially — no external power was compelling it to try.

The institutional structure of Irish Christianity reinforced this constraint. Ireland developed Christianity through monasteries rather than dioceses, with abbots rather than bishops holding real authority, and those abbots answering primarily to their founding family networks rather than to any centralizing hierarchy. The result was a Church embedded in the kinship structures of Irish society, without the institutional reach or the political backing to enforce doctrinal uniformity across the landscape. Local practice varied enormously; accommodation proceeded at the community level without requiring or receiving central authorization.

What this produced is visible in the documentary record with unusual clarity. The *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, the Ulster Cycle, the Fenian Cycle — mythological material that a more institutionally aggressive Church would have suppressed — were preserved in monastic scriptoria by monks who were frequently the descendants of the filid they had nominally displaced. The *dinnseanchas* tradition continued, hagiographically rewritten: wells sacred to goddess-figures became wells of saints, hills associated with the *sídhe* acquired patron saints whose feast days coincided with the old quarter-days, and the pilgrimage mountain Croagh Patrick layered Patrick's forty-day fast onto a site whose pre-Christian sacred significance the surrounding landscape made unmistakable. The convergence between St. Brigid and the goddess Brigid — analyzed in the preceding section as a theological strategy — is more fully understood as the emergent outcome of a political situation in which no institution possessed the authority or the incentive to prevent it.

The Saxon case could not differ more sharply in its structural conditions. The Christianization of the Saxons was not mission work in any conventional sense but a protracted military subjugation carried out by Charlemagne between 772 and 804, punctuated by the Massacre of Verden in 782, in which thousands of Saxon captives were executed for apostasy and rebellion. The *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae* of 785 imposed Christianity by force of law, prescribing the death penalty for refusing baptism, continuing pagan sacrifice, and cremating the dead in pagan fashion. Newly founded bishoprics at Paderborn, Bremen, and Münster arrived with Carolingian administrative infrastructure behind them — counts, appointed clergy, secular authority — and with Frankish overlords to whom demonstrations of doctrinal orthodoxy were institutionally necessary.

The documentary record of this context is equally revealing, but in an inverse direction. The *Indiculus superstitionum et paganiarum*, compiled for Carolingian missionaries around 743, lists condemned practices with a specificity that itself constitutes inadvertent ethnographic documentation: offerings to the dead, observances at sacred groves (*nimidas*), carved wooden figures, invocations at springs and crossroads. The *Heliand*, the remarkable Old Saxon gospel poem of around 830, shows the accommodation that was available even within this coercive framework — but it is accommodation of literary form and social idiom, not cosmological content. Christ becomes a Germanic lord, the apostles his sworn *comitatus*, but the indigenous spirit beings, the fate-goddesses, the sacred groves do not enter the narrative. The Carolingian church could domesticate Saxon aesthetics; it could not domesticate Saxon cosmology without abandoning the theological positions its political patrons required it to defend.

The long-term failure of the Carolingian strategy is documented in one of medieval Europe's most unexpectedly rich sources. Burchard of Worms, composing his penitential handbook *Corrector sive Medicus* around 1010 — three centuries after the legal imposition of Christianity in Saxony — describes folk practices still current in the German-speaking world with a specificity that can only reflect firsthand knowledge: women who believe they ride at night with Diana, offerings made at trees and springs, consultation of diviners, cattle-protection rituals invoking beings other than Christian saints. The practices Burchard catalogues are not the coherent system of pre-Christian Saxon religion — that system's specialists were displaced, its institutional forms destroyed. What survives is fragmented, decontextualized, and persistent precisely because it was never integrated into a framework that could have genuinely transformed it.

The comparison isolates several variables whose effects can be tentatively distinguished. The presence of coercive state apparatus made suppression institutionally available and accommodation institutionally dangerous — Irish Christianity had neither condition. The treatment of indigenous learned specialists determined whether the cosmological knowledge embedded in those specialists survived in legible form or fragmented into undocumented folk practice — the filid were integrated, the Saxon priests displaced. The speed of conversion precluded the generational community-level processes through which displacement operates — the Irish conversion had no legal start date, the Saxon conversion did. Taken together, these variables explain more of the variance in outcome than the shared theological tradition that both cases nominally inhabited.

## 3.2 Islam's Political Spectrum: From Ṣūfī Traders to Jihād States

The same analytical logic applies within a single tradition with even more force, because the theological variable is here held perfectly constant. The same scripture, the same five pillars, the same jinn framework — and outcomes ranging from quiet absorption to violent suppression, tracking not theological variation but political condition with uncomfortable precision.

Islam entered West Africa primarily through trans-Saharan trade networks, which determined the social type of its initial expansion agent: the merchant, living as a stranger in non-Muslim communities, dependent on local hospitality and commercial relationship, with powerful incentives to avoid alienating potential partners through aggressive religious condemnation. These merchant communities maintained their practice as a marker of identity and commercial trustworthiness without imposing it on surrounding populations. The Ṣūfī orders that followed — the Qādiriyya, the Tijāniyya — deepened this pattern. The *shaykh* whose authority depended on spiritual charisma and whose *zāwiya* served functions of education, legal mediation, and healing had structural incentives almost identical to those of the Irish monastic abbot: his institutional survival required embedding in local social networks, which meant accommodating the cosmological frameworks those networks inhabited.

The bori spirit-possession complex of Hausa culture was the primary test case. The *iskoki* — named spirits governing domains from iron-working to sexuality to illness — were gradually reclassified as jinn in the Islamic framework, retaining their Hausa names, their specific personalities, and the ritual protocols for engaging them, while acquiring a nominally Islamic cosmological address. The learned Islamic establishment in Hausa cities maintained formal disapproval while the practice continued openly. This was not hypocrisy but equilibrium — the functional work the spirits did was not being done by any Islamic institution, and the political conditions required to enforce suppression did not exist.

Usman dan Fodio's jihād of 1804 changed this equilibrium in ways that precisely replicate the Carolingian dynamic. Reform ideology, military conquest, and state formation combined to produce both systematic suppression of bori as shirk and a consequence that suppression consistently generates and consistently fails to anticipate: bori became a site of resistance. Its female leadership and non-elite social base positioned it explicitly against the new Sokoto scholarly establishment. The practice that had been ambient folk custom became a self-conscious identity marker, more resilient after suppression than before it, carrying the additional charge of political opposition that accommodation would never have generated.

Southeast Asia extended this pattern across a different ecological and cultural context while reproducing its structural logic. The *wali songo* — the nine saints traditionally credited with Islamizing Java — are remembered in popular tradition not as suppressors of indigenous practice but as masters of it, converting Javanese not by condemning the spirit world but by demonstrating superior command over it. Whether historically accurate or not, this narrative reveals the accommodation logic that Javanese Islam internalized about its own origins, and it produced an institutional culture — expressed formally in Nahdlatul Ulama's *Islam nusantara* theology — that treats local cosmological accommodation not as a deviation from Islamic orthodoxy but as one of its legitimate expressions. The Muhammadiyah movement's simultaneous push toward purification and condemnation of Javanese *kejawen* practices replicates, within Indonesian Islam, the Ṣūfī/reformist tension that the West African cases made visible. The argument is live, the outcome unresolved, and the political variable — which interpretive tradition holds institutional authority in a given context — remains the primary determinant of which direction local practice moves.

## Section 4: Human Factors — Who Did the Expanding, and Who Carried the Old Beliefs

## 4.1 The Missionary Type: Incentive Structures and Accommodation Pressure

Structural conditions — theological architecture, political apparatus, speed of conversion — do not implement themselves. They are mediated by human beings whose social positions, institutional accountabilities, and daily practical requirements shape how structural pressures are translated into actual behavior. The same coercive political environment produces different outcomes depending on who is doing the converting; the same accommodating political environment produces different outcomes depending on the social relationships the expansion agent depends on for survival. Attending to the specific human type doing the expanding is not a retreat from structural analysis but its necessary complement.

The Buddhist monk's structural situation deserves particular attention because it so directly determined the accommodation pattern Buddhism consistently produced. The *vinaya* — the monastic code governing every aspect of a monk's daily life — mandated that monks depend on lay communities for food, receiving their daily meal through the alms round. This was not incidental but doctrinally central: the monk's dependence on lay generosity was itself a mechanism for generating merit in the lay community, making the relationship between monastic and lay Buddhism structurally symbiotic. A monk who condemned his lay supporters' relationships with local spirit beings too aggressively would, at the most immediate practical level, go hungry. The *vinaya* community's institutional survival was directly indexed to its capacity to maintain lay support, which meant maintaining the goodwill of communities whose cosmological lives were populated with beings official Buddhism might otherwise have found problematic. The incentive to accommodate was not a strategic calculation made at the level of missionary policy — it was built into the daily structure of monastic existence.

The monk's situation differed from that of Christian missionaries in ways that track the divergence in outcomes with considerable precision. Early Christian wandering charismatics — Paul moving between Mediterranean communities, dependent on hospitality networks — shared some structural features with Buddhist monks: dependence on local support, no institutional hierarchy demanding doctrinal reports, accountability primarily to the communities being served. Their letters suggest flexibility in practice that later, more institutionalized Christianity would foreclose. The shift to episcopal structure changed the incentive environment decisively. A Carolingian bishop appointed to a newly conquered Saxon diocese answered to Frankish secular authority whose investment in visibly orthodox Christianity was political as well as theological. Accommodation that might have been pastorally sensible became institutionally dangerous — it could be read as insufficient commitment to the Christianizing project that justified Frankish expansion in the first place. The bishop's survival depended not on the goodwill of his Saxon parishioners but on the approval of his Frankish patrons.

The Jesuit case illustrates how acutely missionaries themselves recognized this incentive-structure problem. Matteo Ricci's accommodation of Chinese ritual practice — tolerating ancestor veneration, adopting Confucian scholarly dress, arguing that Chinese classical texts contained implicit Christian content — represented a conscious attempt to replicate something like the Buddhist monk's community-embedded position, building Christian authority through demonstrated cultural competence rather than imposed doctrinal conformity. The Chinese Rites Controversy that ultimately destroyed this project, culminating in Clement XI's condemnation in 1715, was not primarily a theological dispute but an institutional one: Rome's authority over its missions required that accommodation decisions flow downward from the hierarchy rather than upward from missionaries in the field. The incentive structure of a centralized institution with universal doctrinal claims could not indefinitely tolerate the precedent of local accommodation negotiated without authorization.

The Islamic expansion agent's variety was itself a structural feature of Islamic civilization's different relationships between religious and commercial authority. The merchant carried religious identity as a marker of trustworthiness in long-distance trade networks — his practice was simultaneously personal obligation and commercial signal, and aggressive condemnation of trading partners' religious practices was bad for business in the most immediate sense. The Ṣūfī shaykh's authority depended on a combination of spiritual charisma, scholarly lineage, and the practical services his institution provided: the *zāwiya* as school, court of mediation, and healing center made the shaykh's position in local society structurally analogous to the Irish abbot's, embedded in and dependent on the community he served. The jihād state's representatives had entirely different dependencies — their authority derived from military and administrative power that required no local community goodwill to sustain itself, removing the accommodation incentive entirely.

## 4.2 Who Carried the Old Beliefs: Gender, Marginality, and Functional Necessity

If the first human variable concerns who brought the new religion, the second concerns who maintained the old one. Across every case examined in this article — Irish fairy-lore, Saxon folk practice, Hausa bori, Sudanese zār, Malay bomoh tradition, Javanese kejawen — a consistent sociological pattern emerges that is too regular to be coincidental. Women, hunters, blacksmiths, healers, and socially marginal groups disproportionately carried indigenous spirit practice into and through the centuries of official religious transformation around them. Explaining this pattern requires attending to what the spirit beings actually did, and for whom.

Indigenous spirit complexes were not primarily cosmological luxury goods — speculative theology for those with the leisure to speculate. They were practical technologies for managing the most urgent and uncontrollable domains of human experience: the illness of a child, the failure of a crop, the difficulty of a labor, the inexplicable death of livestock, the outcome of a hunt in which survival was genuinely at stake. The beings governing these domains were not interchangeable with the official religious institution's resources. A parish priest could offer last rites; he could not reliably cure a sick cow. A mosque's Friday sermon addressed collective Muslim identity; it did not provide the specific ritual protocol required to placate the spirit whose displeasure, in the local cosmological framework, had caused the well to run dry. The gap between what official religion offered and what these practical domains required was the ecological niche that indigenous spirit practice continued to occupy regardless of theological condemnation.

Women occupied this niche with particular regularity because the domains most inadequately served by official religious institutions — fertility, childbirth, infant illness, household protection, the management of domestic misfortune — were disproportionately women's practical concerns in virtually every society under examination. Islamic institutions were male-dominated in their scholarly and legal dimensions; Catholic parish structure centered on a male clergy; Buddhist monasticism's most rigorous forms were male. Women had less access to official institutional resources and more practical dependence on the spirit complex's functional capacities. Janice Boddy's landmark study of zār possession in Islamic Sudan demonstrated that the spirit-possession ceremony gave women not only ritual resources for managing illness but a structured space for articulating social grievances and embodying, through the spirits who mounted them, identities and authorities unavailable in their ordinary social positions. The zār was doing work that no Islamic institution was designed to do, in a domain — women's psychological and social experience within patriarchal structures — that official Islam in that context largely did not address.

The same functional logic operated for occupational specialists whose work placed them in regular contact with forces that required management beyond the official religion's reach. Irish blacksmiths maintained relationships with spirit beings governing iron — an unpredictable and dangerous material whose successful working seemed to require more than technical skill. Hausa hunters in the bori complex maintained relationships with bush spirits governing the dangerous transition between settled and wild space. Malay fishermen maintained protocols with sea spirits governing waters that could kill without warning. These were not superstitions in the pejorative sense — residual irrationalities clinging to populations insufficiently educated in correct theology. They were functional relationships with the cosmological dimensions of genuinely hazardous practical domains, maintained by people whose lives depended on those relationships working.

The suppression paradox that runs through every case in this article follows directly from this analysis. Demonization consistently failed not because it lacked institutional authority or theological force but because it offered no substitute for the functional work the condemned practice was doing. Burchard of Worms catalogued cattle-protection rituals in 11th-century Germany not because Saxon peasants were confused about Christian theology but because their cattle needed protecting and the spirit complex had historically provided the means. Dan Fodio's reform movement drove bori underground in northern Nigeria without eliminating the illnesses, difficult childbirths, and domestic crises that the bori ceremony had addressed. The Irish *bean feasa* maintained fairy-lore into modernity not in defiance of Catholic teaching but alongside it, serving the practical domains that the parish priest did not reach. The spirits persisted because they were working, and nothing adequately replaced them.

## Section 5: Why Spirit Beliefs Never Die — The Cognitive Science of Religion

## 5.1 Pascal Boyer: Why Spirit Beings Have the Shape They Do

The preceding sections have documented a pattern so consistent it demands a unified explanation. Across every tradition examined, every political context, every missionary strategy, indigenous spirit beliefs persisted. Demonization drove them underground; they resurfaced. Sanctoral displacement redirected them; the old practices continued alongside the new. Jihād suppression politicized them; they returned wearing the political charge their suppression had given them. No strategy, however coercively backed or theologically sophisticated, succeeded in eliminating the beliefs it targeted. The Irish fairy mounds continued receiving offerings three centuries after Patrick. The Saxon springs continued receiving gifts three centuries after Charlemagne. The Hausa *iskoki* continued mounting their human hosts three centuries after dan Fodio. Explaining this requires moving below the level of institutional strategy and political condition to ask what is generating these beliefs in the first place — and why what generates them is impervious to theological argument.

The cognitive science of religion provides the most rigorous available answer. Its foundational claim is methodologically precise: religious beliefs are not sui generis phenomena requiring their own explanatory framework, nor are they the products of confusion, trauma, or insufficient education. They are natural outputs of ordinary cognitive mechanisms that evolved for purposes entirely unrelated to religion and that generate religious content as a predictable byproduct of their normal operation. Pascal Boyer's contribution was to identify which mechanisms are doing this work and why they consistently produce beings with the specific features — the particular combination of familiarity and strangeness — that characterizes spirit entities across every human culture yet documented.

Boyer's starting point is the observation that the human mind is not a general-purpose reasoning device but a collection of specialized systems, each tuned to process specific domains of information rapidly and automatically. Several of these systems are directly relevant to the generation of religious belief. The *agent detection system* is a hair-trigger mechanism tuned by evolutionary pressure to detect the presence of intentional agents in the environment. Its calibration strongly favors false positives — the cost of failing to detect a predator vastly exceeds the cost of falsely detecting one where none exists — which means it spontaneously populates ambiguous stimuli with agents. The rustling in undergrowth, the feeling of being watched in an empty room, the face perceived in cloud formations: these are the agent detection system doing its job, and its job is not to be epistemically cautious but to be fast.

Once an agent is detected, the *theory of mind system* fires automatically, building a rich model of the detected agent's intentions, emotional states, knowledge, and likely behavior. This system evolved for tracking other humans — modeling their beliefs and desires to predict and influence their actions — but it operates indiscriminately, attributing mental states to moving geometric shapes on screens, to thermostats, to weather systems. It cannot be switched off by the knowledge that the detected agent is probably not real, because it operates below the level of deliberate reasoning. Critically, the mental models it generates include what Boyer identifies as the *Full Access Agent* property: detected agents are automatically modeled as having access to socially relevant information — what you have done, what you intend, what your moral status is — that you have not explicitly shared. The ancestors know what you did. The gods see what is in your heart. This property appears in spirit beings across every culture, not because any particular theology specifies it but because the cognitive system generating the beings automatically attributes it.

The question Boyer's framework then addresses is why spirit beings have the specific features they do — why they are neither maximally ordinary nor maximally strange. The answer is the *Minimally Counterintuitive* (MCI) agent concept. Religious beings consistently violate a small number of default inferences from one familiar ontological category while preserving the rest. The ghost passes through walls — a violation of physical solidity — but remembers insults and seeks revenge, preserving the full social and psychological inference system that makes it a tractable agent. The ancestor has no physical body but knows your thoughts and cares about your moral behavior, preserving the intentionality and moral concern that make propitiation meaningful. The sacred mountain has no biological life but punishes trespassers, preserving enough agentive inference to motivate avoidance and offering behavior.

The violations are what make the concept memorable and interesting; the preserved inferences are what make it cognitively workable. A being that violated all default inferences simultaneously — that had no intentions and no non-intentions, that was both everywhere and nowhere and neither — would be theologically sophisticated in the manner of negative theology's *via negativa* but cognitively unworkable. You cannot pray to it, fear it, propitiate it, or negotiate with it, because none of the social-interaction cognitive machinery can get purchase on something so thoroughly counterintuitive. Mystical theology consistently tends toward this limit and consistently fails to generate popular religious engagement, which is precisely what Boyer's framework predicts.

Experimental evidence supports the MCI memory advantage. Items violating two to three default inferences from a familiar ontological category are recalled more reliably and with greater confidence than either fully intuitive items (too ordinary to flag for retention) or maximally counterintuitive ones (too incoherent to process). The domovoi, the hantu, the iskoki, the sídhe are all MCI-formatted beings. They are memorable by cognitive design — not the design of any intentional agent but the design of selection pressure on the memory systems that retained information most useful for navigating a world populated with both real agents and dangerous ambiguities.

This is why suppression consistently fails. Indigenous spirit beings are not held primarily by conscious theological commitment that can be dislodged by counter-argument or official condemnation. They are generated and maintained by cognitive systems operating below deliberate reasoning, systems that every human being carries regardless of cultural background or official religious affiliation. Every child born into a community where these beings circulate will spontaneously regenerate belief in them, because the cognitive machinery that generates the belief is universal human equipment. Burchard of Worms was not documenting the stubborn irrationality of insufficiently converted Saxons. He was documenting the predictable output of human minds doing what human minds do.

The MCI framework also explains why displacement consistently outperforms demonization as a long-term strategy. Demonization reclassifies an MCI being — the domovoi is now a demon — without eliminating the cognitive machinery generating it. The being continues to be generated and experienced with all its original functional valence; it simply now carries a negative theological label that sits uneasily atop the cognitive reality. Displacement redirects the cognitive machinery toward a new but equally MCI-formatted being — St. Brigid, with her healing wells and her February feast day — who can perform the functional work the old being did while satisfying the same cognitive architecture that made the old being believable. The machinery is not defeated; it is given a new object that fits its requirements equally well.

## 5.2 Harvey Whitehouse: How Ritual Form Shapes Community and Resistance

Boyer's framework explains why spirit beliefs are generated and retained at the level of individual cognition. Whitehouse's Modes of Religiosity theory addresses a different but complementary question: why do religious practices take the specific ritual forms they do, and how do those forms create communities whose bonds vary so dramatically in their intensity, their scale, and their resistance to disruption? The answer locates religious transmission in the architecture of human memory and identifies two distinct transmission strategies, each exploiting a different memory system and each producing characteristically different social consequences.

Whitehouse's theory emerged from fieldwork in Papua New Guinea in the late 1980s, where he observed two groups sharing the same cultural background practicing radically different ritual forms. The Pomio Kivung movement conducted regular, repetitive, low-intensity communal ceremonies organized around explicit moral codes and codified cosmological beliefs — calm, orderly, doctrinally clear, and geographically widespread. A splinter group that broke away underwent a dramatic ritual intensification: all-night ceremonies, physical demands, extraordinary sensory experiences, collective emotional peaks that members described as transformative and ineffable. The contrast was sharp enough to demand a theoretical account, and Whitehouse's account generalizes across the full range of human religious practice.

The *doctrinal mode* is characterized by high-frequency, low-arousal, routinized ritual practice — the daily prayer, the weekly service, the seasonal liturgical cycle. Its transmission vehicle is *semantic memory*, the cognitive system that stores general knowledge and conceptual content independently of the specific occasions on which it was acquired. You know the meaning of the Eucharist without necessarily remembering which of hundreds of Masses you attended was the one where you first understood it. Doctrinal transmission requires explicit verbal instruction to compensate for the low arousal that makes episodic encoding weak, which creates institutional pressure toward codification, textual preservation, specialist clergy, and the development of orthodoxy as a meaningful category. Its social consequence is the large-scale anonymous community: membership is constituted by shared doctrinal content that can be specified, transmitted through texts, and verified through confession or catechism. A Catholic and another Catholic in another country have a basis for solidarity without ever having met, because their shared identity is constituted by shared content rather than shared experience.

The *imagistic mode* operates through the opposite pole: low-frequency, high-arousal ritual involving physical ordeal, unusual sensory stimulation, or emotionally overwhelming collective experience. Its transmission vehicle is *episodic memory* — the system encoding specific autobiographical events with vivid experiential detail, emotional intensity, and strong personal significance. The neuroscience here is precise: emotional arousal at the moment of encoding significantly enhances memory consolidation, with stress hormones modulating amygdala activity in ways that produce unusually durable and detailed memories of high-arousal events. The all-night initiation ceremony is not an inefficient transmission strategy — it is extraordinarily efficient episodic memory encoding. The initiate will remember *this specific night* for the rest of his life, and that memory will constitute his identity as a full member of the group.

Because imagistic communities are unified by shared episodic memory of a specific experience, they are structurally limited to those who were present. You cannot extend imagistic community membership to those who were not there, because the bond is the shared memory, and the memory is irreducibly particular. This is why imagistic communities are characteristically small, intensely cohesive, and extraordinarily loyal — the social bond has the specificity and emotional depth of the episodic memory constituting it.

The implications for the persistence of indigenous spirit practice are direct and powerful. Bori ceremonies, zār possession rituals, and spirit-initiation complexes across sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia are paradigmatically imagistic: low frequency, high arousal, episodic memory encoding, small-community bonds constituted by shared transformative experience. Whitehouse's framework predicts that these practices will be the most resistant to suppression of any religious form, because their community bonds are not constituted by doctrinal propositions that can be replaced by counter-propositions but by shared episodic memories of overwhelming experiences that no theological argument can retroactively undo. Dan Fodio could offer correct doctrine; he could not offer an experience equivalent in emotional intensity and community-constituting power to the bori ceremony. The imagistic bond survived because nothing in the reformist toolkit was designed to replace what it provided.

The modes framework also illuminates the internal structure of the world religions themselves. Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam are primarily doctrinal in their institutional forms — their capacity to expand to billions of anonymous co-believers depends on the scalability of doctrinal transmission. But each contains imagistic practices nested within the larger doctrinal structure: Vajrayāna initiation, Christian mystical orders, Sufi *dhikr* at its most intense. These consistently produce the small, intensely cohesive communities characteristic of imagistic practice. They are not aberrations within their respective traditions but the mechanism through which the traditions generate the deepest community bonds available to them — bonds that the large-scale doctrinal community, for all its reach, cannot replicate.

## Further Directions

1. **Robert McCauley and Thomas Lawson's ritual competence theory** predicts which rituals will be performed frequently versus rarely based on whether gods figure as agents or patients in the ritual structure — reading it alongside Whitehouse's modes would add a formal syntactic dimension to the analysis of why certain indigenous rituals resist substitution more stubbornly than others.
2. **The neuroscience of episodic memory consolidation** — particularly work on amygdala-mediated emotional enhancement of memory encoding — provides the biological substrate for Whitehouse's modes theory and would ground the imagistic mode's resistance to suppression in mechanisms observable below the level of behavior.
3. **Cross-cultural experimental testing of the MCI hypothesis** has expanded considerably beyond Boyer's original formulations, with researchers including Ara Norenzayan, Cristine Legare, and Rachel Watson-Jones examining how MCI concepts interact with cultural learning and credibility-enhancing displays — this literature would sharpen the cognitive baseline established here.
4. **The application of modes theory to new religious movements** — where imagistic intensity functions as a recruitment and retention mechanism in contexts where doctrinal content alone is insufficient — would test the framework under contemporary conditions with good documentary evidence and relative theoretical control.
5. **Webb Keane's semiotic analysis** in *Christian Moderns* of how Protestant assumptions about sincere speech and transparent reference made certain accommodations cognitively impossible that Catholic semiotics permitted — extending this to Islamic and Buddhist semiotic economies would integrate the theological variable with the cognitive one in ways the present section only gestures toward.
6. **The relationship between Boyer's Full Access Agent property and Norenzayan's Big Gods argument** — addressed in the following section — deserves explicit bridge treatment: the Full Access Agent is the cognitive mechanism through which moralizing omniscient deities achieve their behavioral effects, and tracing the connection precisely would unify the individual-cognitive and macro-historical levels of analysis.

## Section 6: Big Gods and the Scaling of Cooperation — Why World Religions Won

## 6.1 The Cooperation Problem and the Big God Solution

Boyer's framework explains why spirit beings are cognitively natural and why suppression cannot eliminate them. Whitehouse's framework explains why certain ritual forms create bonds of extraordinary intensity and why those bonds resist disruption. But neither framework, on its own, addresses a different and equally pressing historical question: why did the traditions carrying Big God theologies — Buddhism with its karmic moral order, Christianity and Islam with their omniscient moralizing deities — expand so dramatically while the indigenous traditions they encountered did not? The cognitive naturalness of MCI spirit beings does not explain why the yakṣa's tradition stayed in its valley while the Buddha's teaching crossed mountain ranges and ocean channels. Something beyond cognitive architecture was doing the work of differential expansion, and Ara Norenzayan's *Big Gods* argument identifies what it was.

The problem Norenzayan is solving is one of the most consequential in human history. The evolved social psychology of *Homo sapiens* was calibrated for groups of roughly 150 individuals — the scale at which direct reputation tracking, reciprocal altruism, and kin selection reliably generate stable cooperation. Within this horizon, you know who cheated last season, who kept their promise, who can be trusted with a debt. Beyond it, these mechanisms break down. You cannot track the reputation of every individual in a trading city of ten thousand, let alone an empire of millions. You cannot rely on future reciprocation from strangers you will never encounter again. Yet humans demonstrably built cooperative societies at precisely these scales — cities, empires, long-distance trading networks, legal systems governing the behavior of strangers toward each other — and the question is what made this possible.

Norenzayan's answer centers on a specific type of supernatural agent distinguished not by power alone but by a precise combination of cognitive and moral properties. A *Big God* is broadly aware of human behavior including its hidden dimensions, specifically concerned with whether humans adhere to prosocial norms governing honest dealing and treatment of others, and disposed to respond to moral behavior with consequences — punishment or reward — that matter to the people whose behavior is being monitored. This combination is not universal among supernatural beings. Zeus was powerful but his moral interest in human inter-personal behavior was selective and inconsistent. The nāga cared about proper reverence at its river and the welfare of its immediate devotee community. The domovoi monitored household behavior but not the behavior of strangers in the market. None of these beings provided what Norenzayan identifies as the operative mechanism: the continuous, universally scoped sense of moral surveillance that modifies behavior toward strangers in contexts where no human observer is present and no future interaction is expected.

The behavioral economics literature provides the most direct evidence for how this mechanism works. The *watching eyes effect* — the well-replicated finding that even minimal, obviously non-sentient observer cues significantly increase prosocial behavior in economic games — demonstrates that human reputation-management cognition activates automatically in response to perceived observation, operating below deliberate reasoning. Haley and Fessler's stylized eye images increased contributions to economic games without participants consciously deciding to be more generous; Bateson and colleagues' eye images above a university common room coffee station nearly tripled contributions to the honesty box. The agent detection system interprets observer cues as evidence of potential watchers; the reputation-management system adjusts behavior accordingly. If minimal, obviously artificial eye images produce this effect, a cognitively compelling belief in an omniscient moral agent who is genuinely and continuously watching should produce it continuously, across every social situation, including those involving anonymous strangers with whom no future interaction is anticipated.

This is what Big God beliefs provided that parochial god traditions could not: an extended monitoring environment that made cooperative behavior toward strangers cognitively equivalent to cooperative behavior toward known community members under observation. The person who believes God sees everything they do in private carries an observer into every anonymous transaction. The person whose supernatural beings care primarily about proper ritual treatment of themselves and the welfare of their immediate community does not. The cooperative asymmetry this generates, compounded across thousands of transactions and millions of individuals over generations, is a genuine functional advantage in the contexts created by expanding trade networks, agricultural intensification, and political consolidation.

Norenzayan and Shariff's priming experiments provided experimental traction on this mechanism. Participants subliminally primed with religious concepts — God, divine, sacred, spirit — shared significantly more with anonymous strangers in dictator games than control participants, with the effect driven specifically by participants who already held god-beliefs. A crucial parallel finding: secular justice-system primes — court, jury, police, contract — produced equivalent prosocial effects. This equivalence is not a complication for the Big God hypothesis but its most important implication, pointing toward what Norenzayan calls the substitutability of divine and secular monitoring as cooperative technologies — an implication developed fully in the section's second half.

## 6.2 Big Gods, Parochial Gods, and the Competitive Logic of Religious Expansion

The Big God hypothesis reframes the entire comparative history developed in preceding sections. The expansion of Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam into territories governed by indigenous spirit traditions was not only a matter of military conquest, commercial contact, or missionary zeal. It involved a genuine functional advantage in the specific cooperative challenges that expanding political economies posed — challenges that parochial god traditions, calibrated for smaller and more homogeneous communities, were not designed to meet.

The distinction between Big Gods and parochial gods is not a distinction between sophisticated and primitive religious thought. The nāga tradition encoded genuine ecological knowledge about river systems and flood management. The bori complex provided functional resources for illness management, social mediation, and community cohesion that no Islamic institution replicated. The sídhe maintained the cosmological and agricultural relationships that Irish communities depended on for practical survival. These were not failures of theological sophistication but appropriate calibrations for the cooperative scales at which they operated. The nāga was an effective cooperative technology for the riparian community depending on its river; it was not an effective cooperative technology for an expanding empire requiring coordination among strangers across multiple ecological zones and ethnic communities. The parochial god's limitation was not intellectual but scalar.

This scalar mismatch explains the consistent pattern of world religion expansion without reducing it to conquest or deception. Communities incorporating into larger political and economic systems — through trade, through imperial absorption, through agricultural intensification linking previously isolated communities into market networks — faced new cooperative challenges that their existing religious technologies were not calibrated to handle. The Big God tradition arriving alongside the merchant or the monk offered a cooperative infrastructure genuinely useful for the new scale of interaction. The conversion that followed was not simply the replacement of false belief with true belief, or the imposition of foreign culture through force, but the adoption of a cooperative technology appropriate to a new social scale — with the parochial tradition persisting, as documented throughout this article, in the domains of intimate community life where it continued doing necessary work.

Norenzayan's framework requires an important qualification that the preceding sections' evidence consistently supports. Big God religions promoted cooperation within the moral community defined by the religion while remaining fully compatible with exploitation of, hostility toward, and violence against out-groups. The in-group prosociality generated by Big God monitoring does not extend automatically to those outside the moral community's boundaries, and the historical record of world religion expansion documents this with painful regularity — the same cognitive system that generated extraordinary charity toward co-religionists generated the Crusade, the jihād against apostates, the colonial missionary's confidence that indigenous populations existed outside the circle of full moral consideration. The cooperative advantage of Big God beliefs is real; it is also bounded, and the location of the boundary has been among the most consequential — and most contested — questions in the history of each tradition.

The final movement of Norenzayan's argument addresses the most striking contemporary data point for the Big God hypothesis: the existence of highly secular, highly cooperative societies in which Big God beliefs have substantially attenuated without apparent collapse of cooperative norms. If Big God beliefs were necessary rather than historically contingent cooperative technologies, their attenuation should produce cooperative breakdown. The Scandinavian evidence — among the world's least religious societies by survey measures, yet among the highest in social trust, lowest in corruption, and most generous in collective welfare provision — suggests instead that Big God beliefs are *sufficient* but not *necessary* for cooperative scaling. Norenzayan's explanation is that the cooperative institutions bootstrapped into existence by centuries of Big God religion — impartial legal systems, honest bureaucracies, public education, impersonal markets — can sustain cooperation without active divine monitoring once they are sufficiently developed, because they perform the same function through secular means. The court and the police force extend monitoring into anonymous social situations; the legal contract makes defection costly regardless of whether the parties believe God is watching. The experimental equivalence of religious and secular justice-system primes on prosocial behavior is precisely what this substitutability hypothesis predicts.

The secularization implication that follows is precise and often misunderstood. Secularization is not a universal process driven by modernization, education, or scientific literacy per se. It is a conditional process that depends on the prior development of secular cooperative institutions robust enough to substitute for divine monitoring. Societies where Big God beliefs are still doing significant cooperative work — where legal institutions are weak, corruption is prevalent, and impersonal trust among strangers remains thin — will not secularize on the Scandinavian model regardless of economic development, because removing the Big God monitoring before the secular substitute is functional would genuinely degrade cooperative capacity. The prediction is not that religion will persist because people are insufficiently rational but that it will persist where it is still doing necessary cooperative work that nothing else is yet doing — a prediction with immediate implications for the contemporary cases, from Nigerian Pentecostalism to Indonesian Islamic revival, that the preceding sections of this article have examined.

## Further Directions

1. **Joseph Henrich's *The WEIRDest People in the World* (2020)** provides the most historically specific account of how Western Christianity's particular institutional interventions — especially the medieval Church's prohibition of cousin marriage and its dissolution of extended kinship structures — built the impersonal cooperative institutions that eventually substituted for Big God monitoring, offering a more granular mechanism for Norenzayan's principle 8 that connects directly to the Irish and Saxon cases examined earlier.
2. **Dominic Johnson's *God Is Watching You* (2015)** develops an independent evolutionary account of supernatural punishment beliefs that converges with but differs from Norenzayan's in treating supernatural punishment as an individual-level psychological adaptation rather than a group-level cultural technology — the tension between these levels of analysis is theoretically productive and unresolved.
3. **The karma system as Big God functional equivalent** — whether impersonal cosmic moral accounting produces the same behavioral effects as a personal moralizing deity — is an open empirical question with significant implications for the Buddhist comparative case; experimental comparison of karma-priming and God-priming effects on prosocial behavior in Buddhist populations would directly test whether the cognitive mechanism requires a personal agent or merely a moralized cosmic monitoring system.
4. **The Seshat Global History Databank controversy** — the contested *Nature* paper by Whitehouse, Turchin, and colleagues testing whether moralizing gods preceded or followed increases in societal complexity in the historical record, and the subsequent reanalyses disputing the original findings — deserves careful reading as the most ambitious quantitative test of the Big God hypothesis at historical scale and an illustration of the genuine difficulty of establishing causal direction in macro-historical data.
5. **The feminist critique of Big God theology** — the moralizing Big God is historically male, his moral community has historically excluded women, enslaved people, and colonial subjects from full membership, and his institutional capture by male hierarchies shaped whose cooperation was enabled and at what cost — integrating this analysis with Norenzayan's cooperative technology argument requires asking whose cooperation Big Gods historically generated and what structural exclusions that generation depended upon.
6. **Contemporary authoritarian secular surveillance states** — China's social credit system, North Korea's personality cult, Soviet-era collective monitoring — as functional analogues to Big God monitoring deserve theoretical treatment; if secular panopticism produces equivalent cooperative effects to divine surveillance, this would strongly support the substitutability hypothesis while raising questions about whether the cooperative benefits of Big God monitoring can be disentangled from the political authoritarianism that secular equivalents tend to produce.
7. **The application to new religious movements** in contexts of state weakness — Pentecostalism's extraordinary growth in sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America precisely in regions where secular cooperative institutions are least developed — provides a natural experiment in the substitutability hypothesis's predictions, with the further implication that the pattern of world religion expansion documented in this article may be ongoing rather than historically closed.
8. **Will Gervais and Norenzayan's subsequent work** on *perceived divine monitoring* as a distinct variable from general religiosity — their finding that it is specifically the watching property of god-beliefs rather than doctrinal content or ritual participation that drives prosocial effects — sharpens the cognitive mechanism and would anchor deeper investigation of how the Full Access Agent property identified by Boyer connects to the macro-historical cooperative dynamics Norenzayan documents.

## Section 7: Synthesis and Conclusion — A Multi-Causal Model

## 7.1 Toward a Weighted Multi-Causal Model

The preceding sections have moved through successive levels of analysis — theological, political, human, cognitive, macro-historical — each contributing explanatory power that the others cannot supply alone. The task of a conclusion is not to declare one level the winner but to specify how these levels interact, which variables carry the most weight under which conditions, and what the assembled framework reveals that no single level of analysis could reveal on its own.

The most important structural finding is that two variables together explain the majority of variance in encounter outcomes across the cases examined: the *cosmological carrying capacity* of the expanding tradition and the *presence or absence of coercive state apparatus* behind the expansion. These variables are largely independent of each other — a tradition with high carrying capacity can expand with or without coercive backing, and coercive expansion can be attempted with high or low carrying capacity — and their combinations produce the four characteristic outcome patterns that the comparative evidence consistently displays.

Where high cosmological carrying capacity meets the absence of coercive state backing — Buddhist expansion into mainland Southeast Asia, Ṣūfī trade-network Islam into West Africa, Irish monasticism into the mythological landscape of its own island — absorption and displacement are the dominant outcomes. The tradition's theological architecture accommodates indigenous beings without internal contradiction; the expansion agent's dependence on local community goodwill incentivizes accommodation; and the slow generational pace of conversion gives the community-level negotiation processes through which absorption operates the time they require. The result is not the elimination of indigenous cosmology but its gradual repositioning within the expanding tradition's framework — the nāga becomes the temple guardian, the goddess becomes the saint, the bori spirit becomes the Muslim jinn — while the functional work the indigenous beings performed continues largely uninterrupted under new theological nomenclature.

Where low cosmological carrying capacity meets the presence of coercive state apparatus — Carolingian Christianity in Saxony, reformist jihād in Hausa Nigeria, Spanish colonial Christianity in Mesoamerica — aggressive demonization is the dominant institutional strategy. But the outcome is not suppression; it is the underground persistence and politicization of the suppressed practice. Burchard of Worms documents this in 11th-century Germany; the colonial Inquisition documents it in 16th-century Mexico; the sociology of post-jihād bori documents it in 19th and 20th-century northern Nigeria. The pattern is consistent enough to constitute a near-law: coercive suppression without functional substitution drives indigenous practice below the threshold of official visibility without eliminating it, and the act of suppression frequently generates a political charge — resistance identity, gendered solidarity, occupational community — that makes the surviving practice more durable than accommodation would have produced.

The secondary variables — missionary type, treatment of indigenous intellectual specialists, speed of conversion, gender division of religious labor, competitive religious market conditions — function primarily as modifiers that amplify or constrain the effects of the two primary variables. Slow conversion amplifies the absorption tendency of high-carrying-capacity traditions by allowing community-level negotiation time to operate; rapid conquest constrains it by creating an artificial terminus after which official divergence from popular belief must be immediate. Integration of indigenous intellectual specialists amplifies absorption by preserving the cosmological knowledge that the absorption process requires; their elimination constrains it by fragmenting that knowledge into undocumented folk practice. The gender variable operates consistently across all cases as a structural predictor of which indigenous practices survive most stubbornly — those governing the domains most inadequately served by official religious institutions and most practically necessary for the populations depending on them.

The cognitive variables identified in Section 5 function differently from all the others: they are not modifiers of outcome but limiting conditions on the entire enterprise. Boyer's MCI framework establishes that indigenous spirit beliefs are generated by universal cognitive mechanisms operating below deliberate reasoning, which means that no combination of theological architecture, political force, missionary strategy, and institutional pressure can produce genuine elimination of these beliefs at the population level. What changes under religious encounter is not whether spirit beings are believed in but how they are classified, named, and officially positioned within the expanding tradition's framework. The domovoi does not disappear — it becomes a demon, or a saint's adversary, or a residual superstition that educated people deny believing while continuing to propitiate. Whitehouse's modes framework adds that the most resistant practices are those organized around imagistic ritual forms, whose community bonds are constituted by shared episodic memory rather than doctrinal content and therefore cannot be replaced by theological argument or doctrinal counter-proposal. The Hausa bori ceremony, the Sudanese zār possession ritual, the Irish initiation patterns encoded in seasonal festival practice — these survived where more doctrinally organized indigenous practices did not, because their social bonds were of a kind that no expanding tradition's official program was designed to dissolve.

Norenzayan's Big Gods argument completes the framework by explaining the macro-historical asymmetry that the preceding analysis documents without fully explaining: why world religions expanded while indigenous traditions generally did not, regardless of the specific encounter strategies deployed. The cooperative-technological advantage of Big God beliefs — their capacity to extend moral monitoring into anonymous large-scale social interactions beyond the reach of direct reputation tracking — gave the expanding traditions a genuine functional advantage in the contexts created by growing trade networks, imperial consolidation, and agricultural intensification. This advantage was not theological but sociological, and it operated through the cognitive mechanism Boyer identified: the Full Access Agent property of compelling moralizing deities activated reputation-management cognition continuously across all social situations, generating cooperative behavior toward strangers at scales that parochial god traditions were not calibrated to produce.

## 7.2 What Persists, What Transforms, and What This Tells Us

The spirits survived. This is the empirical finding that the entire article has been building toward, and it requires stating plainly before it is qualified. Across three major religious traditions, across every political configuration from voluntary trade-network conversion to military conquest, across fourteen centuries of documented encounter, indigenous spirit beliefs persisted in every case examined. They did not persist unchanged — they were reclassified, renamed, repositioned, driven underground, politicized, feminized, and fragmented — but the beings remained, the practices continued, and the functional relationships between human communities and their local supernatural inhabitants proved more durable than any institutional program for their elimination.

What changed under religious encounter was the *classification* of beings whose existence and practical importance remained constant. The transformation was genuinely significant — being reclassified as a jinn rather than an autonomous spirit entity, or as a saint's sacred spring rather than a goddess's residence, is not a trivial change in a person's cosmological world. It altered the ritual protocols through which engagement was legitimated, the institutional authorities whose approval was required, and the social contexts in which practice could be publicly acknowledged. But it did not alter the underlying structure of the relationship: the being inhabited a specific place, governed a specific domain, required specific forms of acknowledgment, and responded to human behavior in ways that mattered practically. The theological frame around this relationship changed; the relationship itself persisted.

The ecological dimension of this persistence deserves more attention than the comparative religion literature has typically given it. Indigenous spirit complexes frequently encoded genuine environmental knowledge — taboos on specific fishing practices, prohibitions on clearing certain forest areas, seasonal agricultural restrictions — with spirit beings serving as the cosmological enforcement mechanism for what were functionally environmental management protocols. Where expanding traditions preserved spirit beings through absorption or displacement, they inadvertently preserved these protocols. The nāga temples of mainland Southeast Asia maintained riparian sacred zones that regulated water use and watershed integrity. The Irish pattern wells sanctified under saints' names maintained water management infrastructure that pre-Christian communities had developed over generations. Where expanding traditions suppressed spirit beings through demonization without offering functional substitutes, the environmental management knowledge encoded in the suppressed tradition sometimes fragmented along with it — a dimension of the encounter's consequences that connects the historical analysis developed here to contemporary concerns about indigenous ecological knowledge and its preservation.

The persistence of spirit beliefs also carries implications for how secularization is understood in the contemporary world. Norenzayan's substitutability hypothesis predicts that Big God beliefs will attenuate where secular cooperative institutions become sufficiently robust to replace divine monitoring as the operative cooperative technology. But the MCI beings documented throughout this article are not Big Gods — they are parochial spirits governing intimate domains of practical life, and no secular institution, however robust, is designed to replace what the domovoi does for the household or what the bori complex does for the woman managing a difficult childbirth in a context where medical infrastructure is absent. The secularization of Big God beliefs and the attenuation of parochial spirit beliefs are different processes operating through different mechanisms, and conflating them produces predictions that the evidence does not support. The Scandinavian societies most thoroughly secularized from Big God belief have not eliminated belief in ghosts, luck, meaningful coincidence, or the sense of presence in certain natural places — the MCI beings persist at the popular level even as the moralizing omniscient deity fades from institutional prominence.

The encounter between world religions and indigenous cosmologies was never the simple replacement narrative that missionary history and its secular critics have both, in different ways, tended to tell. It was a negotiation conducted at every level simultaneously — theological, political, institutional, personal, cognitive — and its outcomes were determined not by the superiority of any tradition's truth claims but by the interaction of structural conditions that this article has attempted to map. The spirits that Buddhist missionaries found in the rivers of Southeast Asia, that Christian monks found in the fairy mounds of Ireland, that Ṣūfī traders found in the iron-working shrines of West Africa, were not waiting to be replaced. They were working. And because they were working — because the cognitive architecture generating belief in them is universal human equipment, because the functional domains they governed remained inadequately served by official institutions, because the imagistic community bonds constituting their most intense ritual expressions were of a kind that no doctrinal tradition could dissolve — they continued to work, under new names, in new cosmological frameworks, doing what they had always done. The theological frame changed. The relationship between human communities and the beings inhabiting their landscapes, their households, their bodies, and their fears endured.
