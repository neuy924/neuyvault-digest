# Beyond the Boundary: Transgression, Non-Dual Experience, and the Limits of Language in Buddhist and Comparative Perspective

-----

## Section 1: Introduction — The Scandal That Teaches

### 1.1 The Problem of the Holy Fool

In a temple in Hangzhou, a monk eats meat and drinks wine in open defiance of the Vinaya — the monastic code he swore to uphold — and is venerated as a living bodhisattva. In the mountains of Bhutan, a wandering yogi sleeps with hundreds of women and mocks the most learned lamas of his generation, and is credited with subduing demons and transmitting awakening. In fifteenth-century Japan, a certified Zen master visits brothels, publishes explicit erotic poetry under his own name, and is remembered as one of the most profound figures in the history of Japanese Buddhism. In the streets of Moscow, a naked man raves in winter, insults the tsar to his face, and after his death is canonised by the very Church whose hierarchies he publicly humiliated.

These four figures — 濟公 Jìgōng, Drukpa Künley (*’Brug pa Kun legs* 竹巴袞列), Ikkyū Sōjun (一休宗純), and the Russian *yurodivye* or “fools for Christ” — belong to different centuries, different continents, different religious civilisations. They share no obvious historical connection. And yet the structure of their sanctity is, in each case, organised around the same paradox: the holy person is recognisable precisely by their violation of the codes that ordinarily define holiness.

This recurrence demands explanation. The temptation is to account for it biographically — to read these figures as unusually honest human beings who found institutional religion’s demands for propriety incompatible with their genuine spiritual attainment, and who paid the social cost of that incompatibility with conspicuous courage. This reading is not wrong. But it is insufficient. It explains the individual and misses the type. What requires explanation is not why *this particular monk* drank wine, but why multiple unconnected traditions have produced, recognised, and eventually enshrined figures for whom transgression is not incidental to their sanctity but **constitutive of it** — figures whose violation of religious norms is not despite their holiness but, in some sense to be specified, *because* of it.

The central question this article addresses is therefore structural rather than biographical: **what work does transgression perform within a soteriological system?** A soteriological system is a coherent account of what human beings are, what is wrong with their ordinary condition, and what practices, realisations, or transformations constitute liberation from that condition. Within such a system, transgression — the violation of the system’s own norms — would seem to be straightforwardly counterproductive. The Vinaya exists because certain forms of behaviour are held to obstruct awakening. Violating the Vinaya, on this view, should deepen bondage rather than dissolve it. The puzzle is that certain figures, in certain traditions, under certain conditions, appear to do precisely the opposite: their violation of the system’s norms is received, by significant communities of practitioners and devotees, as evidence of the system’s deepest truth rather than its negation.

Understanding how this is possible requires taking seriously the internal logic of each tradition — not merely describing what transgressive saints do, but asking what doctrinal framework makes their behaviour coherent as a soteriological act rather than a moral failure. The answer, as this article will show, differs significantly across traditions and tracks the underlying soteriology with surprising precision.

-----

### 1.2 Scope and Method

This article examines four traditions: Chinese Chan Buddhism as represented by the figure of Jìgōng (1130–1209); Tibetan Vajrayāna Buddhism as represented by Drukpa Künley (1455–1529) and the broader *smyon pa* (“holy madman”) tradition; Japanese Rinzai Zen as represented by Ikkyū Sōjun (1394–1481) and his poetic collection the *Kyōunshū* 狂雲集; and Eastern Orthodox Christianity as represented by the *yurodstvo* tradition, with particular attention to the Russian holy fools of the fifteenth through eighteenth centuries.

The method is **structural and comparative** rather than historical or genealogical. This article does not argue that these traditions influenced one another, or that the similarities between them reflect shared historical origins. The comparison is synchronic: it asks what structural features the four cases share, where they diverge, and what those convergences and divergences reveal about the relationship between transgression and soteriology in each system. Where the cases converge, the convergence suggests something about the logic of religious transgression as such. Where they diverge — and the divergences are as illuminating as the convergences — the divergence tracks specific doctrinal commitments about the nature of moral law, the structure of liberation, and the relationship between conventional form and ultimate reality.

The article proceeds in three movements. The first establishes the comparative morphology of transgressive sanctity: who these figures are, what they do, and what structural functions their transgression serves across traditions. The second examines the experiential territory that transgressive practice points toward — the phenomenology of non-dual awareness that Buddhist doctrine identifies as both the ground and the goal of antinomian behaviour — and asks whether this territory can be examined seriously outside a purely religious framework. The third movement examines language itself: the *Heart Sutra’s* 般若波羅蜜多心經 central equation 色即是空 空即是色 as philosophical backbone, the problem of translating the untranslatable across languages and traditions, and finally the mantra — the post-propositional close of the *Heart Sutra* — as the point at which language acknowledges its own limit and surrenders into sound.

-----

### 1.3 Roadmap

The argument moves deliberately inward. It begins at the social surface — the spectacle of the transgressive saint as observed from outside — and moves progressively toward the interior: first the doctrinal logic that makes transgression coherent, then the experiential territory that doctrine points toward, then the question of whether language can capture that territory, then the discovery that sound may operate where language cannot.

This inward movement is not incidental to the article’s argument. It enacts the same trajectory that the traditions under examination describe: from the observable form of the practice toward the formless reality the practice approaches. The reader who follows the argument to its end will find themselves at a boundary — the boundary where propositional knowing reaches its limit and something else, whether we call it direct experience, sonic resonance, or awakened mind, begins.

What lies beyond that boundary cannot be argued. It can, perhaps, be pointed to. That is what the transgressive saints, the untranslatable equations, and the post-propositional mantras are all, in their different registers, attempting to do. This article attempts the more modest task of mapping the pointing — tracing, with the tools available to academic inquiry, the shape of a gesture that those tools cannot themselves complete.

## Section 2: Four Figures, Four Traditions

### 2.1 濟公 Jìgōng — The Drunken Monk of Hangzhou

The historical figure behind the legend was **Li Xiuyuan** 李修緣, born around 1130 in Tiantai 天台, Zhejiang, into a family with deep connections to Buddhist patronage. Ordained at **Lingyin Monastery** 靈隱寺 in Hangzhou under the Chan master Huiyuan 慧遠, he received the dharma name **Daoji** 道濟 — “one who aids through the Way.” He died in 1209. These biographical coordinates are thin, and they were almost immediately overwhelmed by legend. What the tradition preserved and elaborated is not the historical monk but an **icon** — a figure whose every attribute is precisely calibrated to violate monastic expectation.

The canonical Jìgōng wears torn, filthy robes where a monk should be clean and dignified. He carries a cracked fan — an object that cannot perform its function — and a gourd of wine. He eats meat publicly, staggers through marketplaces, sleeps in the open. He is, in the language of the tradition, *diankuang* 癲狂 — holy madness, a compound of *dian* (disorder, inversion) and *kuang* (wildness, frenzy). The term is not pejorative within the tradition. It names a recognised mode of spiritual operation, one in which the ordinary relationship between inner realisation and outward form has been deliberately, purposefully inverted.

By the Ming and Qing periods, Jìgōng’s legend had been assembled into the sprawling 240-chapter novel *濟公全傳* (*Complete Biography of Jìgōng*), in which he moves through Southern Song society as a trickster-bodhisattva, consistently intervening on behalf of the poor and powerless against corrupt officials and wealthy oppressors. This populist dimension is inseparable from his religious significance: his transgression of monastic norms and his transgression of class hierarchy are understood as expressions of the same underlying freedom. The monk who will not be confined by the Vinaya is the same figure who will not be intimidated by the magistrate.

His cult has never been confined to monastic Buddhism. Throughout the Sinophone world, and with particular intensity in Taiwan, Jìgōng is worshipped through **spirit-medium** (*jītóng* 乩童) practice — possessed mediums through whom he dispenses oracles, diagnoses illness, and resolves disputes. He belongs simultaneously to Chan Buddhism, Daoist folk religion, and popular devotional culture, answering fully to none, available to all.

-----

### 2.2 Drukpa Künley — The Divine Madman of Bhutan

**Drukpa Künley** (*’Brug pa Kun legs*, 1455–1529) was born into the Gya clan of central Tibet, trained in the **Drukpa Kagyü** school, and received instruction from some of the most accomplished masters of his generation — including Pema Lingpa, the great treasure-revealer. He then spent most of his adult life wandering through Tibet and Bhutan, sleeping outdoors, composing irreverent songs, seducing women of every social class, drinking prodigiously, and heaping scorn on pompous lamas and wealthy patrons alike.

His biography — preserved in the *namtar* 名傳 (liberation biography) genre, which presents a saint’s life as a map of the path to awakening rather than a conventional historical record — is among the most exuberantly transgressive texts in the Buddhist canon. Women number in the hundreds; encounters with demons, rendered in explicit comic detail, end with their subjugation and conversion. His primary instrument of both pleasure and liberation is described, without euphemism, as the “flaming thunderbolt of wisdom” — the *vajra* 金剛 of Tantric iconography made literal, the penis as ritual implement.

This is not mere ribaldry. It is the **Tantric logic of transformation** (*sgyur ba*) in its most concentrated form. Where the Vinaya tradition asks practitioners to renounce sexual desire as an obstacle to liberation, the Vajrayāna tradition holds that defilements (*kleśa* 煩惱) can, in the hands of an accomplished practitioner, be transformed into the path itself. The “poison as medicine” principle (*dug las sman sgrub*) does not invite ordinary practitioners to indulge their desires — it describes what is possible for someone who has, through rigorous practice, dissolved the ego-grasping that makes desire binding. Drukpa Künley’s transgressions are therefore not despite his realisation but **demonstrations** of it: only someone genuinely free from attachment can engage desire without being captured by it.

His presence in Bhutan remains vivid and immediate. The painted phalluses that appear on the walls of houses throughout the country — a tradition credited to his blessing — are not obscene decorations but **protective symbols**, wards against evil and invitations to fertility. He is the country’s most beloved saint, a figure whose irreverence toward institutional religion paradoxically secured his place at the centre of popular devotion.

-----

### 2.3 Ikkyū Sōjun 一休宗純 — The Crazy Cloud

**Ikkyū Sōjun** (1394–1481) was born, according to tradition, to a court lady who may have been a consort of Emperor Go-Komatsu — a claim that gave him a proximity to imperial legitimacy he deployed with characteristic irony throughout his life. Ordained young, he trained under several masters before receiving **formal dharma transmission** (*inka shōmei* 印可証明) from the Rinzai master Kasō Sōdon — and then, by his own account, discarding or burning the certificate, refusing the institutional authority it conferred.

The gesture was not anti-Buddhist. It was a precise diagnosis. By the Muromachi period, the *inka* system had become entangled with **temple patronage networks**, aristocratic sponsorship, and succession politics. Certificates of enlightenment were being sold. Lineages were being fabricated. The sign of awakening had become entirely detached from its referent — a commodity circulating in a market that had nothing to do with liberation. Ikkyū’s refusal to keep his certificate was the opening move in a sustained, lifelong polemic against this deformation, conducted not through philosophical treatise but through **poetry, behaviour, and spectacle**.

The *Kyōunshū* 狂雲集 — “Crazy Cloud Collection” — comprises roughly one thousand poems in Classical Chinese (*kanbun* 漢文), composed across his adult life. The collection’s self-designation as “crazy cloud” (狂雲 *kyōun*) is already meta-commentary: the cloud that cannot be grasped, that leaves no trace, that moves without institutional permission — *crazy* because it operates entirely outside the coordinates of respectable Chan discourse. The most explicitly erotic poems, many addressed to the blind singer **Mori** 森 with whom he spent his final decades, deploy the language of meditation (*samādhi* 三昧), original face (*honrai no menmoku* 本來の面目), and awakening alongside explicit sexual description — not as metaphor but as **doctrinal identity**, the erotic and the awakened occupying the same register simultaneously.

The collection also contains meditations on skeletons, impermanence, and death — and the two sequences, erotic and *memento mori*, are not in tension. Together they perform the *Heart Sutra’s* central equation from opposite directions: the beautiful body is already empty; the empty body is fully, completely alive.

-----

### 2.4 The *Yurodivye* — Holy Fools of Orthodox Christianity

The Eastern Orthodox tradition of **юродство** (*yurodstvo*, “foolishness for Christ’s sake”) emerges from the Byzantine *salos* tradition — most fully exemplified by St. Symeon of Emesa (6th century, Syria), described by Leontius of Neapolis as a man who dragged a dead dog through the city gates, overturned market stalls, and threw nuts at the congregation during the liturgy, while secretly fasting, praying through the night, and performing quiet miracles for those in genuine need.

The Russian tradition, which developed from the tenth century onward and reached its fullest expression between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries, produced figures of extraordinary cultural force: **St. Basil the Blessed** (Василий Блаженный, c.1469–1552), who rebuked Ivan the Terrible to his face and is buried in the cathedral that bears his name beside the Kremlin; **St. Xenia of Petersburg** (Ксения Петербургская, c.1730–c.1803), who after her husband’s death gave away her possessions, dressed in his clothes, answered only to his name, and wandered the city for decades. The *yurodivye* typically practised nakedness in public, exposure to cold, deliberate self-humiliation, and the performance of apparent insanity — raving, nonsensical speech, shocking behaviour.

The theological grounding is explicitly **Pauline**: *“We are fools for Christ’s sake”* (μωροὶ διὰ Χριστόν, 1 Cor. 4:10), and more broadly the Pauline paradox that divine wisdom appears as foolishness to the world (1 Cor. 1:18–25). The *yurodivyi* performs the kenotic emptying (*κένωσις*, Phil. 2:7) of social selfhood — stripping away every mark of status, dignity, and legibility in imitation of Christ’s self-emptying.

The critical structural distinction from the Buddhist cases emerges here with precision: the *yurodivyi* does not, in the typical case, actually violate the moral law. His transgression is **semiotic rather than actual** — he appears to transgress, performs madness and indecency, but his underlying ascetic practice is ferociously rigorous. St. Basil’s nakedness is not sexual licence; his raving is not blasphemy. What is transgressed is the **social legibility** of holiness — the expectation that sanctity presents itself in recognisable, institutionally approved forms. The audience misreads holiness as madness, and that misreading is itself the teaching: it reveals the poverty of their perceptual framework, not the saint’s moral standing.

This distinction — between transgression that actually violates conventional norms (Buddhist) and transgression that performs apparent violation while concealing rigorous observance (Christian) — is not a minor technical difference. It tracks a fundamental divergence in how each tradition understands the relationship between the moral law and the path to liberation, and it will become the structural axis around which the comparative analysis of the following sections turns.

## Section 3: The Structural Logic of Transgressive Sanctity

The four figures introduced in Section 2 are not simply eccentric individuals whose personal psychology happened to express itself through violation of religious norms. Each operates within a tradition that has — however reluctantly, however retrospectively — **recognised their transgression as soteriologically significant**: as pointing toward, demonstrating, or participating in the very liberation the tradition exists to transmit. This recognition demands explanation. Before asking what doctrinal frameworks make transgression coherent (Section 4), we must ask what **functions** transgression performs — what it does to the audience, to the institution, and to the tradition’s self-understanding — that earns it this recognition despite the obvious scandal it produces.

Four structural functions emerge from the comparative analysis. The first two are shared across all four cases, though with important variation in emphasis and target. The third reveals the deepest divergence in the typology. The fourth is a structural irony that every case instantiates, and that raises the sharpest questions about what transgressive sanctity ultimately accomplishes.

-----

### 3.1 Epistemological Rupture — Jamming the Conceptual Processor

The most fundamental function of transgressive sanctity, visible across all four traditions, is the production of a specific kind of **cognitive failure** in the observer.

The Chan and Rinzai traditions provide the most explicit theoretical account of why this failure is valuable. From the Tang masters onward, Linji-school 臨濟宗 pedagogy had developed a repertoire of techniques designed to disrupt what Buddhist psychology calls *fenbie* 分別 — discriminating cognition, the mind’s habitual activity of categorising, labelling, comparing, and judging. This discriminating activity is not merely an intellectual habit. It is, in the Buddhist analysis, the mechanism through which the constructed self perpetuates itself: by dividing experience into subject and object, by filing every encounter under a known category, by maintaining the boundary between self and world, between sacred and profane, between the monk who enlightens and the student who is enlightened. Linji Yixuan’s 臨濟義玄 hitting and shouting, Yunmen Wenyan’s 雲門文偃 cryptic monosyllables, Zhaozhou’s 趙州 “Mu” 無 — all operate by presenting the student’s discriminating mind with something it **cannot process**: something that refuses to fit any available category, that jams the filing system before it can operate.

Transgressive sanctity extends this pedagogy from the encounter between master and student to the encounter between saint and society. When Jìgōng staggers through Hangzhou eating meat and drinking wine, the observer’s mind reaches for a category — monk, drunkard, madman, bodhisattva — and finds that none fits without remainder. The categories are mutually exclusive, and the figure occupies all of them simultaneously. When Ikkyū publishes erotic poetry under his own name as a certified Zen master, the reader cannot file the poems under “dharma literature” (too obscene) or “erotic verse” (too obviously doctrinal). The cognitive dissonance cannot be resolved by choosing one category over the other, because both are simultaneously, demonstrably accurate.

This failure — this moment before re-categorisation, when the discriminating processor stalls — is, in the Buddhist account, precisely the gap through which direct seeing (*jianxing* 見性) becomes possible. The transgression does not cause awakening. It creates a **momentary opening** in the conceptual armour that ordinarily prevents it. The saint’s body is the kōan 公案 made flesh.

The *yurodivyi* operates by the same mechanism, though the theological framing differs. St. Basil’s nakedness in the Moscow winter produces the same cognitive stall in the observer: the categories “holy man” and “naked madman in the snow” are mutually exclusive in the observer’s framework, and yet both apply. The Pauline warrant makes the disruption explicit: divine wisdom *is* foolishness to the world (1 Cor. 1:18–25). The *yurodivyi’s* performance is designed to make this Pauline paradox **viscerally experienced** rather than merely intellectually acknowledged — to produce in the observer a felt encounter with the inadequacy of their own perceptual framework.

-----

### 3.2 Social Critique Through Embodiment

The transgressive saint’s body is not only an epistemological instrument. It is simultaneously a **political one** — a medium through which specific, historically situated critiques of religious and social power are conducted.

Each of our four figures directs their transgression at a specific institutional target, and the target is consistently the **complicity of organised religion with existing power structures**. Jìgōng’s alignment with the poor of Hangzhou against corrupt officials and wealthy merchants is inseparable from his violation of monastic propriety: the monk who refuses the respectability of clean robes and sober conduct is the same figure who refuses the class alignment that respectable monasticism had come to represent. His *diankuang* 癲狂 is simultaneously spiritual freedom and social solidarity — the two are, within the logic of his tradition, the same gesture.

Drukpa Künley’s scorn is directed with particular precision at the **institutionalised lamaism** of fifteenth-century Tibet — at the wealthy, politically connected monasteries, the lamas who accepted aristocratic patronage and delivered political legitimation in return, the entire apparatus of religious authority that had made Tibetan Buddhism inseparable from the structures of feudal power. His wandering poverty, his contempt for deference, his willingness to mock the most revered figures of his generation — these are not failures of social competence but **refusals of a specific social contract** between religion and power.

Ikkyū’s target is the most precisely identified: the *inka* 印可 system, the dharma transmission certificates that had become commodities, and the Rinzai establishment — particularly the **Daitokuji** 大徳寺 lineage — that had built its institutional prestige on their circulation. His transgression is calibrated to be **uncertifiable**: a scandalous poem attributed to a man publicly visiting blind singers cannot be issued an *inka* certificate, cannot be incorporated into the lineage economy, cannot be sold. The explicitness is strategic. It produces a form of dharma expression that the corrupted institution literally cannot absorb.

The *yurodivye* exercised a form of **prophetic speech** (*parrēsia*, παρρησία — fearless truth-telling) that operated under the specific protection that apparent madness afforded. St. Basil’s denunciation of Ivan the Terrible was possible precisely because a naked madman cannot be charged with sedition in the way a bishop can. The performance of insanity created a zone of **licensed transgression** — a space in which power could be addressed directly, without the usual protocols of deference, because the speaker had placed themselves outside the social order that those protocols maintained. The tsar who executed a bishop for similar speech could not execute a fool without confirming the fool’s diagnosis of his tyranny.

-----

### 3.3 The Critical Divergence: Real Versus Performed Transgression

The two structural functions identified above are shared, with variation, across all four traditions. The third function — or rather, the third question — reveals the **deepest divergence** in the comparative typology, and it tracks the underlying soteriology of each tradition with precision.

The divergence is simple to state: in the Buddhist cases, the transgression is **actual**. Jìgōng drinks wine. Drukpa Künley has sex. Ikkyū visits brothels. The Vinaya is genuinely violated; the conventional moral code is genuinely breached. The doctrinal framework that makes this coherent — the two-truths doctrine, the Tantric transformation logic — is precisely a framework for making **actual transgression** soteriologically legible, not for explaining away the transgression as merely apparent.

In the Christian case, the transgression is typically **performed and apparent rather than actual**. The *yurodivyi* appears to transgress — appears mad, appears indecent, appears to violate the social codes that in Christian ethics are grounded in moral law — but the underlying reality is, in most documented cases, ferocious asceticism and rigorous moral observance. St. Basil is not actually licentious; his apparent licentiousness is a costume worn over extreme self-denial. St. Xenia does not abandon Christian ethics when she abandons her name and social identity; she intensifies them.

This distinction is not a minor technical matter. It reflects a fundamental difference in how the two traditions understand **the relationship between the moral law and the path to liberation**. For the Madhyamaka Buddhist, the moral law is conventionally real but ultimately empty — it has no inherent, independent existence, and therefore its violation, by someone with the appropriate realisation, can serve as a demonstration of its ultimate nature. For Orthodox Christianity, the moral law is not merely conventional — it reflects the nature of God and of the human person made in God’s image. It cannot be transcended by realisation; it can only be fulfilled, deepened, or hidden under an apparently transgressive surface.

The *yurodivyi’s* transgression is therefore better understood as a **hermeneutic trap** than as an actual moral breach. It confronts the observer with an apparent scandal that, on closer inspection, reveals itself as extreme holiness in disguise — which indicts not the saint’s moral standing but the observer’s perceptual framework. The Buddhist transgressive saint says: *look, the precept is empty.* The Christian holy fool says: *look, your idea of what holiness looks like is inadequate.* Both disrupt the observer’s framework. The disruption operates at different levels of the tradition’s own normative structure.

-----

### 3.4 The Paradox of Institutionalisation

There is a final structural irony that every case in the typology instantiates, and that raises uncomfortable questions about what transgressive sanctity ultimately accomplishes.

Every figure examined in this article — the monk expelled from his monastery, the wandering yogi who mocked all lamas, the Zen master who burned his certificate, the naked prophet who insulted the tsar — has been **absorbed, enshrined, and institutionalised** by the tradition their transgression challenged. Jìgōng has a temple. Drukpa Künley’s lineage produced a recognised tulku succession. Ikkyū became abbot of Daitokuji — the very institution whose corruption he had spent his life denouncing. The *yurodivye* were canonised by the Holy Synod. Their images appear on icons, their relics in reliquaries, their biographies in hagiographic collections produced by the institutional apparatus they transgressed.

This absorption is not hypocrisy, or not only hypocrisy. It reflects the institution’s sophisticated recognition that transgressive figures, precisely by performing the tradition’s deepest claims rather than merely administering them, **renew the institution’s legitimating narrative**. The monastery that can point to Jìgōng demonstrates that awakening exceeds its own procedures. The Church that canonises St. Basil demonstrates that the Spirit blows where it will, beyond hierarchical control. The transgressive saint becomes, in death, the institution’s most powerful argument for its own vitality — proof that what the institution transmits is real enough to overflow the containers it has built for it.

The paradox is complete: the figure who transgressed the institution to demonstrate the institution’s inadequacy becomes, through canonisation, the institution’s most valuable asset. Whether this represents the tradition’s deepest wisdom — its ability to recognise and incorporate its own excess — or its most sophisticated mechanism of self-defence against genuine critique is a question that the comparative analysis alone cannot answer. It requires exactly the kind of critical attention to power and institutionalisation that the following sections will continue to hold in view.

## Section 4: Doctrinal Foundations — Why Transgression Can Be Soteriological

The structural functions identified in Section 3 — epistemological rupture, social critique, the divergence between real and performed transgression, the paradox of institutionalisation — describe *what* transgressive sanctity does. They do not yet explain *why* specific traditions recognise these functions as soteriologically legitimate rather than simply dismissing the figures involved as moral failures. That recognition requires a philosophical framework — a coherent account of the relationship between conventional form and ultimate reality that makes it possible to see the violation of norms as, under specific conditions, a valid expression of the tradition’s deepest truth rather than its negation.

This section excavates three such frameworks: the **two-truths doctrine** that grounds Buddhist antinomianism broadly, the **Tantric transformation logic** specific to the Vajrayāna cases, and the **institutional polemic** of the *Kyōunshū* as a case where doctrinal argument and literary practice become inseparable. Each framework is internally coherent. Each generates a different type of transgressive sanctity. And understanding their differences is essential for understanding why Jìgōng, Drukpa Künley, and Ikkyū — superficially similar figures — are engaged in philosophically distinct projects.

-----

### 4.1 The Two-Truths Doctrine and the Emptiness of Precepts

The philosophical foundation for Buddhist antinomianism is the **Madhyamaka doctrine of two truths** (*satyadvaya* 二諦), developed most rigorously by **Nāgārjuna** (c. 2nd century CE) in the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* 中論 and transmitted into East Asian Buddhism through **Kumārajīva’s** 鳩摩羅什 translations and the subsequent Sanlun 三論 and Tiantai 天台 schools.

The doctrine distinguishes between two levels of truth that are neither identical nor separable:

**Conventional truth** (*saṃvṛti-satya* 俗諦) is the level at which ordinary experience, language, and ethics operate. Tables exist. Persons exist. The precept against drinking wine exists. These are not fictions — they have genuine conventional reality and genuine practical consequences. To ignore conventional truth in the name of ultimate truth is not wisdom but confusion, and the Madhyamaka tradition is emphatic on this point.

**Ultimate truth** (*paramārtha-satya* 真諦) is the level at which no phenomenon possesses inherent, independent, self-sufficient existence — *svabhāva* 自性. Every phenomenon, examined carefully, is found to be dependently arisen (*pratītyasamutpāda* 緣起): constituted entirely by its relationships to other phenomena, with no core essence that exists independently. This includes the precepts themselves. The precept against drinking wine has no inherent existence — it arises in dependence on conditions: the Vinaya’s institutional history, the communities that transmit it, the conceptual frameworks that make it legible as a rule.

The critical move: if the precepts are ultimately empty of inherent existence — if their conventional reality is real but their ultimate status is like everything else, empty — then the *reason* for observing them cannot be that they are intrinsically, unconditionally binding. Their conventional value is real: for an ordinary practitioner, observing the precepts creates the conditions for mental clarity and ethical sensitivity that make progress on the path possible. But for someone who has **directly recognised emptiness** — who perceives, without merely believing, that no phenomenon has inherent existence — the precept’s conventional authority cannot be the final word.

This is the doctrinal opening through which Jìgōng passes. The couplet attributed to him —

> 酒肉穿腸過，佛祖心中坐
> *Wine and meat pass through the gut; the Buddha sits enthroned in the heart*

— is not a licence for ordinary practitioners to drink. It is a claim about a specific mode of perception: one in which the distinction between pure and impure, permitted and forbidden, has been recognised as conventionally real but ultimately empty. From within that recognition, the wine is just wine — neither defiling nor liberating in itself. The defilement was always a mental construction, not a property of the liquid.

What is crucial — and what popular reception often misses — is that the Madhyamaka framework does not collapse conventional truth into ultimate truth. The two-truths doctrine is not **monism**: it does not say that ultimately everything is the same and therefore distinctions don’t matter. Nāgārjuna insists explicitly that the ultimate truth can only be approached through conventional truth, not by bypassing it. The transgressive saint who has genuinely realised emptiness does not experience the precepts as irrelevant — they experience them as **conventionally real and ultimately empty simultaneously**, which is an entirely different cognitive situation from simply deciding that rules don’t apply to oneself. The latter is not realisation but rationalization — a distinction the tradition marks with care, even if popular hagiography sometimes blurs it.

-----

### 4.2 Tantric Transformation — Poison as Medicine

Drukpa Künley operates within a different philosophical framework — one that shares the Madhyamaka foundation but builds a distinct soteriological architecture upon it. The Vajrayāna tradition does not merely argue that defilements (*kleśa* 煩惱) are ultimately empty and therefore can be transgressed by the realised practitioner. It argues that defilements can be **actively used as the path** — transformed, through specific practices and a specific quality of awareness, from obstacles into fuel.

The operative principle is *dug las sman sgrub* — “accomplishing medicine from poison.” The image is precise: the same substance that destroys an ordinary person, administered by a skilled physician in the right dose and context, becomes curative. Desire, anger, pride, ignorance — the very mental factors that Buddhist ethics and psychology identify as the roots of suffering — are, in the Tantric framework, not to be renounced but **transformed**. Their energy, which in the ordinary mind perpetuates bondage, can in the accomplished practitioner’s mind be redirected toward liberation.

The theoretical grounding is the Vajrayāna claim that **the nature of the defilements is, from the beginning, identical to wisdom**. Desire, at its root, is not other than the discriminating wisdom (*pratyavekṣaṇā-jñāna* 妙觀察智) that perceives phenomena in their particularity. Anger is not other than mirror-like wisdom (*ādarśa-jñāna* 大圓鏡智) that reflects reality without distortion. The defilement and the wisdom are the same energy, the same cognitive event, perceived differently — through the filter of ego-grasping or without it. The practitioner who has dissolved ego-grasping through Tantric practice does not experience desire as a defilement but as wisdom’s self-expression.

This is why the *mahāsiddha* 大成就者 tradition — the eighty-four accomplished masters whose liberation biographies (*Caturāśīti-siddha-pravṛtti*) constitute the Vajrayāna’s founding hagiographic corpus — is populated with figures who achieve liberation through precisely the activities that Vinaya Buddhism prohibits. Saraha the arrow-maker, Virūpa the wine-drinker, Tilopa the sesame-pounder: each achieves awakening through engagement with the material world in its most immediate, sensory, transgressive dimensions. The *dohā* realisation songs they composed — ecstatic, erotic, antinomian — are the literary expression of a **soteriology of transformation** rather than renunciation.

Drukpa Künley stands in this lineage directly. His sexual encounters are not lapses from the path but **enactments of it** — or so the tradition claims, with the crucial proviso that this is only possible for someone who has, through prior practice, dissolved the ego-grasping that makes desire binding. The *namtar* is not inviting readers to imitate his behaviour. It is demonstrating, through the narrative of his life, that liberation is possible even — especially — through the most apparently unpromising material.

The structural difference from the two-truths framework is sharp: Jìgōng’s transgression **demonstrates** that the precepts are ultimately empty. Drukpa Künley’s transgression **transforms** defilement into wisdom. The first is epistemological; the second is alchemical. Both require genuine realisation as their precondition, but they describe that realisation doing different things.

-----

### 4.3 The *Kyōunshū* as Doctrinal Polemic

Ikkyū inherits both frameworks — the Madhyamaka two-truths doctrine and, through Shingon 真言 and Tendai 天台 *mikkyō* 密教 currents available in Japanese Buddhist culture, something of the Tantric transformation logic — but deploys them for a purpose neither Jìgōng’s hagiography nor Drukpa Künley’s *namtar* required: **sustained institutional critique conducted through literary form**.

The *Kyōunshū* is not merely a collection of transgressive poems that happen to have doctrinal content. It is a **unified argumentative project** whose literary choices are inseparable from its philosophical claims. To understand this, it is necessary to hold together three dimensions that are typically examined separately: the institutional target, the doctrinal argument, and the formal strategy.

The **institutional target** is specific: the *inka shōmei* 印可証明 system as it had deformed by the Muromachi period. Ikkyū’s teacher Kasō had given him certification of awakening — and Ikkyū’s refusal to keep the certificate was not anti-Buddhist but **diagnostically precise**: the institution had made the sign of awakening into a tradeable commodity, which meant the sign had become entirely detached from its referent. An *inka* certificate in this economy was evidence not of realisation but of successful navigation of a patronage network. The entire institutional apparatus of dharma transmission had become a machine for producing and distributing this counterfeit currency.

The **doctrinal argument** against this deformation is, at its core, an application of the two-truths doctrine: if the mark of awakening is conventionally real but ultimately empty, then **no external form can guarantee its presence**. A certificate cannot certify what is beyond certification. An institution cannot authorise what exceeds institutional authority. The attempt to capture, certify, and transmit awakening through documents and lineage succession is not merely inadequate — it is a precise inversion of what the tradition actually transmits. The *Heart Sutra* 般若波羅蜜多心經 ends with a mantra precisely because what it points to cannot be propositionally captured; the *inka* system’s pretension to capture it in a document is, from this perspective, a form of fundamental doctrinal error.

The **formal strategy** is where the *Kyōunshū* becomes irreplaceable. Ikkyū does not make this argument in a philosophical treatise. He makes it in poems — and specifically in poems whose transgressive content is calibrated to be **institutionally unabsorbable**. The logic is precise: if awakening cannot be certified, then the demonstration of awakening must take a form that the certification apparatus cannot process. An explicit description of sexual union, written by a certified Zen master in Classical Chinese verse of genuine technical accomplishment, deploying the language of *samādhi* 三昧 and original face 本來の面目 without embarrassment or irony — this is a form of dharma expression that the *inka* economy literally has no procedure for handling. It cannot be certified. It cannot be incorporated into the lineage. It cannot be sold.

The erotic poem and the skeleton poem together — the *Kyōunshū’s* two great sequences — perform 色即是空 空即是色 not as a philosophical proposition but as a **literary enactment**: the beautiful, desiring body is already empty (the skeletons); the empty, mortal body is fully, unreservedly alive (the erotic poems). Neither sequence makes sense without the other. Together they constitute the single argument that the institutional corruption Ikkyū diagnosed had made it impossible to make in any other form: that what the tradition transmits is not a document, not a lineage, not a certificate — but a direct seeing of what form and emptiness are, available in a blind woman’s face as fully as in the meditation hall, irreducible to any institutional procedure, impossible to sell.

## Section 5: Non-Dual Experience — The Territory Transgression Points Toward

The doctrinal frameworks examined in Section 4 are not, in the first instance, philosophical positions adopted for their theoretical elegance. They are attempts to account for something — to provide a coherent description of an experiential territory that practitioners across traditions report encountering, and that the transgressive figures of Sections 2 and 3 are understood to inhabit permanently. The two-truths doctrine does not generate the transgressive saint by logical necessity; it provides the conceptual vocabulary for making coherent what certain practitioners have already discovered in practice. Before the doctrine, there is the experience. Before the *Kyōunshū*, there is whatever Ikkyū found in Mori’s presence that the institution’s procedures could not reach.

This section asks: what is that experiential territory? What do the traditions claim actually happens in the moments — whether in meditation, in erotic encounter, in aesthetic absorption, or in the sudden cognitive rupture produced by a kōan or a transgressive saint — that the doctrinal frameworks are attempting to describe? And can this territory be examined seriously, with philosophical and empirical rigour, outside the religious framework that first mapped it?

The answer, this section will argue, is: partially. The secular frameworks — Western phenomenology, cognitive science, neuroscience — can map significant features of this territory with genuine precision and illuminating convergence with Buddhist accounts. But each reaches a boundary at which its own methods become insufficient, and beyond which the Buddhist tradition’s first-person phenomenological claims remain irreplaceable as descriptions of what the experience is from inside.

-----

### 5.1 The Buddhist Phenomenological Account

Buddhist analysis begins not with extraordinary states but with the **structure of ordinary experience** — and finds, on close examination, that what feels most natural and self-evident about ordinary experience is in fact a complex, active construction.

Every moment of ordinary waking experience, in the Buddhist analysis, involves the generation of a **subject** — the sense of “I” who is having this experience — and an **object** — the thing being experienced — and a **relationship** between them: perceiving, wanting, avoiding, judging, comparing. This tripartite structure feels simply given, simply the way experience is. Buddhism’s fundamental claim is that it is not given but **produced**: assembled, moment by moment, by mental processes that operate largely below the threshold of awareness. The subject is not a fixed entity that pre-exists experience and then has it. The subject is generated *within* each moment of experience — and immediately feels as though it was always already there.

The school that develops this analysis most technically is **Yogācāra** 唯識, whose eight-consciousness model locates the root of subject-generation in *manas* 末那識 — the seventh consciousness, whose specific function is **constant self-referential grasping**: the ceaseless attribution of selfhood to the *ālayavijñāna* 阿賴耶識 (storehouse consciousness), producing the continuous, background sense of “I am” that underlies all specific experiences. This is not a metaphysical claim about the existence of a self. It is a phenomenological claim about a **functional process** — a habit of mental construction so ingrained and so rapid that it is normally invisible.

Non-dual experience, within this framework, is what occurs when this construction **temporarily ceases** — when *manas* stops its ceaseless self-attribution and the tripartite subject-object-relationship structure is not generated. What remains is not nothing, and not blankness. The traditions are unanimous and emphatic: what remains is **awareness itself**, but awareness without the usual overlay of self-referential construction. The Yogācāra calls this *āmalajñāna* 阿摩羅識 — pure, undefiled consciousness. The Madhyamaka frames it differently: not a special consciousness but the **recognition of emptiness** as the nature of the subject-object structure itself — seeing, directly, that the boundary was always constructed, never ultimately real.

Chan and Rinzai Zen tend to resist both formulations as potentially generating new objects of attachment — new things to seek, new states to achieve — and point instead with deliberate bluntness: *this*, right now, before you add interpretation. The term *jianxing* 見性 — “seeing nature” — names not an achievement but a **recognition**: nothing new is acquired. Something that was always obscuring is, for a moment, not obscuring.

The spectrum of states the traditions describe ranges from **momentary glimpses** (*kenshō* 見性 in Japanese Rinzai terminology) through sustained meditative absorptions (*samādhi* 三昧) to what the Pali tradition calls *nirodha-samāpatti* 滅盡定 — the “attainment of cessation,” in which even the subtlest forms of conceptual processing have temporarily stilled. What is crucial for understanding the transgressive saints is the tradition’s insistence that the glimpse is not confined to the meditation hall. Jìgōng in the marketplace, Ikkyū with Mori, Drukpa Künley in the mountains — all are understood to inhabit this recognition continuously, in conditions that formal meditation practice has not traditionally sanctioned. The transgressive saint’s freedom from the institution is, at this level, a freedom from the **location-dependence of awakening** — the assumption that liberation is available only in approved places, through approved procedures.

-----

### 5.2 Secular Convergences — Phenomenology and Cognitive Science

Western philosophy arrived at strikingly similar descriptions of subject-object dissolution through entirely independent routes, beginning in the early twentieth century with the phenomenological tradition.

**Edmund Husserl’s** epoché — the methodological “bracketing” of the natural attitude — revealed, in the space opened by that bracketing, the **intentional structure** of experience: consciousness is always consciousness *of* something, always relational, never a self-contained substance that then reaches out to touch the world. The subject-object structure is not given; it is constituted.

**Martin Heidegger** went further in a different direction. In the mood of **anxiety** (*Angst*), the familiar world of ready-to-hand objects withdraws, significance drains from the usual projects, and something more primordial becomes available — **Being itself**, prior to the subject-object articulation that ordinarily structures experience. The structural parallel with Buddhist accounts is precise: a rupture in the ordinary subject-object construction through which something more fundamental briefly shows.

**Maurice Merleau-Ponty’s** phenomenology of perception approaches the same territory from the body. His central argument is that the subject-object structure of ordinary reflective experience is a **secondary abstraction** from something more primordial: the body’s pre-reflective engagement with the world, which is already non-dual. Merleau-Ponty’s account illuminates Ikkyū’s implicit claim about erotic experience: not as transgression but as **return to the body’s native non-duality**.

Contemporary cognitive science has arrived at convergent conclusions. **Thomas Metzinger’s** neurophilosophical account argues that what we call the self is a **phenomenal self-model** — a representation the brain generates of itself, mistaken for a real entity. This is, in different vocabulary, the Buddhist claim about *manas*: the self-sense is a functional process, not a substance. **Karl Friston’s** predictive processing framework extends this: the self is a particularly high-level predictive model — not a thing but a process, not an entity but a useful fiction. When the predictive model fails — when something occurs that it cannot account for — there is a moment of **open processing** before the model updates. This moment is, structurally, what Chan shock pedagogy is designed to produce.

-----

### 5.3 The Neuroscience of Ego Dissolution — Correlates and Their Limits

Neuroimaging research has identified specific brain systems whose activity correlates with the subject-object construction that Buddhist phenomenology describes — and whose **deactivation** correlates with reports of non-dual awareness.

The **default mode network** (DMN) — active during self-referential processing — shows substantially decreased activity in experienced meditators during deep meditative states. Critically, similar suppression has been observed in flow states, in deep aesthetic absorption, in the peak of sexual arousal, and in participants who have ingested psilocybin — a pharmacological agent that reliably produces reports of ego dissolution and non-dual awareness. The **posterior superior parietal lobe** — responsible for constructing the body’s spatial boundary — shows decreased activity in meditators at peak states, corresponding to reports of boundary dissolution.

These findings establish that **something physiologically real and consistent** occurs in the states the traditions describe. The convergence across induction conditions is particularly significant: it suggests that these states are not artefacts of any specific cultural practice but reflect a more general feature of human neurological architecture.

The limit of the neuroscientific account, however, is the **hard problem of consciousness**: the question of why there is subjective experience at all, why information processing is accompanied by the feel of being. Saying that non-dual experience involves DMN suppression is a third-person description of what the brain does *while* the experience occurs. It does not — and cannot, within the neuroscientific framework alone — tell us what the experience is from inside. Whether the neural correlates are the whole story, or whether they are the instrument through which something the neuroscientific framework cannot capture becomes available, is a question that requires the first-person phenomenological resources that the Buddhist tradition has spent centuries developing.

-----

## Section 6: 色即是空 — Form, Emptiness, and the Performative Poem

The preceding sections have traced a consistent movement: from the social spectacle of transgressive sanctity, through the doctrinal frameworks that make it coherent, toward the experiential territory those frameworks attempt to describe. At each stage, something has resisted full capture — a remainder that the analytical vocabulary identified but could not contain. The structural analysis could map the functions of transgression but not the realisation that makes it meaningful. The phenomenological account could describe subject-object dissolution but not what is found in the dissolution. The neuroscience could measure correlates but not resolve the hard problem of what the experience is from inside.

This resistance is not a failure of the analysis. It is a clue about the nature of what is being examined. And the *Heart Sutra* 般若波羅蜜多心經 — the text that provides the philosophical backbone for the entire tradition this article has been traversing — is a text that **knows about this resistance** and is designed around it.

-----

### 6.1 What the *Heart Sutra* Actually Claims

The *Heart Sutra* is 260 Chinese characters. It is the most recited text in East Asian Buddhism, chanted daily in monasteries across China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam for fourteen centuries. Its familiarity is one of the primary obstacles to reading it.

The text’s central movement begins with **Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva** 觀自在菩薩 in the act of practising deep *prajñāpāramitā* 般若波羅蜜多 and *seeing*: 照見五蘊皆空, “clearly seeing that all five aggregates are empty.” What is perceived is the equation:

> 色不異空，空不異色，色即是空，空即是色
> *Form is not other than emptiness; emptiness is not other than form; form is precisely emptiness; emptiness is precisely form.*

The fourfold structure is not redundant. The first two clauses establish **non-difference**: form and emptiness are not two separate things that happen to be related. The second two clauses assert **immediate identity**: not merely related but the same. The critical word is 即 *jí* — rendered in most English translations as “is,” which loses its force entirely. 即 carries temporal immediacy: *right now, directly, at this very moment*. It insists that the identity is live and present, not a matter of ultimate perspective that transcends ordinary experience.

Two persistent misreadings must be cleared. The first is **nihilism**: emptiness is not the denial of existence but a claim about the **mode of existence** of phenomena — they exist without inherent, independent, self-sufficient nature, constituted entirely by their relationships and conditions. The second is **one-sidedness**: taking only the first direction (色即是空) and ignoring the second (空即是色). 空即是色 insists that emptiness *is* form — that the open, unobstructed nature of reality expresses itself as this specific table, this specific person, this specific moment of encounter. The realisation of emptiness does not dissolve the conventional world. It changes the quality of presence to it.

-----

### 6.2 The Performative Problem

If the equation’s content is as described above, a problem immediately arises that the *Heart Sutra* itself appears to be aware of.

The content claims that **conceptual processing — the discrimination of form from emptiness, the attribution of inherent existence to phenomena — is the fundamental cognitive error** the tradition exists to dissolve. But reading the equation, grasping its propositional content — this is precisely conceptual processing operating at full power. The tool cannot examine itself.

This is not a peripheral puzzle. It is the central problem of the entire Mahayana literary project — and the *Heart Sutra’s* structure is organised around it. The text begins with propositional content and proceeds through an increasingly radical sequence of negations — no eyes, no ears, no mind; no suffering, no origin, no cessation, no path; **no wisdom, no attainment** 無智亦無得 — that systematically dismantle every foothold the reader might attempt to establish, including the foothold of “I understand emptiness.” And then the text abandons propositional language entirely:

> 揭諦揭諦，波羅揭諦，波羅僧揭諦，菩提薩婆訶
> *gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā*

The mantra has no propositional content. It cannot be understood. It can only be **done** — sounded, inhabited, allowed to resonate in the body. The movement from the equation to the mantra is the text enacting its own argument: philosophy reaching its limit and dissolving into sound.

This structure identifies three levels at which the equation can be encountered. **Propositional understanding** grasps the logical structure and adds “form is emptiness” to its conceptual inventory. **Aesthetic encounter** — the experience of the equation landing, of something opening — involves a felt resonance and momentary reduction in the grip of the conceptual processor. **Direct recognition** — *jianxing* 見性 — is the third level, in which the equation is not understood or felt but **seen directly**, without the mediation of the concept.

-----

### 6.3 Poetry as Technology of Non-Dual Encounter

This is where the *Kyōunshū* and the *Heart Sutra* reveal themselves as belonging to the same project — not because Ikkyū is commenting on the sutra, but because both texts are attempting the same impossible task through different instruments.

A logical argument moves from premises to conclusion through inference. Each step invites the reader to evaluate: does this follow? The reader’s discriminating mind is engaged, active, tracking. But a **poem** operates differently, through image, sound, rhythm, juxtaposition, ambiguity, and surprise. These are distinct cognitive instruments that engage the reader through mechanisms that bypass or temporarily suspend the discriminating processor.

**Image** presents rather than argues. **Juxtaposition** places two things beside each other without explaining their relationship. **Ambiguity** refuses the resolution that discriminating mind demands. **Rhythm** entrains attention to a pulse rather than a logical sequence. **Surprise** — and in the *Kyōunshū*, shock — produces a moment of blankness before the categorising machinery re-engages: the gap before re-categorisation, which is precisely the opening that Chan pedagogy has always sought to produce.

When Ikkyū places an explicit description of sexual union beside the language of original face 本來の面目 and *samādhi* 三昧 without metaphorical mediation — asserting not that one *resembles* the other but that they occupy the **same register simultaneously** — the reader’s discriminating mind hits a wall. The cognitive failure this produces is not an obstacle to the poem’s meaning. It **is** the poem’s meaning — the condition in which the poem’s pointing can function, the gap in which what the poem cannot say might briefly show.

The test for whether a poem is genuinely performative rather than merely descriptive is whether it can be **paraphrased without loss**. The *Kyōunshū’s* most extreme poems cannot. Replace the explicit erotic content with a philosophical statement about non-duality and you have not translated the poem — you have destroyed the instrument. The explicitness is not packaging around a separable content. It is the content, because the content is not a proposition but an **experience of cognitive failure** that the proposition about non-duality describes but cannot produce.

The *Heart Sutra* and the *Kyōunshū* are, in this sense, the same gesture at different scales: the sutra performing the equation in the architecture of its own dissolution into mantra; the poem performing it in the architecture of a shock that discriminating mind cannot resolve. Both attempt to produce, in the reader, not understanding but a gap — the gap in which what cannot be said might, briefly, show itself.

-----

## Section 7: Translating the Untranslatable

Every translation is an interpretation. This is a commonplace of translation theory, acknowledged and then typically set aside in the practical business of rendering one language into another. The *Heart Sutra* is a text where this commonplace becomes impossible to set aside — where the act of translation is not merely a practical challenge but a **philosophical one**, because the text’s central claim is precisely that its content exceeds the capacity of propositional language to contain it.

A necessary preliminary: the textual situation is more complicated than the standard narrative suggests. The philologist **Jan Nattier** argued in a landmark 1992 paper that the shorter Chinese *Heart Sutra* attributed to Xuanzang is not a translation from Sanskrit but is itself the **compositional original**, later back-translated into Sanskrit to give it the appearance of Indian authority. If Nattier is correct, the text we call the *Heart Sutra* is a Chinese composition — which means the Sanskrit version that appears to be the original is in fact a translation, and the entire standard account of the text’s transmission requires revision.

-----

### 7.1 Xuanzang’s Chinese — The Native Habitat

**Xuanzang** 玄奘 (602–664) was the most technically precise Buddhist translator in Chinese history. The central equation in his version:

> 色不異空，空不異色，色即是空，空即是色

Consider what Classical Chinese makes structurally available that no other language in this study can replicate.

**Grammatical underdetermination.** Classical Chinese has no grammatical number, no articles, no verb tense, and minimal inflection. The sentence 色即是空 is simultaneously: *form is emptiness / forms are emptiness / this form is emptiness / all form is emptiness / form has always been emptiness / form is right now emptiness*. The grammar **structurally refuses** the determinacy that propositional argument requires. This underdetermination is not a deficiency — it is the instrument.

**The character 色** depicts silk threads being spun — colour, visible surface, the immediate sensory-material world. It carries a **sensuousness** that the standard English rendering “form” almost entirely loses. 色 is felt in the body: it is appetite, desire, visual beauty, the surface of things as they press upon the senses.

**The character 空** depicts a cave or hollow — space as such, the inside of a vessel, the sky one looks up into. Its primary register is **spatial and experiential**: emptiness as openness, as the hollow that makes the vessel useful. 空 gives *śūnyatā* a positive texture that the English “emptiness” consistently fails to convey.

**The connective 即是** carries temporal immediacy: *right here, right now, directly, without mediation*. Together 即是 asserts **live, present, unmediated identity** — not “is ultimately” but *is*, right now, in this moment of encounter, before any interpretive overlay.

**The chiastic sonic structure** — 色不異空 / 空不異色 / 色即是空 / 空即是色 — enacts, in its sound and syntactic form, the reversibility it describes. The ABBA crossing pattern of the chiasm performs the mutual identity: the sentence’s form is the argument’s content.

-----

### 7.2 The Tibetan and Sanskrit Versions

The Tibetan *Heart Sutra* — whose full title, *bcom ldan ‘das ma shes rab kyi pha rol tu phyin pa’i snying po*, renders the text immediately as a **feminine presence** — represents a different set of gains and losses. The central equation:

> གཟུགས་སྟོང་པ་ཉིད་དོ། སྟོང་པ་ཉིད་གཟུགས་སོ།
> *gzugs stong pa nyid do / stong pa nyid gzugs so*

**གཟུགས** (*gzugs*) — form, body, physical shape — has a **somatic concreteness** but lacks the sensory-appetitive charge of 色. **སྟོང་པ་ཉིད** (*stong pa nyid*) — the *-nyid* suffix abstracting *śūnyatā* into “the very quality of being empty” — moves emptiness toward a **technical philosophical property** rather than an experienced texture of openness.

The gain is **philosophical precision**. Tibetan translators worked across generations to standardise technical vocabulary — particularly under the influence of Gelug Madhyamaka epistemology as developed by **Tsongkhapa** (རྗེ་ཙོང་ཁ་པ, 1357–1419). The loss is precisely that richness: the Tibetan *Heart Sutra* arrives pre-contextualised within an interpretive tradition that partially forecloses the Chinese text’s constitutive openness.

The Sanskrit version — if Nattier is correct, a back-translation from Chinese — presents *rūpaṃ śūnyatā śūnyataiva rūpam* almost without a copula: the two terms are **juxtaposed** rather than connected by a verb. This bare juxtaposition resists the propositional reading even more than the Chinese 即是: there is no “is” to grasp, only two terms in proximity whose relationship the reader must inhabit rather than decode.

-----

### 7.3 English Translations — Red Pine and Thich Nhat Hanh

**Red Pine** (Bill Porter) renders the equation as:

> “Form is emptiness, emptiness is form”

The flat copula “is” makes the equation read as a **logical identity statement** — A = B — inviting exactly the philosophical processing that 即 was designed to prevent. **“Form”** for 色 carries Platonic resonances — formal properties, logical structure — abstracting away from the sensory-appetitive concreteness that makes 色 irreducibly bodily. **“Emptiness”** for 空 carries primarily **privative** connotations — absence, void, meaninglessness — that consistently produce the nihilistic misreading the equation is designed to prevent.

**Thich Nhat Hanh** supplements the same formula with the gloss: the wave is not other than water, water is not other than wave. The **wave-water metaphor** is pedagogically beautiful: physically intuitive, it avoids the nihilistic misreading, and it makes the equation accessible. Its cost is equally precise: the wave-water relationship is **fully comprehensible within ordinary experience**. The analogy **resolves the cognitive dissonance** before it can do its work. In his later work, Thich Nhat Hanh translates *śūnyatā* as **“interbeing”** — a neologism that captures the relational dimension but imports a specific interpretive framework, arriving with its own explanation attached.

What the comparative analysis reveals is not that some translators are more competent than others. It reveals that every translation into a language with different grammatical resources **closes something the Chinese holds open**. The underdetermination is lost. The temporal immediacy is weakened. The sensory charge is abstracted. These losses trace, with precision, the **shape of what propositional language as such cannot do**.

And here the reflexive point: the act of translation is itself an enactment of 色即是空. The meaning of the equation is neither located in any specific linguistic form nor separable from form. The meaning is **interdependent with form without being reducible to it** — which is 色即是空 空即是色, enacted in the very activity of trying to carry the sentence that says it across the gap between languages. Every translation fails. Every translation succeeds. The translator’s predicament is the teaching.

-----

## Section 8: The Mantra — Post-Propositional Language

At the close of the *Heart Sutra*, after 250 characters of systematic dismantling, the text stops making propositions. It does not conclude. It does not summarise. It emits a sound:

> 揭諦揭諦，波羅揭諦，波羅僧揭諦，菩提薩婆訶
> *gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā*

The tradition’s own annotation is deliberately minimal: **this mantra is true, not false** (*satyam amithyatvāt*). No mechanism is explained. The text simply asserts the mantra’s truth and stops.

The question this section addresses is the one the text refuses to answer: **what kind of truth can a sound have?** Not propositional truth — *gate* does not assert anything about the world that could be verified or falsified. Not referential truth. Not performative truth in Austin’s technical sense. Something else is being claimed, and the claim is serious enough to have generated two of the most sophisticated theories of language in pre-modern intellectual history.

-----

### 8.1 The Hindu Theory — Mīmāṃsā and *Śabdabrahman*

The **Mīmāṃsā** school arrives at its theory of sacred sound through a question that appears narrow — what guarantees the authority of the Vedas? — but opens into a full philosophy of language. The foundational claim, developed by **Jaimini** (c. 4th century BCE) and elaborated by **Kumārila Bhaṭṭa** (c. 7th century CE), is that **śabda** — the linguistic sound — is *nitya*: eternal, uncreated, not produced by any speaker. When a human being utters the syllable *agni*, they do not create a sound. They **manifest** a phonemic form that exists eternally. The physical sound waves are transient; the phonemic type is permanent.

This has a crucial consequence for mantra: if the word-meaning relationship is eternal and intrinsic rather than conventional and arbitrary, then the **power of a sacred syllable is inherent in its phonemic form**, not derived from its semantic content. The mantra does not work because you understand what it means. It works because its sound-shape has an inherent relationship to the reality it invokes. This grounds a demanding theory of ritual precision: a mispronounced mantra **activates something other than intended** — the phonemic form is the vehicle of power; deform the vehicle and you deform what it carries.

A related account comes from **Bhartṛhari** (c. 5th century CE) in his *Vākyapadīya*. Bhartṛhari posits *sphoṭa* — the unitary, indivisible linguistic entity underlying the sequence of physical sounds: the **whole utterance grasped in a single flash of meaning-recognition**. This *sphoṭa* he identifies with **Brahman** — with ultimate reality itself. Language, at its most fundamental level, is not a human instrument for representing a pre-linguistic world. It is the **ontological fabric of that world**. The cosmic principle is *Śabdabrahman* — the Word-Absolute — and the mantra is its most concentrated expression. Heidegger’s claim that “language is the house of Being” resonates here, though it arrives through entirely different routes. In both cases, language is not a tool that minds use to represent a pre-linguistic world — it is the medium in which world and mind are co-constituted.

-----

### 8.2 The Buddhist Theory — Mantrayāna and Kūkai

The Buddhist Mantrayāna cannot straightforwardly adopt the Mīmāṃsā position — the eternalist claim conflicts with Buddhist impermanence and dependent origination. The Buddhist theory must ground its claims differently, and the result is a philosophical architecture that is in some ways more radical than Mīmāṃsā’s eternalism.

The tradition distinguishes three categories: **dhāraṇī** 陀羅尼 (extended formulae that “hold” the dharma in concentrated form), **mantra** 真言 (shorter formulaic utterances whose propositional content is secondary), and **bīja** 種子 (single seed syllables — *Oṃ*, *Hūṃ*, *Āḥ* — that contain the entire energy of a deity in minimal phonemic unit).

The most philosophically developed Buddhist theory belongs to **Kūkai** 空海 (774–835), founder of the Japanese **Shingon** 真言 school. His key concept is ***hosshin seppō*** 法身説法 — “the Dharmakāya preaches the dharma.” Orthodox Mahāyāna held that the Dharmakāya is beyond expression. Kūkai reverses this: the Dharmakāya is **not** beyond expression. It is continuously, inexhaustibly expressing itself — in all sounds, all phenomena, all sensory experience. The universe is the Dharmakāya’s preaching, and every sound in it is, at the most fundamental level, the Dharmakāya’s mantra.

Within this framework, mantra practice is the practitioner’s gradual **identification with** the reality that was always already expressing itself through them. Kūkai systematises this in the ***sanmitsu*** 三密 doctrine — the “three mysteries”: **身密** (*shinmitsu*, body: mudrā), **口密** (*kōmitsu*, speech: mantra), and **意密** (*imitsu*, mind: visualisation). Transformation requires all three to be aligned simultaneously — a **multimodal embodied cognition theory**: the claim that change at the deepest level of mental functioning does not occur through semantic understanding alone but through the **integrated engagement of body, voice, and concentrated attention**. The mantra bypasses the propositional-semantic processing chain not because it is irrational but because the transformation it seeks operates at a level that propositional processing does not reach.

-----

### 8.3 What Neuroscience and Philosophy of Language Find

Contemporary neuroscience approaches mantra with the same instruments it brings to meditation and music — and finds, with the same combination of genuine illumination and genuine limit, that something physiologically real and consistent is occurring.

**Neural entrainment** — the synchronisation of brain oscillations to external rhythmic stimuli — is one of the most robustly documented effects of rhythmic sound. Sustained rhythmic stimulation reliably shifts dominant neural oscillation from **beta** (active discursive cognition, 15–30 Hz) toward **alpha** (relaxed alertness, 8–12 Hz) or **theta** (deep relaxation and meditative states, 4–8 Hz). Mantra repetition is precisely calibrated rhythmic sound production, and its repetition rate typically falls in the frequency range associated with theta entrainment — the same oscillatory state correlating with deep meditation and what Buddhist phenomenology identifies as the approach to non-conceptual awareness.

The specifically **vocal** dimension adds further mechanisms. Vocalisation activates **vagal tone** through laryngeal vibration stimulating the vagus nerve. Slow, controlled exhalation shifts autonomic balance through respiratory sinus arrhythmia. The resonance of certain phonemes in the chest and cranial vault produces **proprioceptive stimulation** — a felt sense of the body from inside — acting on the interoceptive systems constituting the body’s self-sensing. When Kūkai insists that mantra requires vocalisation rather than mental recitation, this is an implicit recognition that the body’s self-sensing systems are part of the transformation being sought.

The **philosophy of language** offers a different set of resources. J.L. Austin’s speech act theory — distinguishing locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts — provides a useful starting point, but mantra quickly exceeds its categories. Austin’s framework presupposes **semantic content** as the ground of speech acts. The mantra’s effects do not depend on semantic understanding: it is a perlocutionary act whose mechanism is not the hearer’s comprehension of propositional content. Austin’s framework has no category for this.

**Wittgenstein’s** later philosophy offers more traction. Meaning as use within a **form of life** (*Lebensform*) accounts for the Mantrayāna’s insistence that mantra cannot be extracted from the initiation context (*abhiṣeka* 灌頂), the lineage transmission, and the accompanying practices without losing its full efficacy. The mantra means what it does *in that practice*. Outside the form of life, it retains acoustic and physiological effects but what the tradition claims as its deepest effect requires the entire practice-context in which the sound is embedded.

**Terrence Deacon’s** emergence framework suggests that the soteriological effect of sustained mantra practice may be an **emergent property** of the combination of acoustic entrainment, somatic vibration, intentional concentration, symbolic richness, community context, and accumulated practice history. None of these components alone produces the effect. This is consistent with both what the traditions report and what the science finds: something real is happening, identifiable components contribute to it, and it exceeds any single-mechanism explanation.

The limit, as always, is the hard problem. The neuroscience can map what the brain does during mantra recitation. The philosophy of language can locate the practice within its form of life. What neither can determine is the nature of what Kūkai calls **recognition** — the moment in which the practitioner’s separation from the Dharmakāya is seen through. Whether this is a particularly integrated neurological state, an emergent property of sustained practice, or a genuine ontological discovery about the nature of sound and awareness — this is a question that honest inquiry must leave open.

What it cannot leave open is that *gate gate pāragate* is doing something that the philosophical argument preceding it cannot do. The *Heart Sutra* moves from argument to mantra not because the argument has failed but because the argument has succeeded — has done everything argument can do, has cleared every conceptual foothold, has brought the reader to the edge of what propositional language can reach. The mantra is not a supplement to the argument. It is the argument’s honest acknowledgment of its own limit — and the tradition’s claim that beyond that limit, sound continues where language stops.

## Section 9: Conclusion — The Boundary and What Lies Beyond It

An article that has traced the path from transgressive monks through Buddhist philosophy to the neuroscience of mantra recitation might be expected to conclude by synthesising its findings into a unified account — a clean summary of what has been established, what remains open, and what the field might profitably investigate next. This conclusion will not do that, for reasons the article itself has been establishing throughout. A clean synthesis would be a betrayal of the inquiry. It would perform, at the level of scholarly prose, exactly the domestication that Ikkyū’s explicit poems were designed to prevent and that the *Heart Sutra’s* mantra-close was designed to refuse: the conversion of a pointing gesture into a graspable proposition, the substitution of a conclusion for the open encounter that the argument has been sustaining.

What this conclusion attempts instead is honest accounting — of what the article has accomplished, of where each framework has reached its limit, and of what the article itself has been unable to reach.

-----

### 9.1 The Coherent Arc

The article began with a spectacle: the holy fool, whose sanctity is constituted by its apparent negation. It began there because the spectacle is the entry point available to any reader regardless of prior commitment. From the spectacle, the article moved inward in three stages.

The **first stage** (Sections 2–4) was structural and doctrinal: who these figures are, what functions their transgression performs, and what philosophical frameworks make those functions coherent. The structural analysis revealed four functions — epistemological rupture, social critique, the divergence between real and performed transgression, and the paradox of institutionalisation. The doctrinal analysis revealed that the Buddhist cases are philosophically distinct: the two-truths doctrine (Jìgōng), the Tantric transformation logic (Drukpa Künley), and the institutional polemic of the *Kyōunshū* (Ikkyū) generate structurally similar but doctrinally differentiated figures. The Christian case’s divergence — performed rather than actual transgression, grounded in kenotic theology — was shown to track a fundamental difference in how the two traditions understand the moral law’s relationship to liberation.

The **second stage** (Section 5) turned toward experience: the phenomenological territory that the doctrinal frameworks attempt to describe. The convergence between Buddhist accounts of subject-object dissolution, Western phenomenological analysis (Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty), and cognitive science (Metzinger, Friston) was shown to be genuine and illuminating. The neuroscientific evidence established that something physiologically real and consistent is occurring in the states the traditions describe. But each secular framework was shown to reach a boundary: the hard problem remains hard, the first-person character of the experience exceeds what third-person description can capture.

The **third stage** (Sections 6–8) examined language itself. The *Heart Sutra’s* central equation was shown to be not merely a philosophical claim but a performative instrument. The comparative translation analysis demonstrated that every rendering into another language closes something the Classical Chinese holds open — structural revelations of what propositional language as such cannot do. The mantra was shown to be the text’s honest acknowledgment of its own limit: the point at which argument surrenders into sound.

What the arc reveals, taken as a whole, is that these traditions are making claims about **the nature of human consciousness and its relationship to the forms through which it expresses and obscures itself**. Those claims can be examined, with genuine rigour, through the secular frameworks this article has brought to bear. And at the edge of what those frameworks can reach, the traditions’ own first-person phenomenological vocabulary remains as the most precise description available of territory that the secular frameworks have mapped from outside but not entered.

-----

### 9.2 What Each Framework Contributes and Cannot Reach

Honest accounting requires specifying, precisely rather than gesturally, what each framework deployed in this article has and has not established.

**Comparative religion** has established the structural recurrence of the transgressive saint as a genuine cross-cultural type, identified the functions that transgression performs, and located the deepest divergence in the typology in the underlying soteriology rather than in historical accident. What it cannot establish is which tradition’s self-understanding is correct, or whether the structural similarities between traditions reflect shared human phenomenology, convergent cultural logic, or both.

**Phenomenology** has provided the most philosophically rigorous Western vocabulary for the experiential territory at stake and shown that this territory is not the exclusive property of Buddhist traditions. What phenomenology cannot establish is whether the recognition the Buddhist traditions describe as awakening is what Heidegger’s *Angst* or Merleau-Ponty’s motor intentionality is gesturing toward, or whether these are genuinely different experiences that structural description makes appear similar.

**Cognitive science and neuroscience** have established that the states the traditions describe have measurable neural correlates, that something physiologically real and consistent occurs, and that these states reflect features of human neurological architecture available through multiple induction conditions. What neuroscience cannot establish — and what Chalmers’ hard problem prevents it from establishing without a revolution in the field’s foundational assumptions — is the nature of the experience from inside.

**Philosophy of language** has established that mantra cannot be accounted for within frameworks that presuppose semantic content as the ground of linguistic meaning, that Wittgenstein’s use-theory locates mantra’s efficacy within its form of life, and that the *Heart Sutra’s* movement from argument to mantra is philosophically motivated. What it cannot establish is whether the soteriological claims the traditions make for mantra — that it restructures the *ālayavijñāna*, that it participates in the Dharmakāya’s self-expression — are true in any sense beyond the pragmatic effects that acoustic entrainment and embodied practice can explain.

What remains, after all these frameworks have contributed their findings and acknowledged their limits, is a space that none of them can fill and that the traditions claim practice alone can enter. This is not a mystical evasion. It is a precise epistemological statement: **the first-person character of the experience is irreducible to any third-person description**, however sophisticated, and the traditions that have spent millennia developing first-person methodologies for approaching this territory have produced accounts that remain, within their own domain, more precise than anything the secular frameworks have yet generated.

-----

### 9.3 The Suppressed Question — Transgression, Power, and Whose Body Pays

This article has examined what transgressive sanctity points *toward* with sustained attention and, it is hoped, genuine rigour. It has done so while largely **bracketing** a different set of questions whose importance it would be dishonest to minimise in a conclusion.

The four transgressive figures at the article’s centre are all male. The women who appear in their narratives — the hundreds of women in Drukpa Künley’s *namtar*, the blind singer Mori in the *Kyōunshū*, the women at the margins of Jìgōng’s hagiographies — appear primarily as **instruments**: as the material through which male liberation is demonstrated, as the vehicles of male upāya, as the unnamed recipients of transformative encounters whose own soteriological situation the texts do not address. The doctrinal frameworks examined in Section 4 provide no account of what liberation looks like from the position of the woman through whom the male master’s liberation is being demonstrated. The *dohā* tradition addresses the *ḍākinī* 荼吉尼 as *prajñā* 般若 (wisdom), which is a philosophical elevation that simultaneously forecloses asking what the human woman involved experiences or requires.

This is not a peripheral issue that a feminist supplement could address without disturbing the main argument. It goes to the **structural centre** of the typology. If the transgressive saint’s freedom from conventional norms is the visible expression of awakened awareness, and if that freedom consistently expresses itself through the bodies of women who do not appear as subjects of their own liberation, then the question of whether the doctrine is being deployed in the service of realisation or in the service of male privilege — or both, simultaneously, in ways that cannot be cleanly separated — cannot be deferred indefinitely. The Mantrayāna’s insistence that Tantric practice requires the dissolution of ego-grasping as its precondition does not automatically resolve this question. Ego-dissolution and the exploitation of power differentials are not mutually exclusive; the history of Buddhist institutions includes cases where the language of Tantric transformation provided doctrinal cover for conduct that was, by any serious ethical analysis, abusive.

The feminist question is not raised here as a corrective afterthought but as an **honest acknowledgment of this article’s limit**. The comparative-structural method chosen in Section 1.2 allowed the analysis to proceed across traditions without importing the conclusion into the method — but it also allowed the analysis to proceed without fully attending to the bodies on which the structures it was examining were operating. A more complete account of transgressive sanctity would need to examine not only what it points toward but who bears its costs — and would need to ask whether the doctrinal sophistication of these traditions has, at times, functioned as a mechanism for insulating their institutional practices from the ethical scrutiny they deserve.

This article cannot complete that examination. It can name the gap, and in doing so, it hopes to model for the reader something that the traditions themselves, at their best, have always valued: the capacity to recognise the boundary of one’s own seeing — to know where the analysis stops and where something else, requiring different methods and different attention, begins.

That capacity — to hold what has been accomplished alongside honest acknowledgment of what remains unseen — is not a failure of the inquiry. It is, perhaps, the closest that academic writing can come to the gesture that every text examined in these pages has been attempting: the pointing that knows it is pointing, that does not mistake itself for the thing it points toward, and that leaves the reader at the edge of what the words can reach — and trusts them to go further.
