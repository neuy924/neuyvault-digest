# The Porous Person and the Future of Culture: From Meadian Plasticity to Strathernian Fractals

## Abstract
This review article explores the evolution of anthropological thought regarding personhood and cultural transmission, tracing a theoretical lineage from Margaret Mead’s social constructivism to Marilyn Strathern’s relational ontologies. By contrasting the Western "Buffered Individual" with the "Porous Dividual" found in Melanesian and East Asian societies, the text deconstructs the assumption of the autonomous self. Key themes include the manufacturing of adulthood through somatic rituals, the metabolic logic of the "leaky body," and the shift from post-figurative to pre-figurative learning in the age of Generative AI. The article concludes by synthesizing these theories to provide a framework for understanding 21st-century global complexity, suggesting that a return to relational and porous models of identity is essential for navigating modern planetary and digital challenges.

## Section I: Deconstructing the Buffered Self

### 1.1 The Individual vs. The Network: The "Island" Fallacy
In contemporary Western society, the most common way to describe a human being is as an **Individual**. Etymologically, "individual" stems from the Latin *individuum*, meaning "undividable." This linguistic root reveals a deep-seated belief: that at the core of every human is a singular, atomic essence that cannot be split or shared. We often visualize ourselves as islands—autonomous, self-directing, and fundamentally separated from others by the "border" of our skin.

However, if we look across the anthropological record, this "Island" model is a global rarity. Most cultures have historically viewed the person as a **Dividual**—a composite being made up of "parts" of others. In a relational network, you are not a self-contained unit; you are a crossroads where your parents' genetics, your community’s food, and your ancestors’ spirits meet. In this view, if you were to remove all your "connections," there would be no "you" left behind. You are the sum of your relations.

| Feature | The Western "Individual" (Buffered) | The Relational "Dividual" (Porous) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Boundary** | The physical skin; a "hard" shell. | The social network; a "permeable" mesh. |
| **Identity Source** | Internal (thoughts, soul, DNA). | External (exchanges, gifts, lineage). |
| **Moral Responsibility**| Personal and individual. | Collective and "Wantok" (shared). |
| **View of the Body** | Private property; a machine. | Public site; a "Leaky" vessel of exchange. |

### 1.2 The Anthropological Mirror: Making the Familiar Strange
Why do anthropologists spend decades studying the male initiation rites of the Sepik River or the *Hara* (腹) centering of Japanese martial arts? It is not merely to document "exotic" behaviors. Rather, these cultures serve as an **Anthropological Mirror**. 

When we see a society where a person is literally "built" through the ingestion of another's ritual substances, or where the "self" is distributed across a *Wantok* (one-talk/kinship) system, it forces us to look back at our own "Individualism" with fresh eyes. We begin to see that our sense of being a "bounded" person is not a biological fact, but a cultural "construction." This process is known as **Defamiliarization**: taking the things we take for granted—like our privacy or our sense of "owning" our bodies—and realizing they are unique cultural choices. 

### 1.3 Key Concept: Charles Taylor’s "Buffered" vs. "Porous" Self
To understand how we became "Individuals," we must look at the work of philosopher Charles Taylor, who describes a massive shift in human consciousness that occurred during the European Enlightenment. He categorizes this as a move from a **Porous Self** to a **Buffered Self**.

#### The Porous Self (Pre-Modern/Non-Western)
For most of human history, the self was "porous." There was no hard line between the mind and the world. 
* **Permeability:** Spirits, demons, and the *Qi* (氣) of the environment could literally enter the body. 
* **External Meaning:** Meaning was not "inside your head"; it was in the world. A storm wasn't just weather; it was a communication. 
* **Vulnerability:** Because you were porous, you were constantly vulnerable to the influence of others and the divine.

#### The Buffered Self (Modern/Western)
With the rise of modern science and secularism, the West developed the "Buffered Self." We "closed" our pores.
* **The Buffer:** We created a mental "buffer" that separates our internal thoughts from external reality. We no longer believe that a spirit can "enter" us just because we walked past a certain tree.
* **Internalized Meaning:** We believe that meanings, feelings, and "the self" are all located safely inside our skulls. 
* **Disenchantment:** While the buffered self gives us a sense of safety and autonomy, Taylor argues it also leads to "disenchantment"—a feeling of being disconnected from a world that no longer seems "alive" or responsive to us.

## Section II: Margaret Mead and the Plasticity of Human Nature

### 2.1 Challenging the Biological "Given": The Samoan Experiment
In the early 20th century, the dominant psychological view in the West was **biological determinism**. This theory suggested that human behavior—particularly the turbulent emotions of adolescence—was a direct, unavoidable result of physiological changes. Psychologists like G. Stanley Hall popularized the concept of ***Sturm und Drang*** (Storm and Stress), arguing that the "rebellion" and "angst" of teenagers were universal biological imperatives.

In 1925, a young Margaret Mead traveled to the island of Ta'ū in Samoa to test this assumption. Her research question was simple yet revolutionary: *Is the distress of adolescence inherent in the human body, or is it a product of the culture that surrounds the body?*

Mead found that Samoan girls transitioned into adulthood with almost none of the psychological upheaval seen in American youth. In Samoa, sexual experimentation was less stigmatized, social hierarchies were clear, and the community functioned as a large, supportive network rather than a high-pressure nuclear family. 
* **The Takeaway:** Mead’s work in Samoa provided the "existence proof" that a smooth adolescence was possible. If biology were the sole driver, Samoan girls would have been just as "stressed" as American girls. Because they weren't, Mead logically concluded that **culture**, not biology, was the primary architect of the adolescent experience.

### 2.2 The Great Triple-Culture Experiment: Sex and Temperament
Mead’s most profound impact on undergraduate anthropology comes from her 1935 work, *Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies*. In this study, she investigated whether "masculine" and "feminine" personality traits were hardwired. She analyzed three distinct groups in New Guinea, each of which presented a different "script" for how men and women should behave.

#### The Arapesh, Mundugumor, and Tchambuli
Mead’s findings showed that "temperament"—the underlying personality of an individual—is not sex-linked. Instead, cultures select certain traits and label them as the "norm" for everyone, or split them arbitrarily between the sexes.

| Society | Cultural Expectations for Men | Cultural Expectations for Women | Mead's Observation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Mountain Arapesh** | Gentle, cooperative, nurturing, and sensitive. | Gentle, cooperative, nurturing, and sensitive. | Both sexes displayed what the West calls "feminine" traits. |
| **Mundugumor (Biwat)** | Aggressive, competitive, ruthless, and violent. | Aggressive, competitive, ruthless, and violent. | Both sexes displayed what the West calls "masculine" traits. |
| **Tchambuli (Chambri)** | Concerned with gossip, aesthetics, and art; emotionally dependent. | Domineering, managerial, impersonal, and the primary providers. | A "reversal" of 1930s Western gender stereotypes. |

Through these comparisons, Mead argued that human nature is essentially a blank canvas—or "plastic"—capable of being molded into nearly any form. If a culture decides that men should be aesthetic and women should be managerial (as she observed with the Tchambuli), then the individuals within that culture will naturally grow to fit that role. This is the foundation of **social constructivism**: the idea that our "innate" gender roles are actually learned social performances.

### 2.3 The Mechanism of Enculturation: The "Social Script"
How does a culture convince its members that these arbitrary rules are "natural"? Mead identified the primary mechanism as **Enculturation**. This is the process through which a child internalizes the values, behaviors, and expectations of their society.

Mead viewed culture as a "personality writ large." A society chooses a specific "temperament" (like the peacefulness of the Arapesh) and weaves it into every aspect of life: from how parents hold their babies to the stories told around the fire. 
1. **The Mold:** Culture acts as a physical and psychological mold. By the time a child reached adulthood, they had been so thoroughly "enculturated" that they could not imagine being any other way.
2. **The Deviant:** Mead also wrote about the "deviant"—the person whose natural temperament does not fit the cultural mold. For example, a gentle man in the aggressive Mundugumor society. Mead argued that these individuals aren't "broken" or "sick"; they are simply a "mismatch" for their specific cultural script.

## Section III: The Architecture of Initiation: Manufacturing Adulthood

### 3.1 The Social Factory: Creating the Adult Male
If Margaret Mead established that human nature is "plastic" and gender is a "script," then **Male Initiation** is the industrial process by which that script is engraved into the physical body. In many Melanesian societies, adulthood—especially masculine adulthood—is not considered a natural biological result of aging. Instead, it is a social status that must be **manufactured**. While a girl becomes a woman through the biological event of menarche, a boy only becomes a man through a deliberate, and often violent, series of cultural interventions.

This process operates through a specific mechanism known as the **Rite de Passage** (Rite of Passage). First theorized by Arnold van Gennep, this mechanism describes how individuals are moved from one social category to another through three distinct phases:

1.  **Separation:** The boy is physically and symbolically removed from his mother’s world (the domestic sphere).
2.  **Liminality (The Threshold):** The "in-between" state. The initiate is no longer a child but not yet a man. This is a period of intense ritual, trial, and the breaking of old identities.
3.  **Incorporation:** The individual is returned to society with a new status, often marked by a new name, new clothes, and new responsibilities.

### 3.2 The Spermatic Economy: Semen as a Vital Resource
In the Sepik River and Fly River regions (such as among the **Iatmul** and **Keraki**), initiation is grounded in a biological-cultural logic often called the **Spermatic Economy**. This is the belief that masculinity is a finite resource that must be acquired from others. 

The underlying assumption is that boys are born "contaminated" by their mother's blood and "empty" of the masculine essence. To grow, they must undergo a metabolic exchange. As analyzed by researchers like Gilbert Herdt, this often involves **Ritualized Insemination**. In this worldview, semen is viewed as "white blood"—a vitalizing substance that must be transferred from the older, "potent" generation to the younger "empty" generation.

| Ritual Mechanism | Cultural Logic | Objective |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Forced Nose-bleeding** | Purging the "female blood" inherited from the mother. | To cleanse the body of maternal influence. |
| **Ritual Insemination** | Ingesting or absorbing semen (*jerungdu*) from elders. | To "charge" the boy with masculine growth and strength. |
| **Scarification** | Cutting patterns into the skin (e.g., crocodile scales). | To replace the "smooth" skin of childhood with the "tough" skin of a man. |

### 3.3 Numinous Technology: The Bullroarer and the Sound of Power
The psychological architecture of initiation relies heavily on **Secret-Sacred Knowledge**. A central tool in this process is the **Bullroarer**—a flat slat of wood swung on a cord to produce a low, pulsating drone.

In Melanesian ritual, the bullroarer is a "numinous technology." To women and children (the uninitiated), the sound is an ontological fact: it is the literal "voice of the spirits" or the "ancestor’s roar." During the liminal phase, initiates are terrorized by this sound, believing they are about to be consumed by a supernatural entity.

The "climax" of initiation is often the **Revelation of the Secret**: the boys are shown that the terrifying spirit is actually a piece of wood swung by a man. This revelation does not "ruin the magic"; instead, it introduces the boy into the **Political-Religious Hierarchy**. He learns that power resides in the monopoly of the secret. By moving from being someone who is *frightened* by the sound to someone who *makes* the sound, he is incorporated into the collective male authority of the village.

## Section IV: From Past to Future: Typologies of Cultural Learning

### 4.1 The Temporal Architecture of Culture
While Margaret Mead’s early work focused on the specific "scripts" of different societies, her later work—culminating in the 1970 publication of *Culture and Commitment*—shifted toward a much larger question: **How does the speed of change affect how we learn?** Mead moved from analyzing *what* children learn to analyzing the **directionality of modeling**. She realized that the relationship between generations is not a biological constant, but a variable of technological and social velocity.

To explain this, Mead developed a tripartite model that categorizes societies based on their relationship with time.

### 4.2 Post-figurative Cultures: The Authority of the Past
For the vast majority of human history, cultures were **Post-figurative**. In these societies, the future is essentially a repetition of the past. 
* **Direction of Learning:** Children learn primarily from their forebears. 
* **Role of the Elder:** The elders are the ultimate authorities because they have already walked the path the young will follow. 
* **Social Stability:** These cultures emphasize continuity, such as the Confucian *Xiào* (孝).

### 4.3 Co-figurative Cultures: The Present in Transition
A **Co-figurative** culture emerges when a society undergoes a significant rupture—such as a war, a massive migration, or a new technological introduction—that renders the elders’ experience partially obsolete. 
* **Direction of Learning:** Both adults and children look to their **peers** (contemporaries) for behavioral cues. 
* **The "Immigrant" Experience:** Mead noted that this is common in immigrant communities where parents are "immigrants in time."

### 4.4 Pre-figurative Cultures: The Pioneers of the Future
Mead argued that the mid-20th century represents a radical new state: the **Pre-figurative** culture. In this model, the rate of change is so global and so rapid that no one—elder or youth—knows exactly what the future looks like.
* **Direction of Learning:** The traditional flow of knowledge is reversed. Adults begin to learn from their children.
* **Youth as Teachers:** Because the youth are "natives" to the new technological or social reality, they possess an intuitive grasp of the present that their parents lack.

| Culture Type | Learning Direction | Velocity of Change | Primary Authority |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Post-figurative** | From Elders to Youth | Low / Static | Ancestors / Elders |
| **Co-figurative** | Peer to Peer | Moderate / Transition | The Contemporary Group |
| **Pre-figurative** | From Youth to Elders | High / Accelerated | The "Native" Pioneer |

## Section V: The Digital Frontier: Pre-figuration in the Age of AI

### 5.1 Beyond the Human Teacher: Algorithmic Socialization
In Mead’s original model, the "Pre-figurative" shift was driven by youth teaching their elders. Today, that mechanism has been hyper-charged by **Algorithmic Pre-figuration**. We are moving from a "horizontal" flow of knowledge to a **diagonal** flow, where the "Digital Native" looks to Large Language Models (LLMs) for socialization.

### 5.2 The "Data Gap" vs. The Generation Gap
In 2026, the gap is fundamentally about **Interface and Intuition**. While "Immigrants in Time" view AI as a discrete "tool," "AI Natives" view it as a co-constituent of their reality.

| Feature | Mead’s 1970 Pre-figuration | 2026 AI-Driven Pre-figuration |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Teacher** | The Youth (Human) | The Algorithm (Synthetic) |
| **Learning Direction** | Youth → Elder | AI → Youth → Elder |
| **Pace of Change** | Decadal (Cultural shifts) | Monthly (Model iterations) |
| **Nature of Knowledge** | Experience-based | Data-driven / Probabilistic |

## Section VI: The Dividual: Rethinking Personal Identity

### 6.1 Beyond the "Single" Self
Marilyn Strathern deconstructs the Western concept of the "Individual" and replaces it with a model she calls the **Dividual**. In Melanesian societies, the person is viewed as **Partible**—meaning they are made of parts that belong to others.

### 6.2 The Dividual: A Composite of Relations
The mechanism of the **Dividual** relies on the idea that every person is a "microcosm" of their social network. You are "Dividual" because you can be divided.

| The Western "Individual" | The Melanesian "Dividual" |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Ontology:** Atomistic (Self-contained). | **Ontology:** Relational (Composite). |
| **Origin:** You are born as a "whole" person. | **Origin:** You are built by the labor of others. |
| **Boundary:** The skin is a "hard" wall. | **Boundary:** The skin is a "permeable" mesh. |
| **Sociality:** Relationships are "add-ons." | **Sociality:** Relationships are "constitutive." |

### 6.3 The Gift vs. The Commodity: Giving "Parts" of the Self
* **The Commodity (Western Logic):** Alienable transaction.
* **The Gift (Melanesian Logic):** **Inalienable**. It contains the producer's sweat and land. To eat the yam is to ingest a "part" of the farmer.

## Section VII: Somatic Porosity: The Body as a Sieve

### 7.1 The Myth of the Bounded Body
In Melanesian and East Asian worldviews, we encounter the **Porous** or **Leaky Body**. Here, biological substances flow between people to maintain social and spiritual health.

### 7.2 Eastern Energetics: *Qi* (氣) and the Meridiand Portals
In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the body’s porosity is formalized through the concept of ***Qi*** (氣).
* **The Meridiand Portals:** Acupuncture points are literally called *Xué* (穴), meaning "caves." These are the structural "leaks" where the body’s internal energy meets the external world.

| Model | Primary Mechanism | View of the Skin | Goal of Health |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Western Buffered** | Cellular Autonomy | A "Hard" Barrier | Protection from the "Outside" |
| **Melanesian Leaky** | Partible Exchange | A "Permeable" Mesh | Correct Circulation of Substances |
| **Sinitic (Chinese)** | *Qi* Resonance (氣) | A "Sieve" with Portals | Harmonizing Internal & External Flow |

## Section VIII: Filial Piety vs. The Technological Gap

### 8.1 The Confucian Anchor in a Pre-figurative World
East Asian cultures use **Techno-Confucianism** to bridge the generation gap. The primary mechanism is ***Xiào*** (孝 - Filial Piety). While youth possess the "How-to," elders retain the "Why it matters."

### 8.2 The Ritual of "Face-Saving" and Honorifics
In South Korea, the use of ***Jondaemal*** (존댓말 - polite speech) preserves the elder's "Face" (*Chemyeon* / 체면) even when the youth is acting as the teacher.

## Section IX: Partial Connections: Navigating Global Complexity

### 9.1 Beyond the "Whole" Culture
Marilyn Strathern introduced **Partial Connections** (1991). She argued that we are **"More than one, but less than many."**

### 9.2 The Rhizome vs. The Tree
* **The Arborescent Model (The Tree):** Hierarchical and predictable.
* **The Rhizomatic Model (The Ginger Root):** Lateral and unpredictable. This is the metaphor for the **Internet**.

| Feature | The Arborescent (Tree) Model | The Rhizomatic (Network) Model |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Logic** | Hierarchy and "Top-Down" power. | Lateral and "Side-to-Side" connections. |
| **Growth** | Linear and predictable. | Unpredictable; "leaks" in all directions. |
