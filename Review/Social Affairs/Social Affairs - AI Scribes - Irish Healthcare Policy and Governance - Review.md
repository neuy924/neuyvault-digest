# HSE and Irish Hospitals: Stance on AI Scribes

## Executive Summary

Ireland's Health Service Executive (HSE) and the broader Irish hospital ecosystem have moved from cautious observation to active, structured engagement with AI scribe technology. The landmark *AI for Care 2026–2030* national strategy, launched in March 2026, explicitly endorses AI scribe tools as a priority use case, projecting documentation time reductions of up to 40%. Simultaneously, regulatory bodies — HIQA, the Medical Council, and the Data Protection Commission — are developing governance frameworks that impose clear conditions around human oversight, GDPR compliance, and patient consent. In practice, several Irish hospitals and GP practices are already live with AI scribe platforms, while the wider system awaits finalised national guidance before committing to large-scale rollout.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6]

***

## National Policy Context

### AI for Care 2026–2030 Strategy

Launched on 11 March 2026 at Mater Misericordiae University Hospital by Minister for Health Jennifer Carroll MacNeill, *AI for Care* is Ireland's first national strategy dedicated to AI in health and social care, covering the period 2026–2030. The strategy includes 24 defined priority opportunities across clinical care, operations, research and innovation, and public health.[^3][^4][^7]

Clinical documentation is one of the strategy's headline use cases. AI scribe tools are cited as capable of **cutting documentation time by up to 40%**, which the strategy frames as freeing clinicians to spend more time in direct patient contact. The strategy mandates:[^1][^3]

- **Mandatory human oversight** of all AI-generated outputs
- **Compliance with the EU AI Act 2024**
- **HIQA-led national guidance** to govern safe and transparent implementation[^1]

The HSE's AI Implementation Framework — currently pending approval by the HSE Senior Leadership Team — will serve as the primary instrument for activating the strategy's roadmap. It will require funding commitments before deployment can begin at scale, and stipulates that where best-of-breed market solutions exist, they will be adopted rather than developed internally.[^4]

### HSE AI and Automation Centre of Excellence (CoE)

The HSE established an AI and Automation Centre of Excellence (CoE) to govern and coordinate AI adoption across the health system. Its functions include:[^8][^2]

- Setting rules for how AI tools should be used across HSE services
- Running pilot implementations of AI through the National Service Plan 2025
- Maintaining a mandatory central AI inventory to which **all AI solutions deployed within the HSE must be registered**[^9]
- Supporting staff through assessment processes aligned with EU AI Act risk classifications[^9]

The CoE also requires that all proposed AI solutions be assessed using a standardised tool before deployment, ensuring organisational accountability and regulatory compliance.[^9]

### HSE Interim AI Guidance Document

The HSE has published interim AI guidance for staff under its *Digital for Care 2030* framework. The guidance adopts an explicitly **human-centred approach**, stating that "AI should not replace human oversight and human interventions" in either clinical or non-clinical settings. Key requirements from this guidance include:[^9]

- Clinicians must review all AI-driven recommendations before applying them
- AI must be treated as a tool to augment, not replace, human judgement
- All patient data used in AI systems must be access-controlled
- Compliance with GDPR and Medical Device Regulation (MDR) is mandatory alongside EU AI Act requirements[^9]

## Regulatory Framework

### HIQA's National AI Framework

HIQA was commissioned by the Department of Health to develop a **National Framework for the Responsible and Safe Use of Artificial Intelligence in Health and Social Care**. A public scoping consultation was launched in April 2025, followed by a public consultation on the draft national guidance in January 2026.[^10][^5]

The draft guidance is built around **four core principles**:[^11]
1. Accountability
2. Human rights-based approach
3. Safety and wellbeing
4. Responsiveness

HIQA acknowledges that AI is already in use in Irish healthcare for diagnostics, administrative streamlining, and clinical documentation, including "supporting with note taking to enable clinicians to focus directly on the patient during consultations". Its guidance explicitly recognises these benefits while calling for maintained human oversight and patient transparency.[^5]

### Medical Council Position

In October 2025, the Medical Council stated that AI use by doctors brings "significant" ethical, legal, regulatory and professional challenges. On AI scribes specifically, the Council took a nuanced position: if a doctor uses an AI tool to **record consultations**, they must provide more information to patients about how the AI works and how it collects and uses personal information — including a warning that if public generative AI software is used, "personal information becomes public domain". Transparency is described as "essential".[^6]

### Data Protection Commission

The Data Protection Commission has published guidance on AI, large language models, and data protection. It advises clinicians and practices to ensure they understand:

- What personal data the system uses and how it is processed
- Whether any third parties are involved and where the data is sent
- Whether the provider retains or re-uses data in any way
- How the system enables compliance with GDPR obligations[^12]

## Clinical Professional Bodies

### Irish College of GPs (ICGP)

The ICGP has published *The Use of Artificial Intelligence in Irish General Practice*, a dedicated report addressing AI scribes in detail. The report positions digital scribes as "part of the first emerging technologies that are available for GPs" and acknowledges that implementing an AI-supported digital scribe "can vastly improve the documentation process in clinical practice," potentially saving time, improving patient interaction, and producing higher quality clinical notes.[^13]

However, the ICGP issues several critical caveats:

- AI scribes do **not have 100% accuracy** and outputs must always be checked by the clinician — it notes that editing demands "may cancel out time-saving from not typing"
- AI systems used in healthcare are classified as **high-risk** under the EU AI Act and must adhere to stringent regulations
- Obtaining **informed patient consent** is a critical step
- GPs must ensure EEA-based data processing and GDPR compliance, and conduct a **Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)** for their own practice, even if the vendor provides a template[^13]

The ICGP provides a detailed vendor due diligence checklist covering data sharing agreements, accuracy handling, EHR integration, data security, regulatory approval, customisation, and breach policies.[^13]

The Irish College of GPs also held an Autumn Conference in October 2025 dedicated to *Future Ready: AI and Innovations in General Practice*, covering practical applications of AI in consultations, GDPR implications, and medico-legal considerations.[^14]

### Medical Protection (Medico-Legal Guidance)

Medical Protection, the indemnity organisation, has addressed AI scribe use directly. It advises that members can seek assistance for clinical negligence claims arising from AI use, provided the system is not fully autonomous and human oversight is maintained — but **will not normally indemnify for failures of the AI software itself**. Doctors working in HSE or public hospitals are directed to clarify indemnity positions with the State Claims Agency.[^12]

## Hospital-Level Adoption

### Mater Misericordiae University Hospital

The Mater Hospital is at the forefront of Irish hospital AI adoption. In 2023, it became the first Irish hospital to introduce AI technology across its Radiology Department. In June 2025, it launched Ireland's **first hospital-based Centre for AI and Digital Health** at the Pillar Centre for Transformative Healthcare. This centre is developing a *Responsible AI Framework and Policy* to ensure ethical AI use and responsible data management across all specialties, including radiology, oncology, cardiology, and ophthalmology.[^15][^16][^17]

The *AI for Care* strategy launch was specifically held at the Mater, signalling its role as a national reference site.[^7][^1]

### Galway Clinic

Galway Clinic deployed T-Pro's AI-powered clinical documentation platform and achieved HIMSS Stage 6 accreditation, making it one of the most digitally sophisticated hospitals in Europe. T-Pro provides digital dictation and speech recognition to improve documentation speed, accuracy, and quality.[^18]

### Cork University Maternity Hospital (CUMH)

CUMH piloted T-Pro in February 2021 and subsequently extended the platform to all clinical teams and departments. The adoption significantly reduced document turnaround times and is integrated with HealthLink for electronic distribution of clinical correspondence.[^19]

### South East Community Healthcare (Mental Health Services)

South East Community Healthcare adopted T-Pro's cloud-based digital dictation for mental health services across Carlow, Kilkenny, and South Tipperary, in line with eHealth Ireland's *Cloud First Digital Strategy*. The solution replaced analogue tape dictation and enables mobile clinical documentation across remote sites.[^20]

### Western Rheumatology Partnership

Western Rheumatology Partnership has deployed **Heidi Health**, an AI medical scribe, for consultant-led rheumatology consultations. The platform transcribes patient visits, generates clinical notes, and drafts clinic letters. Patient verbal consent is obtained before each session, and the system is represented as GDPR-compliant.[^21]

### General Practice (Primary Care)

AI scribe adoption is most advanced in primary care. The Socrates GP software platform — used by Irish GPs — has begun rolling out *Socrates AI Services* in collaboration with Heidi Health, offering a phased integration of ambient AI natively within the practice management system. Bayview Family Practice is among those publicly announcing Heidi AI transcription in routine use.[^22][^23]

A feasibility study published in January 2026 in *HRB Open Research* tested an AI digital scribe template enhanced for neurological symptom screening in Irish general practices within the ARQ Practice-Based Research Network — all of which routinely use the Heidi AI medical scribe — demonstrating growing integration in academic and clinical GP settings.[^24]

## Key Themes: Enablers and Concerns

### Enablers

| Factor | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| National strategy mandate | AI for Care 2026–2030 explicitly targets clinical documentation as a priority use case[^1] |
| Demonstrated efficiency | Studies document significant reductions in documentation time; international evidence cites 40%+ savings[^3][^25] |
| Clinician burnout reduction | Administrative burden identified as a major driver; AI scribes linked to improved work-life balance[^26] |
| Market-ready solutions | Heidi Health, T-Pro, Microdoc and similar platforms already deployed in Irish settings[^21][^19][^27] |
| EU AI Act alignment | Irish governance frameworks designed to align with EU-wide standards, reducing legal ambiguity[^9] |

### Concerns and Barriers

| Concern | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Accuracy and hallucinations | AI scribe outputs are not 100% accurate; hallucinations in medical summaries remain a documented concern[^13] |
| GDPR and data sovereignty | Requirements to process data within the EEA and obtain DPIAs are complex for smaller practices[^13][^12] |
| Patient consent ambiguity | Debate over whether verbal consent during consultation is sufficient and how to document it[^12][^13] |
| EHR integration gaps | Poor integration with existing electronic health records identified as a key usability barrier[^26] |
| Medico-legal liability | Indemnity coverage gaps for AI software failures; indemnifiers do not cover tool defects, only clinician judgement errors[^12] |
| Digital inequality | Smaller GP practices with limited digital resources face disproportionate governance burden compared to large organisations[^28] |
| Bias and inequality risks | Medical Council warns AI could reinforce bias, particularly affecting vulnerable or underrepresented patient groups[^6] |

***

## Overall Assessment

Ireland's health system has adopted a **cautiously progressive** stance on AI scribes. At the policy level, the HSE and Department of Health are strongly supportive, having placed clinical documentation prominently within the *AI for Care* national strategy and committing to structured rollout under EU AI Act governance. Regulatory bodies — HIQA, the Medical Council, and the Data Protection Commission — are converging on a framework emphasising mandatory human oversight, transparency to patients, and robust data governance as preconditions for deployment.

At the hospital level, adoption is real but uneven. The Mater, Galway Clinic, CUMH, and South East Community Healthcare have demonstrated successful implementations. Primary care is advancing fastest, driven by platforms like Heidi Health and T-Pro integrated with GP software. The principal constraint on accelerated rollout is not reluctance toward the technology itself, but the pending finalisation of HIQA's national guidance and the HSE AI Implementation Framework — both expected to clarify governance requirements for organisations yet to act.[^4][^5][^9]
