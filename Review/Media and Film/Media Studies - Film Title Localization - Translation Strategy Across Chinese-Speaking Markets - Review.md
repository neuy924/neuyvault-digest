# Hollywood Film-Title Localization in Mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong: Translation Strategy, Paratext, and Transnational Circulation

## Abstract

This review article examines Hollywood film-title localization in mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong as an interdisciplinary problem at the intersection of translation studies, cultural theory, literary theory, and film theory. Rather than treating title translation as a minor lexical exercise or a simple choice between literal and free rendering, the article argues that localized film titles function as compressed paratextual thresholds through which films become culturally legible, commercially marketable, and affectively pre-sold to regional audiences. The review synthesizes key concepts including domestication, foreignization, explicitation, and transcreation, and places them in dialogue with paratext theory, glocalization, reception aesthetics, genre theory, the sociology of cultural production, intertextuality, and transnational cinema. Across these frameworks, the article shows that title localization is not merely derivative of an original film text but constitutive of the film’s public identity as it circulates globally.

A central argument is that mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong are all target-oriented title-localization systems, but they operationalize target orientation differently. Mainland China often privileges legibility, standardization, and explicitation; Taiwan more often balances accessibility with tonal and affective calibration; Hong Kong frequently favors vivid, commercially forceful transcreation. These regional tendencies are not absolute rules, but they provide a useful comparative framework for analyzing how one Hollywood film can enter three Sinophone markets under three different public identities. The article further argues that titles shape not only recognition but reception: they cue genre, structure the horizon of expectations, and participate in the affective contract between film and viewer before the first image appears.

By integrating insights from translation studies and film/media theory, the article proposes that localized film titles should be understood as small but analytically dense sites where translation strategy, promotional logic, audience expectation, institutional field pressures, intertextual memory, cultural identity, and transnational circulation converge. In doing so, it repositions title localization as a valuable object of study for understanding how Hollywood is regionally remade in Greater China.

## 1. Introduction: Why Film Titles Matter

### 1.1 Film titles as compressed cultural and commercial texts

Film titles are among the smallest units in cinema, yet they are also among the most heavily burdened with meaning. A title must identify, attract, frame, differentiate, and circulate a film across markets, media formats, and audience groups. It is therefore not merely a label attached to a finished work. It is a condensed cultural and commercial text that participates in shaping how the work is imagined before it is encountered. In a theatrical or platform context saturated with posters, thumbnails, trailers, festival blurbs, press headlines, and algorithmic listings, the title often functions as the first interpretive threshold. It does not simply name the film; it helps produce the conditions under which the film will be received.

This dual function becomes especially visible in translation. When a Hollywood title moves into another language, the translator or localizing institution is not dealing only with lexical transfer. What is at stake is the redistribution of tone, genre cues, emotional promise, prestige, and market legibility. A title may preserve ambiguity, clarify premise, heighten danger, soften emotional nuance, or replace allusive resonance with direct communicative force. In that sense, title localization sits at the intersection of language, marketing, and cultural mediation.

### 1.2 Why Greater China is a useful comparative field

Mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong form an especially productive comparative field for the study of title localization because they share overlapping written traditions while maintaining distinct media histories, linguistic ecologies, and promotional cultures. The three markets are linked enough to make comparison meaningful, yet different enough to make the comparison analytically rich. They should not be treated as a single undifferentiated “Chinese” space of reception.

At a broad level, the three markets often appear to display different localization tendencies. Mainland Chinese titles frequently favor clarity and premise legibility; Taiwanese titles often preserve more tonal or emotional nuance; Hong Kong titles are well known for vivid, commercially punchy reformulations. Even when these tendencies are not absolute, they provide a strong starting hypothesis for comparative analysis.

### 1.3 Research questions and scope

This article asks three related questions. First, how can Hollywood film-title localization in mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong be described more precisely than through a simple literal-versus-free distinction? Second, what theoretical frameworks best explain why the three markets often localize titles differently? Third, what does title localization reveal about broader questions of cultural identity, genre signaling, paratextual framing, and transnational circulation?

The focus here is on **Hollywood film titles localized for the three Sinophone markets**, rather than on all forms of audiovisual translation. The article is concerned primarily with theoretical explanation and comparative patterning, not with constructing an exhaustive database of every title variant. Its aim is to synthesize translation studies, cultural theory, literary theory, and film theory into a more robust framework for understanding localized titles as culturally and industrially significant thresholds.

### 1.4 Core argument and article structure

The core argument is that Hollywood film-title localization in mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong is best understood as a **paratextual and transnational practice** shaped by overlapping translation strategies and media logics. Domestication is the broad norm, but it operates differently across the three markets. Mainland China often leans toward domestication reinforced by explicitation; Hong Kong often domesticates through transcreation; Taiwan more often occupies a hybrid space in which tonal calibration and selective source-flavor retention remain visible.

The article proceeds in four stages. It first reviews scholarship and establishes the conceptual vocabulary of domestication, foreignization, explicitation, transcreation, and paratext. It then compares regional title-localization patterns in Greater China. Next, it expands the analysis through glocalization, reception aesthetics, genre theory, the sociology of cultural production, intertextuality, and transnational cinema. It concludes by arguing that localized film titles should be understood not as minor accessories to distribution, but as compact sites where translation strategy, audience expectation, industrial power, and global circulation intersect.

## 2. Scholarship Review and Conceptual Foundations

### 2.1 Film-title translation as a subfield of audiovisual translation

Film-title translation occupies an unusual position within audiovisual translation. It is a marginal object in terms of textual length, yet a highly consequential one in terms of public visibility and market effect. Much of audiovisual translation scholarship has focused on subtitling, dubbing, voice-over, and multimodal transfer, where the central problem is how verbal meaning interacts with sound, image, and timing. Title translation shares some of these concerns, especially the problem of cross-cultural mediation, but it differs in one decisive respect: a film title is not primarily a line within the work, but a threshold to the work. It circulates before viewing, outside viewing, and often independently of viewing.

This difference has two consequences. First, title translation is more exposed to **promotional pressure** than many other forms of audiovisual translation. Second, it is more compressed. A subtitle can distribute meaning across many lines; a title must condense genre, tone, market positioning, and symbolic resonance into a very small linguistic unit. As a result, title translation often appears less constrained by line-by-line fidelity and more strongly shaped by questions of communicative force.

| Feature | Subtitling / dubbing | Film-title translation |
|---|---|---|
| Relation to main text | Embedded within film | Precedes and frames film |
| Primary pressure | Semantic transfer + timing | Impact + legibility + framing |
| Degree of compression | Distributed | Extreme |
| Market function | Secondary | Primary/promotional |

### 2.2 Greater China research on Hollywood title localization

Within this broader field, Greater China offers one of the richest comparative sites for studying title localization. Scholarship on Hollywood title translation across mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong has repeatedly noted that the three markets do not simply produce script variants of the same translation. Instead, they often generate different naming strategies that reflect distinct linguistic habits, promotional cultures, and regional media histories.

The most useful starting point is corpus-based work comparing title practices across the three regions. This line of research has shown that mainland China often relies more visibly on **literal translation and transliteration**, while Hong Kong shows a stronger tendency toward **adaptation**, and Taiwan frequently occupies a more mixed position. That pattern matters because it complicates the naïve assumption that freer translation always means more domestication and literal translation always means more source orientation.

### 2.3 Limits of the literal-versus-free binary

One recurring weakness in film-title discussion is the persistence of a simple **literal-versus-free** binary. That binary is intuitively attractive because title differences are immediately visible. Yet it is too blunt to explain what film-title localization is actually doing. A title can be lexically close to the source and still be culturally domesticating; it can be lexically distant and still preserve some source-side function; it can be explicit without being free; and it can be creative without being analytically random.

| Operation | Main question |
|---|---|
| Literal translation | How close is the wording to the source? |
| Domestication | How far is foreignness reduced for the target audience? |
| Explicitation | How much implicit information is made overt? |
| Transcreation | How far is the title re-authored for equivalent effect? |

### 2.4 Toward an interdisciplinary framework

Because of these limitations, the article adopts an interdisciplinary framework rather than a narrowly linguistic one. Translation studies remains central, especially through the concepts of **domestication**, **foreignization**, **explicitation**, and **transcreation**. But film-title localization also requires **paratext theory**, since titles frame reception before viewing; **reception aesthetics**, because titles shape the horizon of expectations; **genre theory**, because titles pre-sell affect; **the sociology of cultural production**, because title outcomes are institutionally mediated; **intertextuality**, because titles carry textual memory; **glocalization**, because global media are regionally remade; and **transnational cinema**, because localized titles participate in the film’s global circulation.

## 3. Translation-Studies Framework

### 3.1 Domestication and foreignization

Any serious account of film-title localization must begin with the tension between **domestication** and **foreignization**. These concepts are useful not because they provide a complete theory of title translation, but because they establish the most basic orientation of the translated title toward its target audience. Domestication reduces perceived foreignness and makes the title more readily intelligible within the receiving culture. Foreignization, by contrast, preserves some source-side alterity, whether in wording, reference, ambiguity, or cultural texture. In practice, film-title translation tends to privilege domestication because titles operate under strong communicative and promotional pressure.

| Strategy | Orientation | Typical effect in film titles |
|---|---|---|
| Domestication | Target-oriented | Fluency, clarity, familiarity, local legibility |
| Foreignization | Source-oriented | Retained strangeness, ambiguity, cultural residue |

### 3.2 Explicitation as a commercial and interpretive strategy

Domestication and foreignization alone do not capture the full dynamics of title localization, because many titles are altered not only to sound more local but to become more **explicit**. **Explicitation** refers to a shift in which the target title makes information more overt than the source title did. This is particularly common in commercial film-title translation, where implicit English phrasing may be judged too opaque, too understated, or too culturally indirect for local marketing purposes.

| Type of explicitation | What becomes more overt |
|---|---|
| Genre explicitation | Horror, romance, action, sci-fi cues |
| Narrative explicitation | Plot premise, mission, conflict |
| Affective explicitation | Emotional stakes, intimacy, loss, danger |

### 3.3 Transcreation and the problem of lexical fidelity

If explicitation intensifies what is already latent in the source title, **transcreation** goes further by loosening the bond between source wording and target wording altogether. In transcreation, the objective is not lexical fidelity but equivalent or enhanced **effect**. The localized title is allowed to function as a new piece of persuasive language tailored to local audience expectations, genre habits, and commercial discourse.

| Approach | Primary goal | Risk | Reward |
|---|---|---|---|
| Literal rendering | Preserve wording | Flatness, opacity | Source recognizability |
| Explicitation | Clarify meaning | Over-determination | Faster uptake |
| Transcreation | Reproduce effect | Loss of source texture | Strong local impact |

### 3.4 How these strategies overlap in title localization

In practice, film titles are rarely produced by a single pure strategy. A localized title may be domesticated, explicit, and transcreative at the same time. It may also retain small foreignizing residues while still functioning mainly as a target-oriented threshold. This overlap is precisely why a more differentiated conceptual vocabulary is necessary.

## 4. Titles as Paratexts

### 4.1 Genette and the title as threshold

A productive way to understand film-title localization is to begin not with translation in the narrow linguistic sense, but with **paratext**. In Gérard Genette’s formulation, the paratext is a threshold: a zone that mediates between text and audience and shapes how the text is approached before it is fully encountered. The title is one of the most powerful parts of that threshold because it is usually the first verbal element through which a work becomes publicly knowable.

| Paratextual role of the title | Function before viewing |
|---|---|
| Identification | Gives the film a stable public name |
| Framing | Suggests how the film should be understood |
| Positioning | Places the film within genre and market space |
| Anticipation | Shapes emotional and interpretive expectation |

### 4.2 Why film titles behave differently from literary titles

Paratext theory also helps explain why film titles often behave differently from literary titles. A literary title can sustain opacity, allusion, understatement, or interpretive delay because literary reading allows time for ambiguity to unfold. A film title, by contrast, usually operates in a much more compressed and competitive environment. It appears on posters, multiplex listings, streaming interfaces, search results, trailers, press coverage, and social-media feeds. It must often communicate within seconds.

| Dimension | Literary title | Film title |
|---|---|---|
| Temporal horizon | Slow reading, delayed unfolding | Immediate recognition, instant uptake |
| Tolerance for opacity | Often high | More constrained |
| Promotional burden | Secondary or limited | Heavy and explicit |
| Market competition | Lower visibility pressure | High visibility pressure |

### 4.3 Titles within promotional ecologies: posters, trailers, platforms

A further implication of paratext theory is that the title never acts alone. It exists within a wider **promotional ecology** that includes posters, teaser images, trailers, distributor copy, ticketing interfaces, streaming thumbnails, reviews, hashtags, and fan discourse. The title is one node in this ecology, but it is an unusually dense node because it condenses the film into a short repeatable phrase.

### 4.4 Paratext and expectation management

The most important consequence of this framework is that titles function as tools of **expectation management**. They do not only identify films; they prepare viewers to receive them in certain ways. A title may foreground fear, urgency, intimacy, grandeur, melancholy, or mystery. It may narrow the audience’s interpretive range or preserve some openness. In either case, it helps establish what the viewer believes the film is before the film begins.

| Title tendency | Likely effect on expectation |
|---|---|
| Premise-explicit | Faster narrative and genre recognition |
| Tonally atmospheric | Stronger mood-based anticipation |
| Commercially vivid | Heightened affective and promotional intensity |
| Source-retentive | Greater ambiguity or foreign texture |

## 5. Regional Title-Localization Patterns in Greater China

### 5.1 Mainland China: legibility, standardization, and explicitation

Among the three markets, mainland China most often appears to favor titles that are **legible, standardized, and premise-forward**. This does not mean that all mainland titles are literal, nor that they are devoid of stylistic variation. Rather, the dominant tendency is toward making the film quickly intelligible within a broad commercial environment. Titles often reduce ambiguity, foreground genre, and render narrative stakes more explicit than the English source title does.

| Mainland tendency | Likely result |
|---|---|
| Premise clarification | Faster audience uptake |
| Genre marking | Clearer horror, romance, thriller, or sci-fi cues |
| Standardized diction | Broad-market readability |
| Reduced ambiguity | Lower interpretive openness, higher immediate accessibility |

### 5.2 Taiwan: tonal calibration, affect, and moderated domestication

Taiwan often occupies a more intermediate position. Titles are generally domesticated enough to remain accessible, but they are often less aggressively explanatory than their mainland counterparts. What stands out more strongly is **tonal calibration**. Taiwanese title localization often appears especially sensitive to mood, emotional register, and affective atmosphere.

| Taiwanese tendency | Likely result |
|---|---|
| Tonal sensitivity | Stronger mood and affective framing |
| Moderated explicitation | More room for emotional nuance |
| Balanced domestication | Accessibility without full flattening |
| Register calibration | Titles that sound smoother, softer, or more elegant |

### 5.3 Hong Kong: commercial vividness and transcreative retitling

Hong Kong is the most striking case for **transcreation**. Its title culture has long been associated with vivid, energetic, and commercially forceful retitling. In this market, a title may function almost like a miniature advertising slogan: it is designed not only to identify the film, but to grab attention, intensify genre cues, and maximize memorability.

| Hong Kong tendency | Likely result |
|---|---|
| Punchy retitling | Stronger commercial memorability |
| Heightened affect | More fear, urgency, danger, or spectacle |
| Colloquial vividness | Sharper local resonance |
| Lexical flexibility | Greater distance from source wording |

### 5.4 Comparative synthesis: three target-oriented systems, three logics

The three markets should not be arranged along a simplistic scale from “faithful” to “unfaithful.” All three are fundamentally **target-oriented**, but they operationalize target orientation differently. Mainland China tends toward **domestication through clarity and explicitation**. Taiwan tends toward **domestication moderated by tonal and affective calibration**. Hong Kong tends toward **domestication via transcreative commercial vividness**.

| Market | Dominant logic | Main payoff |
|---|---|---|
| Mainland China | Legibility + explicitation | Broad accessibility and immediate genre/premise recognition |
| Taiwan | Tonal calibration + moderated domestication | Emotional nuance and atmospheric framing |
| Hong Kong | Transcreation + commercial punch | Memorability, affective intensity, and market impact |

## 6. Glocalization, Regional Identity, and Cultural Politics

### 6.1 Hollywood as a global form locally remade

To understand film-title localization in Greater China, it is not enough to treat titles as isolated translation decisions. A more productive approach is to frame Hollywood as a **global cultural form** that is repeatedly remade through local acts of naming, framing, and circulation. This is where **glocalization** becomes useful. Hollywood does not arrive in mainland China, Taiwan, or Hong Kong as a stable symbolic object. It becomes legible through regionally specific thresholds that reshape its public identity.

| Concept | Relevance to title localization |
|---|---|
| Global form | Hollywood film circulates across markets |
| Local remaking | Title is adjusted to fit regional media logic |
| Glocalization | Global product acquires region-specific public identity |
| Threshold effect | The localized name becomes the first local interface |

### 6.2 Mainland, Taiwan, and Hong Kong as distinct Sinophone media cultures

A glocalization perspective also clarifies why “Chinese title translation” is too blunt a category. Mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong belong to overlapping Sinophone worlds, but they operate as distinct media cultures with different linguistic ecologies, audience expectations, and promotional traditions.

| Region | Frequently observed tendency | Cultural implication |
|---|---|---|
| Mainland China | clearer, more premise-forward wording | emphasis on legibility and standardization |
| Taiwan | more tonal or affective modulation | emphasis on mood and calibrated accessibility |
| Hong Kong | more adaptive or punchy retitling | emphasis on local expressivity and commercial impact |

### 6.3 Language politics, regional identity, and symbolic positioning

Once the three markets are treated as distinct media cultures, title localization can also be read as a form of **symbolic positioning**. It is not only that a film is renamed; rather, the renaming encodes assumptions about what counts as persuasive, elegant, familiar, or culturally resonant in each region.

### 6.4 Title localization as micro-evidence of glocalization

From this perspective, film titles provide unusually compact evidence for glocalization. A full film involves many layers of adaptation, censorship, publicity, and reception, but the title offers a concise and publicly visible trace of those processes.

## 7. Audience, Genre, and the Horizon of Expectations

### 7.1 Reception aesthetics and anticipatory framing

If paratext theory explains why titles matter before the film begins, **reception aesthetics** explains how they matter to viewers. The key concept here is **Hans Robert Jauss’s** “horizon of expectations”: audiences do not encounter works in a vacuum, but through historically formed anticipations shaped by previous texts, genre knowledge, cultural conventions, and publicity.

| Title tendency | Likely effect on the horizon of expectations |
|---|---|
| Premise-explicit | Faster narrative orientation, stronger genre certainty |
| Tonally atmospheric | Greater mood-based anticipation |
| Commercially vivid | Heightened expectation of sensation or spectacle |
| Source-retentive | More ambiguity, openness, or perceived foreignness |

### 7.2 Genre theory and affective pre-selling

Genre theory sharpens this argument by showing that titles do not merely identify films; they also help **sell genre as an emotional contract**. The title is one of the earliest and most compressed of those signals. It tells viewers not only what kind of film they are encountering, but what kind of affective experience they are about to buy into.

### 7.3 Action, horror, romance, and science fiction as contrasting cases

The value of genre theory becomes clearer when specific genre clusters are compared. In **action**, titles often benefit from rapid legibility. In **horror**, the title’s role in expectation management becomes even more pronounced. **Romance** and **science fiction** reveal different pressures: romance titles often work through emotional framing, while science-fiction titles oscillate between conceptual intelligibility and atmospheric or spectacular scale.

| Genre | Dominant anticipatory need | Common localization pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Action | velocity, conflict, danger | premise clarity or punchy spectacle |
| Horror | dread, threat, fear | affective amplification, sensational cues |
| Romance | intimacy, emotional stakes | tonal calibration, relational framing |
| Sci-fi | wonder, concept, scale | balance of explicitation and atmosphere |

### 7.4 How localized titles cue different viewing contracts

Taken together, reception aesthetics and genre theory suggest that localized titles function as **contracts of approach**. They do not determine interpretation absolutely, but they help cue the kind of attention, emotion, and expectation the film solicits.

## 8. Institutions, Intertextuality, and Field Effects

### 8.1 The sociology of cultural production: distributors, marketers, censors

If earlier sections have shown that localized film titles operate as paratexts and expectation-shaping devices, a further question follows: **who** produces these thresholds, and under what constraints? A useful answer comes from the sociology of cultural production. A localized title is best understood as an institutional compromise rather than a purely linguistic artifact.

| Institutional actor | Likely influence on title choice |
|---|---|
| Distributor | market positioning, audience reach, release strategy |
| Marketing team | memorability, affective pull, promotional fit |
| Censorship/regulatory environment | limits on wording, taboo, ideological sensitivity |
| Exhibitors/platforms | metadata legibility, thumbnail visibility, searchability |
| Franchise management | continuity with prior installments or brand identity |

### 8.2 Franchise branding and house style

The sociology of cultural production becomes even more visible in cases of **franchise branding** and **house style**. Not every film arrives as a blank slate. Some titles inherit prior naming conventions from earlier installments, bestselling novels, major stars, or distributor practices.

| Constraint type | Effect on localization |
|---|---|
| Franchise continuity | preserves recognizable naming pattern |
| Distributor house style | creates recurrent tonal or rhetorical preferences |
| Prestige branding | discourages overly lurid or colloquial retitling |
| Platform branding logic | favors shorter, cleaner, more searchable titles |

### 8.3 Intertextuality, allusion, and textual memory

Institutional mediation is only one side of the problem. The other is **intertextuality**. Film titles rarely function as purely denotative strings. Many derive part of their force from prior texts, idioms, scriptural echoes, literary memory, genre conventions, or franchise associations.

| Intertextual source | What may be at stake in translation |
|---|---|
| Idiom or proverb | culturally embedded resonance |
| Literary echo | prestige, canonical memory, tonal depth |
| Religious or scriptural phrase | symbolic seriousness or ambiguity |
| Franchise recall | serial continuity and audience recognition |

### 8.4 What is lost, displaced, or reinvented in localization

Once institutions and intertextuality are brought together, title localization can be seen as a process of selective **loss, displacement, and reinvention**. Some source textures are inevitably thinned, but localization can also produce gains.

| Localization outcome | Typical cause |
|---|---|
| Loss of ambiguity | pressure toward premise clarity |
| Weakening of allusion | low transferability of source reference |
| Reinvented affect | market need for stronger emotional hook |
| Stable franchise memory | value of serial recognizability |

## 9. Transnational Cinema and the Global Life of Titles

### 9.1 Beyond source versus target

A transnational-cinema perspective makes it possible to move beyond the simple model in which Hollywood is the stable source and mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong are merely target markets receiving translated titles. In practice, film titles participate in the ongoing public construction of a film as it circulates internationally.

| Simplified model | More transnational model |
|---|---|
| Stable source → translated target | Film identity continuously reshaped in circulation |
| Translation as secondary transfer | Localization as constitutive public remaking |
| One original name, many equivalents | Many regionally active title identities |

### 9.2 Localized titles as part of Hollywood’s transnational remaking

Seen in this light, localized titles do not merely interpret Hollywood for local audiences; they help **remake Hollywood itself** as a transnational cultural formation.

| Market-level title effect | Transnational implication |
|---|---|
| Greater premise clarity | Hollywood appears more narratively legible |
| Greater tonal emphasis | Hollywood appears more mood-driven or emotionally refined |
| Greater commercial vividness | Hollywood appears more spectacular or sensational |

### 9.3 Uneven circulation, regional thresholds, and global reception

A transnational-cinema framework also highlights the **unevenness** of circulation. Films do not enter all markets under identical conditions. They move through different censorship systems, exhibition structures, publicity cultures, language politics, and platform ecologies.

| Regional threshold | Possible effect on reception |
|---|---|
| Premise-forward threshold | quicker genre recognition, broader immediate accessibility |
| Tonally calibrated threshold | stronger mood-oriented reception, softer framing |
| Punchy transcreative threshold | higher affective intensity, sharper promotional impact |

### 9.4 Implications for film studies and translation studies

The transnational perspective has implications for both film studies and translation studies. Titles should be treated as part of the film’s international life rather than as marginal accessories to distribution.

| Discipline | What title localization reveals |
|---|---|
| Film studies | films acquire multiple public identities in circulation |
| Translation studies | translation is constitutive, not merely derivative |
| Cultural/media studies | global media are remade through local thresholds |

## 10. Conclusion

### 10.1 Synthesis of the main findings

The preceding discussion shows that Hollywood film-title localization in mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong cannot be adequately explained by a simple distinction between literal and free translation. Titles are **paratextual thresholds**, **promotional instruments**, and **regionally coded interfaces** through which films enter public life.

| Market | Dominant tendency | Main effect |
|---|---|---|
| Mainland China | Domestication + explicitation | legibility, premise clarity |
| Taiwan | Moderated domestication + tonal calibration | mood, affective nuance |
| Hong Kong | Domestication via transcreation | vividness, memorability, impact |

### 10.2 Theoretical contribution of an interdisciplinary approach

The article’s main theoretical contribution lies in showing that film-title localization becomes much more intelligible when treated as an **interdisciplinary problem**. Translation studies explains operative mechanisms, while paratext, glocalization, reception aesthetics, genre theory, sociology of cultural production, intertextuality, and transnational cinema explain the wider conditions under which those mechanisms become persuasive.

### 10.3 Limits of the present framework

The framework developed here is intentionally synthetic, and that synthesis creates several limitations. Regional tendencies are recurring logics rather than universal rules, theoretical breadth risks diffuseness, and the essay remains more conceptual than fully corpus-driven.

| Limitation | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Regional generalization | not every title fits the dominant pattern |
| Theoretical breadth | risks diffuseness if not tightly organized |
| Limited corpus demonstration | reduces precision about frequency and change over time |

### 10.4 Future directions: corpus studies, genre comparisons, platform-era shifts

Several future directions follow naturally from this review: a coded corpus by decade and genre, closer genre-specific comparison, attention to the platform era, and more direct audience-reception work.
