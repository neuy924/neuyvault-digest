# Three Proposed Titles

1. **Regional Food, Power, and Identity in the Philippines: Colonial Histories, Culinary Difference, and the Politics of Taste**
2. **Rethinking Filipino Cuisine: Region, Coloniality, and the Making of a National Canon**
3. **Unequal Tables: Philippine Regional Food, Hegemony, and the Politics of Belonging**

# Abstract

This review article rethinks “Filipino cuisine” as a plural, historically layered, and unevenly valued culinary field rather than a single unified national tradition. It argues that Philippine food emerged through long processes of interaction among Austronesian-maritime foodways, Chinese—especially Fujian/Hokkien—commercial influence, Spanish colonial rule, and American domestic modernity. Rather than treating these influences as a simple sequence of borrowings, the article interprets them through **creolization**, emphasizing indigenization, transformation, and regional differentiation. From this perspective, the national cuisine is not a neutral inventory of dishes but the contingent outcome of unequal processes of canon formation.

The article pays particular attention to regional variation within the Philippines, comparing the Manila–Tagalog–Kapampangan colonial core with Ilocano, Bicolano, Visayan, Maranao, and broader Bangsamoro food worlds. It argues that the Manila–Tagalog corridor functioned as a contact zone and nationalizing center, while Pampanga became a prestige kitchen whose culinary authority was closely tied to colonial proximity and elite hospitality. By contrast, other regional cuisines remained differently positioned in relation to the national canon: some as local heritage, some as distributed regional systems, and some—especially Maranao and Bangsamoro foodways—as morally and politically distinct formations structured by halal, minority visibility, and autonomy. These differences are analyzed through **hegemony** and **internal colonialism**, which clarify why some foodways became nationally normative while others remained marked as regional, ethnic, or peripheral.

The article also examines culinary language as a historical archive of contact and hierarchy. Hokkien loanwords in Tagalog show how food vocabularies record unequal historical incorporation, while regional naming systems in Ilocano and the Visayan sphere demonstrate the persistence of classificatory autonomy. Finally, the article extends beyond discourse and canon formation to explore **embodiment, taboo, and sensory politics**. Drawing on Bourdieu and Mary Douglas, it distinguishes between food boundaries organized by classed disgust and those organized by moral law, especially in relation to halal. The result is a model of Philippine cuisine as a field of overlapping but unequal food worlds, structured by history, region, class, religion, and bodily memory.

# 1. Introduction: Rethinking “Filipino Cuisine”

To speak of “Filipino cuisine” as though it were a single, stable culinary object is immediately useful and immediately misleading. Useful, because the term names a recognizable national field of food practices, tastes, and emblematic dishes; misleading, because the apparent unity of that field conceals deep regional, linguistic, religious, and historical differences. What is often presented as a coherent national cuisine is better understood as a layered and uneven formation produced by long interaction among Austronesian foodways, Chinese commercial networks, Spanish colonial rule, American modernity, and the persistent internal differentiation of the archipelago itself. In theoretical terms, this is not a simple narrative of borrowing but one of **creolization**: culinary forms, ingredients, and vocabularies were continuously absorbed, localized, and transformed rather than merely copied. The Philippines is not a culinary monoculture but a densely plural food world, and any serious account of its cuisine must begin from that plurality rather than treat it as a secondary complication.

This review proceeds from the premise that cuisine is not only a matter of ingredients, recipes, or taste combinations. Cuisine is also a social classification system, a historical archive, and a political field. It records patterns of migration, trade, colonial encounter, religious difference, and regional hierarchy. It reveals which tastes come to stand for refinement, which foods are coded as ordinary or provincial, and which culinary traditions are excluded from the national center even while being celebrated as local heritage. For this reason, the problem is not merely to describe Filipino food, but to ask how certain foods and regions came to represent the nation, while others were pushed to the margins or understood as special cases. In that sense, the study of Filipino cuisine is inseparable from the study of canon formation and **hegemony**: the process by which the tastes and foodways of dominant groups come to appear natural, universal, and nationally representative.

A further complication is that the national label “Filipino” compresses multiple scales of identity. Some culinary formations are best understood at the level of the nation; others at the level of ethnolinguistic group, region, city, religious community, or historical contact zone. Manila food culture, for instance, cannot be read in quite the same way as Ilocano foodways, Bicolano cuisine, or Maranao halal practice. Even the terms used to identify culinary worlds differ in scale and structure: Ilocano and Maranao refer to specific ethnolinguistic communities; Bicolano refers to a regional-linguistic formation; the Visayan sphere refers to a broad cluster of related languages and peoples rather than a single homogeneous identity; Bangsamoro operates both as a historical Muslim umbrella and as a contemporary autonomous political framework. The culinary map of the Philippines is therefore inseparable from a map of overlapping social and territorial formations. Read through the lens of **internal colonialism**, this also means that relations among these food worlds are not horizontal or equal: some regions become central to the definition of the nation, while others are positioned as peripheral, minority, or exceptional.

A national cuisine, then, is not simply “there” waiting to be described. It is assembled through repetition, prestige, circulation, media, schooling, tourism, and state discourse. Some foods become nationally legible because they emerge from metropolitan centers, because they fit dominant ideas of festivity or domestic respectability, or because they are easier to translate into the language of heritage. Other foods remain legible mainly as regional, minority, rustic, or specialized traditions. This asymmetry is especially important in the Philippine case, where the Manila–Tagalog–Kapampangan axis has exercised outsized influence on what later appeared as standard or representative Filipino food. To rethink Filipino cuisine, therefore, is not to deny national coherence altogether, but to interrogate the historical process by which some local foodways acquired national authority. That process was not neutral; it was structured by colonial prestige, class formation, and what later became a regime of **bourgeois domesticity**, in which hygiene, order, moderation, and middle-class household norms shaped food respectability.

## 1.1. The problem of a singular national cuisine

The idea of a singular Filipino cuisine often carries an implicit narrative of synthesis: a little Malay, a little Chinese, a little Spanish, a little American, blended into one national whole. While this formulation captures something real about historical layering, it risks smoothing over two essential facts. First, the layers did not arrive evenly across the archipelago. Second, they were not absorbed into an empty container, but into already differentiated local worlds with their own ecologies, ingredients, vocabularies, and social meanings. A cuisine formed in a colonial port-city environment does not simply scale up to represent the whole archipelago. Nor should food traditions shaped by Muslim southern histories be read as mere regional variants of a lowland Christian norm. The language of **creolization** is useful here precisely because it emphasizes transformation through local encounter, not the passive accumulation of foreign elements.

The problem of singularity is thus not only empirical but historiographic. A unified national cuisine is often the retrospective effect of nation-building rather than its culinary starting point. What comes to count as “Filipino food” is usually a curated selection of dishes, flavor profiles, and symbolic forms capable of traveling across regions and representing the nation to itself and to outsiders. Such a selection almost inevitably privileges some histories over others. In the Philippine case, foods associated with Manila, Tagalog urban life, Kapampangan prestige, fiesta culture, and colonial lowland Christian society have often been especially visible. Meanwhile, Ilocano, Bicolano, Visayan, Maranao, and Bangsamoro foodways may be acknowledged as important, but often under the category of the regional, the ethnic, or the local specialty rather than as coequal foundations of the national food imagination. This is a problem of **hegemony**: one culinary center acquires the power to define what seems ordinary, prestigious, and national.

This does not mean that a national cuisine is false. Rather, it means that it is produced through hierarchy. “Filipino cuisine” is best understood not as a neutral inventory of dishes but as a field in which representation is uneven. The question, then, is not whether Filipino cuisine exists, but how it has been assembled, who has been centered in that process, and which culinary traditions remain only partially incorporated into the national canon. Put differently, the singular national cuisine is less an origin than a political and cultural achievement—one built through selection, repetition, and unequal visibility.

## 1.2. From comparative cuisine to regional analysis

One useful entry point into this problem is comparison. Comparing Filipino cuisine with Malay and Chinese cuisines clarifies broad historical affinities, especially the Austronesian-maritime substrate shared with island Southeast Asia and the deep Chinese, particularly Fujian/Hokkien, imprint on food forms and vocabulary. Such comparison helps identify major structures: the sour-salty-braised logic often associated with Filipino food, the aromatic and coconut-rich layering common in many Malay food systems, and the noodle, soy, bun, and wrapper traditions linked to southern Chinese commercial networks. Yet comparison alone has limits. If used too broadly, it risks turning “Filipino cuisine” into a static national unit facing equally static neighbors.

The more revealing move is therefore to shift from external comparison to internal differentiation. Once attention moves inside the Philippines, the national category opens into multiple culinary regions with different historical trajectories. The Manila–Tagalog corridor reveals one kind of story: urban contact, commercial centrality, Hokkien lexical influence, and later nationalizing power. Pampanga reveals another: proximity to the colonial center, agricultural wealth, and the making of a prestige cuisine. Ilocos points toward frugality, fermentation, and strong northern identity. Bicol foregrounds coconut, chili, and regional flavor intensity. The Visayan sphere complicates any simple regional map by containing multiple related yet distinct culinary worlds. Maranao and Bangsamoro foodways introduce a further shift, bringing halal, Muslim sociality, and minority politics to the center of analysis. Here the concept of **internal colonialism** becomes especially useful, because it helps explain why not all regional cuisines enter the national frame under equal conditions: some are normalized, others folklorized, and others marked as religiously or politically outside.

Regional analysis therefore does more than add local color. It changes the scale of the question. Instead of asking what Filipino cuisine is in the singular, we begin to ask how different regional food systems entered into unequal relations with one another, and how some became central to the imagination of the nation while others remained peripheral, parallel, or oppositional. Comparison thus becomes more powerful when it is brought inward, toward the uneven spatial and political structure of the archipelago itself.

## 1.3. Review aims, scope, and key questions

This review aims to synthesize these perspectives into a historically and theoretically informed account of Philippine food. Its purpose is not to provide an encyclopedic survey of dishes, but to develop an interpretive framework for understanding how cuisine, region, language, class, religion, and colonial history intersect. The article begins from the long historical formation of Philippine food, including Austronesian foundations, Chinese contact, and Spanish and American colonial transformations. It then turns to regionalization within the Philippines, with particular attention to Tagalog/Manila, Kapampangan, Ilocano, Bicolano, the Visayan sphere, Maranao, and Bangsamoro contexts. A further line of inquiry concerns food vocabulary and culinary classification, especially the importance of Hokkien loanwords in Tagalog and the persistence of regionally distinct naming systems elsewhere.

Beyond historical description, the review asks a set of broader analytical questions. How did the colonial center shape the later national food canon? In what ways were external culinary influences indigenized rather than simply copied? How do religion and halal discipline produce a different culinary grammar from that of the lowland Christian mainstream? How do class, disgust, and **bourgeois domesticity** influence which regional foods come to be seen as refined, proper, or nationally representative? And how might embodiment, taboo, and sensory politics complicate a purely discursive account of cuisine by showing how food hierarchies are lived through the body? These questions are designed to keep the discussion moving between material history and symbolic order, between the circulation of ingredients and the production of legitimacy.

The central argument that emerges is that Philippine cuisine is best understood as a plural and contested field rather than a unified tradition. Its apparent national coherence is real but historically produced. It rests on layered processes of **creolization**, on the **hegemonic** power of the colonial and postcolonial center, and on the continued negotiation of regional, religious, and minority food worlds that do not fit neatly into one canonical model. To rethink Filipino cuisine, then, is to move from inventory to structure, from recipe to history, and from the language of national essence to the language of unequal but interconnected food worlds.

# 2. Historical Foundations: Malay Matrix, Chinese Contact, Colonial Layering

Any serious account of Philippine food must begin from history, but not from history understood as a neat succession of external influences. Philippine cuisine was not built by adding one civilizational layer after another onto a passive local base. It emerged through long processes of encounter, adaptation, and unequal incorporation. The most useful starting point is the idea of a precolonial Austronesian or maritime Southeast Asian substrate—a broad food world shaped by rice, fish, preservation, souring, coconuts, root crops, and coastal exchange. Yet even this “base” was not a static origin. It was itself part of a regional system of mobility and circulation stretching across island Southeast Asia. What later became “Filipino cuisine” therefore began not as isolation but as relation.

To describe this early substrate as a **Malay matrix** is analytically helpful so long as the phrase is not taken too literally or ethnically. It does not imply that Philippine food was simply Malay in the narrow national sense. Rather, it points to a wider maritime and Austronesian field in which ingredients, techniques, and broad taste structures circulated. Rice-centered meals, seafood reliance, souring techniques, preserved condiments, and coconut use all belong to this older regional ecology. In theoretical terms, this stage matters because it prevents later Chinese, Spanish, or American influences from being mistaken for absolute beginnings. The local was already regional before it was colonial. Even at this early point, then, the history of Philippine food is better understood through **mobility and interaction** than through sealed origins.

The next major historical formation came through long Chinese contact, especially from **Fujian/Hokkien networks**, later joined in more selective ways by **Cantonese** influences. This contact did not simply import a ready-made Chinese cuisine into the Philippines. Instead, it introduced forms, ingredients, commercial routines, and vocabularies that were selectively absorbed into Philippine food worlds, especially in urban and port-oriented contexts. Noodles, soy-based seasoning, buns, wrappers, bakery goods, and culinary terminology entered into the archipelago through these trade and settlement networks. Here **creolization** becomes a crucial term again: Chinese forms remained recognizable, but they were reworked through local ingredients, taste preferences, and household uses. What mattered was not replication, but indigenized transformation.

Spanish colonialism altered this field more sharply by reorganizing not only food consumption, but also ritual, class prestige, and the spatial hierarchy of the archipelago. Under Spanish rule, lowland Christian society was integrated into a new order of parish, town, feast, tribute, and elite mediation. Food became part of that order. Certain dishes and modes of hospitality acquired prestige because they were linked to church calendars, fiesta culture, elite households, and proximity to colonial authority. Spanish rule also intensified the transpacific circulation of ingredients and techniques through the Manila galleon world, making colonial Philippine food not merely Iberianized but connected to a broader Spanish imperial ecology that included Mexico and the Americas. The result was not a simple Hispanization, but another stage of selective **creolization**, in which foreign prestige forms were localized and indigenized.

American rule then introduced a different regime of culinary transformation. If Spanish colonialism had elevated feast, ritual, and elite hospitality, American colonialism increasingly valued hygiene, nutrition, schooling, domestic science, refrigeration, and packaged consumer modernity. Food prestige was gradually recoded through the norms of **bourgeois domesticity**: the orderly kitchen, the clean table, the rational household, the disciplined mother, the modern appliance. This did not erase earlier layers, but it altered the moral language in which food respectability was judged. The center of gravity shifted from aristocratic or ecclesiastical prestige toward middle-class domestic order and technologized consumption. In this sense, the history of Philippine food is not only about ingredients and dishes, but about successive regimes of legitimacy.

This layered history is important because it shows why the national cuisine cannot be read as a simple synthesis. Each layer entered under different social conditions, through different routes, and with different consequences for class, region, and cultural authority. The Austronesian-maritime substrate supplied ecological and structural continuity. Chinese contact supplied commercially mediated forms and vocabularies. Spanish colonialism restructured prestige and ritual hierarchy. American colonialism recoded domestic respectability and middle-class aspiration. What later appeared as “Filipino cuisine” was produced through the interaction of all these forces, but not on equal terms. Some regions became privileged sites of incorporation, while others remained differently positioned within the evolving culinary order. This is why historical formation must be linked not only to influence, but to **hegemony**: the power of some layers and regions to define the meaning of the national.

## 2.1. Austronesian and maritime Southeast Asian foundations

Before Chinese trade and European empire, the Philippines already belonged to a wider maritime Southeast Asian world. The most important point here is not to reconstruct a pure precolonial cuisine, but to identify durable structures that continued to shape later food history. These include rice as staple and social anchor, fish and seafood as everyday proteins, the widespread use of souring agents, the importance of drying, fermenting, smoking, and salting, and the coexistence of cultivated crops with foraged or semi-wild ingredients. These are not marginal details; they are the deep grammar of the food system from which later regional cuisines grew.

Seen this way, the Philippines shared important continuities with island Southeast Asia more broadly. The archipelago belonged to a world of coastal trade, boat mobility, and culinary pragmatism, where preservation techniques were essential and taste was tied closely to ecology. Sourness, for instance, should not be understood simply as flavor preference but as part of a broader practical and sensory strategy in hot, humid, maritime environments. Fish sauces and preserved condiments likewise belong to this older ecology of storage, transport, and protein extension. The significance of this layer is that it undercuts any account of Philippine food that begins with colonialism as if history started only with Europe or China.

The phrase **Malay matrix** is therefore best read as a heuristic for this older maritime field. It indicates a shared Austronesian-regional substrate without collapsing all variation into a single ethnic narrative. In theoretical terms, this early layer complicates later stories of external “influence,” because it reminds us that Philippine foodways were already products of translocal circulation before colonial empire. The local was from the beginning relational, ecological, and mobile. That does not eliminate specificity; it explains how specificity was historically formed.

This substrate also helps explain why some elements of later Philippine cuisine feel structurally closer to Southeast Asian foodways than to continental East Asian or European ones. Even when Chinese noodles or Spanish stews entered the archipelago, they were absorbed into a food world still anchored by rice, seafood, souring, and local condiments. In other words, later influences were not layered onto a blank surface; they were refracted through an already existing regional sensory order. That is precisely why **creolization** is more useful than diffusion as a model of change.

## 2.2. Fujian/Hokkien and Cantonese influences

The Chinese layer in Philippine food history is best approached not through “Chinese cuisine” in the abstract, but through the specific southern Chinese networks most relevant to the archipelago. Above all, this means **Fujian/Hokkien** contact, with **Cantonese** influence arriving later and more selectively. This distinction matters because it shifts the discussion from vague civilizational borrowing to concrete trade and migration channels. Southern Fujian merchants and settlers were especially important in Manila and related commercial zones, and their imprint can be seen not only in dishes and ingredients but in food vocabulary itself.

The most enduring consequences of this contact lie in **forms and food grammar**. Noodle dishes, wrappers, buns, soy-based seasoning logics, bakery products, and minced or filled snack forms all entered the Philippine food world through southern Chinese commercial and culinary channels. Many familiar Filipino foods associated with pancit, lumpia, siopao, hopia, and related categories are best understood through this history. Yet their significance does not lie in proving foreign origin for its own sake. What matters is how these forms were localized—how fillings changed, sauces shifted, textures adapted, and meanings moved from mercantile or urban Chinese contexts into broader Filipino household and street-food ecologies.

This is a classic case of **creolization**. Chinese contact did not displace the earlier Austronesian-maritime substrate; it supplied forms that became naturalized within it. A noodle dish in the Philippines did not remain identical to a Fujian original. A bun or spring-roll form did not remain socially tied to an immigrant enclave. Over time, these foods entered the everyday grammar of urban and lowland life, especially in the Manila-facing world. The linguistic evidence is especially important here, because the density of Hokkien-derived culinary vocabulary in Tagalog suggests that contact was not superficial. It shaped not only menus but classification itself—how ingredients and food types were named and organized.

Cantonese influence, while real, operated differently. It is often more visible in restaurant, dim sum, roast-meat, or urban commercial settings than in the deepest household layer of Filipino food. This makes the Chinese contribution historically uneven even within itself. The dominant point, however, remains that the Chinese imprint in the Philippines was neither generic nor uniform. It was regionally specific, commercially mediated, and unequally absorbed. This matters later for **hegemony**, because regions most exposed to Chinese commercial urbanity—especially the Manila–Tagalog corridor—were also better positioned to shape what would later become nationally visible food culture.

## 2.3. Spanish and American colonial transformations

Spanish colonialism changed Philippine food not simply by adding European dishes, but by reorganizing the social order in which food acquired value. Under Spanish rule, lowland Christian society was integrated into a regime of towns, parishes, fiestas, tribute, and mediated elite authority. Food acquired symbolic weight within this structure. Feasting, hospitality, meat abundance, celebratory dishes, sweets, and elaborate preparations became associated with public ritual and social prestige. This was not merely culinary change; it was a transformation in the politics of display. Certain kinds of food became legible as honorable, festive, and status-bearing because they were embedded in colonial Catholic ritual and elite sociability.

Spanish influence also worked through the **transpacific imperial system**. Philippine food history under Spain cannot be reduced to Iberian transfer alone, because the colony was connected to New Spain and the broader Pacific circulation of crops, techniques, and tastes. This helps explain why “Spanish” influence in the Philippines often already carried American and Mexican dimensions. Colonial food, then, was neither a direct copy of Spain nor a simple local residue. It was a creolized imperial formation in which local cooks, ingredients, and tastes reworked imported prestige forms. The result was the consolidation of a lowland Christian culinary center tied to fiesta culture, elite houses, and the symbolic economy of the colonial town.

American rule altered this order by privileging a different kind of legitimacy. If Spanish prestige centered on feast, ritual, and patronal hospitality, American prestige centered increasingly on **cleanliness, nutrition, rational management, education, and modern domestic order**. This is where the concept of **bourgeois domesticity** becomes indispensable. Food respectability came to depend not only on the dish itself, but on the household practices surrounding it: refrigeration, canned goods, measured cooking, hygienic kitchens, scientifically informed motherhood, and the moral pedagogy of proper domestic life. This was not just a shift in cuisine, but in the disciplined production of middle-class subjectivity.

Under American influence, the kitchen became more explicitly a site of instruction, modernization, and consumer aspiration. The valued home was clean, efficient, regulated, and technologically updated. Packaged and refrigerated foods could signal advancement. Nutritional reasoning and domestic science entered food discourse alongside earlier ideas of abundance and celebration. The shift here was profound: Spanish colonial food prestige often turned on public ritual and elite hospitality; American colonial food prestige increasingly turned on private household order and middle-class competence. Both systems were hierarchical, but they legitimized food differently.

The combined result was a double historical inheritance. The lowland Christian core retained the prestige of fiesta, ceremony, and layered colonial dishes, while also absorbing the newer prestige of hygiene, appliances, and respectable domestic modernity. This double inheritance became central to the making of the later national canon. Some regional cuisines could be more easily incorporated into that canon because they fit, or could be made to fit, these dominant standards. Others—especially cuisines marked by strong local bitterness, pungency, halal rules, or minority identities—entered national discourse under more unequal conditions. Here the historical story passes directly into **hegemony** and **internal colonialism**: colonial transformations did not produce one common national culinary field, but a stratified one.

# 3. Regionalization Within the Philippines

If the previous section established that Philippine cuisine emerged through layered historical contact, this section argues that those layers did **not** settle evenly across the archipelago. The national field of “Filipino cuisine” is therefore not only historically mixed but **regionally stratified**. Some areas became privileged sites of incorporation, prestige, and national legibility; others retained stronger local culinary logics or entered the national imagination only as regional specialties, minority foodways, or exoticized margins. This is where the concepts of **hegemony** and **internal colonialism** become especially useful. Hegemony helps explain how certain regional cuisines came to appear ordinary, prestigious, and nationally representative, while internal colonialism helps explain why some regions were incorporated into the national frame under unequal terms, especially along lowland-Christian versus Muslim-southern lines.

Regionalization in the Philippines is not simply a matter of different ingredients or recipes. It also reflects different **historical contact zones**. The Manila–Tagalog corridor was shaped by port-city commercial exchange and dense Hokkien lexical and culinary influence; Pampanga developed as an agriculturally rich and politically proximate prestige zone; northern regions such as Ilocos retained strong local agrarian and fermented food identities; Bicol developed a powerful coconut-and-chili profile; the Visayan sphere preserved multiple related but distinct naming systems and culinary worlds; and Maranao/Bangsamoro foodways remained structured by halal, Muslim sociality, and southern political history rather than by the lowland-Christian colonial mainstream. What matters here is not to turn each region into a sealed culinary box, but to show that “Filipino cuisine” is best understood as a field of **unequal regional formations** rather than one evenly shared tradition.

This regional unevenness also changes how we think about national cuisine. A national canon is often narrated as though it simply gathers the best of the regions. In practice, however, some regions contribute to the canon from positions of **centrality**, while others contribute from positions of **folklorization** or **minority marking**. The Manila–Tagalog–Kapampangan axis has exercised particular power in this regard. Manila was not only the colonial port and later capital, but also a linguistic and media center through which certain foods became nationally normalized. Pampanga, meanwhile, acquired a reputation for culinary excellence closely tied to politics, moral discourse, and prestige. By contrast, southern Muslim foodways such as Maranao and broader Bangsamoro cuisines entered national discourse under different signs: halal, minority identity, interreligious encounter, and more recently regional autonomy. The result is not a neutral map of diversity, but a hierarchy of visibility.

## 3.1. Tagalog/Manila and Kapampangan as the colonial core

The **Tagalog/Manila corridor** occupies a special place because it functioned simultaneously as **contact zone, commercial center, colonial capital, and later nationalizing center**. Binondo, in the heart of Manila, is widely recognized as the oldest Chinatown in the world and as a major center of Chinese-Filipino culinary fusion, which underscores how central Manila was to the naturalization of noodle dishes, buns, wrappers, soy-based seasoning logics, and Chinese-derived urban food culture. The linguistic evidence strengthens this point: Gloria Chan-Yap’s study of Hokkien influence on Tagalog cookery shows that culinary vocabulary in Tagalog records deep, structured borrowing rather than superficial contact. Food words here are historical evidence of Manila’s role as a place where commerce, language, and cuisine intertwined.

In theoretical terms, Manila illustrates **hegemony through normalization**. Foods and terms that emerged in an urban-commercial, Tagalog-facing environment were more likely to circulate outward as “Filipino” rather than remain marked as local. Pancit, bakery culture, Chinese-Filipino snack forms, and market foods could become ordinary and nationally legible precisely because they were mediated through the capital. This is not to say that Manila invented national cuisine by itself, but that its centrality let it function as a **filter and amplifier** through which some culinary forms became canonical. What later appeared as the national mainstream was therefore partly the sedimented outcome of urban centrality.

**Kapampangan cuisine** occupies a related but distinct position. If Manila was a contact zone and normalizing center, Pampanga became one of the nation’s chief **prestige kitchens**. Scholarship on Kapampangan cuisine explicitly links food to politics and moral discourse, showing that Kapampangan cooking is not merely a regional recipe collection but a field through which citizenship, child-rearing, democracy, and social values are imagined. This makes Pampanga especially important for the making of the national canon, because it supplied not only dishes but also a discourse of culinary seriousness and excellence. Its reputation as the country’s “culinary capital” is therefore not just about flavor; it is about historical proximity to power, agricultural wealth, and the conversion of regional cooking into national prestige.

Together, Manila and Pampanga formed the **colonial core of culinary authority**. Manila contributed circulation, lexical absorption, and commercial normalization; Pampanga contributed ceremonial prestige and moralized culinary expertise. This division is analytically useful because it shows two modes of hegemony at work: the ordinary power of the capital to define what is common, and the prestige power of a favored region to define what is excellent. Seen this way, the national canon did not arise from regional equality. It emerged from a center-weighted structure in which Manila and Pampanga were disproportionately able to stand in for the nation.

## 3.2. Ilocano, Bicolano, and the Visayan sphere

Outside the colonial core, regional cuisines remained highly differentiated. **Ilocano foodways** are often associated with frugality, fermentation, vegetables, preserved ingredients, and strong local identity. While the current sources used here are less direct on Ilocano theory than on Tagalog or Kapampangan food culture, the broader point remains that northern cuisines were not organized around the same metropolitan-commercial logic as Manila or the same prestige discourse as Pampanga. Rather, they reflect a different ecological and social history—one tied more closely to agrarian pragmatics and regionally anchored taste. In the framework of **hegemony**, Ilocano cuisine is important precisely because it shows that the national mainstream did not erase strong northern sensory worlds, even if it often subordinated them to the category of the regional.

**Bicolano cuisine**, by contrast, is strongly legible through its coconut-rich and chili-forward profile, but its significance extends beyond flavor. Studies of Albay and Bicol cuisine show that food has been central to local identity formation and that women’s labor and family transmission are key to its reproduction. One study found that Bicolano cuisines contribute to local cultural identity while also functioning as livelihood and family legacy, especially through women’s role in income augmentation and recipe inheritance. In theoretical terms, Bicol demonstrates that regional cuisine is not only a sensory field but also a **gendered social institution**. It is reproduced through domestic labor, kinship, and small-scale economic strategies, which means that regional distinctiveness is sustained materially as well as symbolically.

This makes Bicol analytically significant for two reasons. First, it shows how a strong regional sensory profile can coexist with a strong local labor structure. Second, it reveals how regional food can be both **authenticity marker** and **marketable heritage**. In postcolonial terms, Bicol is a clear example of how local cuisines can be celebrated for difference while the domestic and gendered work that maintains them remains less visible. Regional identity here is not merely declared; it is continually cooked, sold, and inherited.

The **Visayan sphere** requires a different approach because it is not one cuisine or one people, but a broad cluster of related language communities spread across the central Philippines and beyond. This means that any attempt to speak of “Visayan cuisine” as singular risks repeating at a smaller scale the same problem posed by “Filipino cuisine” at the national scale. The more useful point is that the Visayan sphere preserves multiple local naming systems, dish taxonomies, and culinary identities that resist easy consolidation into one regional brand. This internal plurality is itself significant: it reminds us that regionalization is not always toward a single coherent culinary region but can also take the form of a **distributed cultural field**.

In relation to the national canon, the Visayan sphere often appears less as a single prestigious center and more as a set of internally varied regional worlds. This makes it harder for any one Visayan culinary logic to dominate national representation in the way Tagalog/Manila or Kapampangan foodways have done. Yet that very diffuseness is analytically important. It shows that the Philippine culinary map cannot be reduced to a simple center-versus-region binary; some regions are themselves internally plural and therefore less easily canonized. In this sense, the Visayan sphere illustrates the limits of national and regional simplification alike.

## 3.3. Maranao and Bangsamoro food worlds

The most important break with the lowland-Christian colonial core appears in **Maranao and broader Bangsamoro food worlds**. Here the issue is not simply regional variation in ingredients or flavor, but a distinct organization of food around **halal, Muslim sociality, and minority political history**. Tallara’s study of Maranao-Muslim food culture in Quiapo shows that Maranao restaurants in Manila operate as more than places to eat: they are sites of interreligious encounter, minority visibility, and negotiated commensality in a predominantly Catholic urban environment. They serve only halal-permissible foods and thereby make Muslim moral order publicly present within the capital.

This makes Maranao cuisine analytically different from cuisines that are primarily sorted through **classed taste** or regional prestige. Here food is centrally about **taboo, purity, trust, and lawful ingestion**. In Mary Douglas’s terms, these are questions of boundary and order, not simply preference. In the Philippine context, this means Maranao food cannot be adequately understood as just another “regional cuisine.” It belongs to a different moral and social grammar—one that positions the body, the kitchen, and commensality within Islamic discipline. That difference becomes particularly visible in a city like Manila, where halal practice marks both minority identity and a claim to public legitimacy.

The term **Bangsamoro** expands this frame from one ethnolinguistic community to a broader political and civilizational umbrella. Bangsamoro does not refer to a single cuisine but to a field of Muslim southern foodways now increasingly articulated through autonomous regional institutions and halal development initiatives. This matters because it shifts food from the level of minority survival to that of **regional state-making**. In theoretical terms, Bangsamoro cuisine can therefore be read as a **counter-canon**: not merely a regional supplement to the Manila-centered national mainstream, but an alternative culinary formation anchored in different principles of legitimacy, belonging, and public order.

Read through **internal colonialism**, the contrast becomes sharper. Lowland Christian cuisines associated with Manila and Pampanga were long better positioned to define the national norm, while Muslim southern cuisines were more often rendered peripheral, specialized, or politically marked. Yet precisely because of this history, Maranao and Bangsamoro food worlds carry special analytical force. They expose the limits of any national canon that assumes one dominant civilizational center. They insist that the Philippine culinary field contains not just regional variants but also competing normative orders—different ways of organizing food, morality, and belonging. This is why regionalization in the Philippines must be understood not only as culinary diversity, but as an unequal political geography of food.

# 4. Language, Vocabulary, and Culinary Classification

If cuisine is a historical archive, then **food vocabulary is one of its most revealing records**. Culinary language preserves traces of trade, migration, prestige, and everyday contact that may be less visible when one looks only at finished dishes. In the Philippine case, food words show that culinary influence did not spread uniformly across the archipelago. Some language zones—especially **Tagalog in the Manila-facing corridor**—absorbed dense layers of Hokkien-derived vocabulary, while other regions preserved stronger local naming systems and semantic worlds. This means that the history of Philippine food is not only about ingredients and techniques, but also about **classification**: who named what, in which language, and under what social conditions.

This section argues that culinary vocabulary should be read through the same theoretical frame used for cuisine more broadly. At one level, food words show **creolization**: borrowed terms were not simply copied, but naturalized within Philippine languages and often detached from their original social contexts. At another level, vocabulary reveals **hegemony**, because the lexical worlds most closely tied to commercial and metropolitan centers were more likely to become nationally legible. A Tagalog culinary term shaped by Hokkien contact could circulate widely and appear ordinary, while local naming systems in Ilocano or the Visayan sphere might remain regionally marked. Linguistic evidence therefore helps explain how some foodways became central to the national imagination while others remained peripheral or locally bounded.

Food vocabulary also complicates any overly simple model of influence. The issue is not merely that one language borrowed from another. The more interesting question is how different semantic fields were reorganized. Gloria Chan-Yap’s work on Hokkien borrowings in Tagalog shows that cookery terms do not appear randomly; they cluster in identifiable categories. This suggests that contact affected not just isolated dish names, but broader culinary classification systems. The history of Philippine food is therefore partly a history of how languages absorbed, translated, and hierarchized edible worlds.

## 4.1. Hokkien loanwords and Tagalog culinary language

The strongest documented case of Chinese lexical influence on Philippine culinary language is **Tagalog**, especially in its Manila-facing historical context. Chan-Yap’s study identified a substantial body of Hokkien borrowings in present-day Tagalog and showed that food-related words formed a significant and structured subset, including terms connected to meat, vegetables, soy products, cooked foods, and particular preparation types. Her work also argues that what earlier scholars loosely treated as “Chinese” influence in Tagalog is, more precisely, overwhelmingly **Hokkien** in origin.

This is an important historical finding because it indicates that Chinese contact in the Philippines was not merely commercial in a narrow sense; it penetrated **everyday culinary language**. When a language borrows not only a few prestige foods but also routine classificatory terms, the contact has moved into the level of ordinary social life. In theoretical terms, this is a strong case of **creolization** through language. Hokkien terms did not remain alien labels attached to foreign dishes. They were absorbed into Tagalog usage and became part of the lexical infrastructure through which many speakers encountered food itself.

At the same time, the Tagalog case also illustrates **hegemony through linguistic centrality**. Because Tagalog later became tightly linked to Manila, mass media, and national linguistic authority, the culinary vocabulary of this zone enjoyed unusually favorable conditions for wider circulation. In other words, Hokkien-derived food words in Tagalog were not only records of contact; they also became part of the language through which some Chinese-shaped culinary forms were normalized as simply “Filipino.” The capital did not just absorb foreign influence; it helped nationalize it.

This point matters for the broader argument of the article. The national culinary canon is not built only from dishes; it is built from words. A food form that enters Tagalog and becomes lexically ordinary has a different path to national visibility than a food form that remains labeled only within a regionally bounded language. The unequal circulation of vocabulary therefore parallels the unequal circulation of cuisine itself. Here, contact linguistics becomes evidence for canon formation.

## 4.2. Regional naming systems and lexical diversity

If Tagalog shows the densest documented Hokkien culinary imprint, other Philippine regions demonstrate the persistence of **local naming systems** that resist easy absorption into one national lexical order. One revealing example is the family of vegetable-broth dishes known under multiple regional names: **bulanglang** in parts of South-Central Luzon, **gule magalang** in Pampanga, **laswa** in Western Visayas, **law-oy** or **utan** in parts of the Visayas and Mindanao, and related northern forms such as **dinengdeng** or **inabraw** in Northern Luzon. Even when the dishes overlap structurally, their naming remains regionally distributed rather than nationally standardized.

This lexical diversity is not a trivial matter of synonymy. It shows that culinary classification in the Philippines remains deeply **territorialized**. Similar ingredients and cooking logics can belong to different semantic worlds depending on region, language, and local history. In theoretical terms, this pushes against any easy linguistic version of national integration. The Philippines is not simply a place where one culinary language spread outward from the center; it is also a place where multiple local taxonomies persisted. These taxonomies preserve regional ways of thinking about vegetables, broths, condiments, and flavor relations that do not reduce neatly to a single national vocabulary.

This is especially important for thinking about the **Visayan sphere**, which is internally plural rather than linguistically singular. Terms such as *laswa*, *law-oy*, and *utan* reveal a distributed field of related but distinct culinary naming practices. They remind us that “Visayan cuisine” cannot be treated as a homogeneous unit any more than “Filipino cuisine” can. Lexical diversity here reflects a broader social reality: food worlds in the central Philippines are held together less by one standard culinary language than by overlapping local traditions. Regionalization, in this sense, is as much a matter of naming as of cooking.

The northern case is likewise instructive. The distinction between dishes such as *dinengdeng* and more southernly named vegetable soups is not only about ingredients or seasoning; it also marks the endurance of a distinct Ilocano-inflected semantic world. Linguistic persistence here reinforces regional identity. Put differently, food names help anchor culinary belonging. They are part of what prevents the national canon from fully dissolving local food cultures into one standardized lexicon.

## 4.3. Food words as evidence of hierarchy and contact

Food vocabulary does more than record diversity; it also reveals **hierarchy**. The fact that Hokkien-influenced culinary terms are so well documented in Tagalog, while many other regional naming systems remain locally bounded, reflects unequal historical contact and unequal later circulation. The Manila-facing zone stood at the intersection of trade, migration, and political centrality, so its lexical world had more opportunities to travel and become normalized. By contrast, many regional food terms remained attached to local ecologies and linguistic communities, even when the dishes themselves were widely shared in related forms. Vocabulary, then, is one place where **hegemony** becomes visible at the level of language.

This perspective also helps refine the idea of **internal colonialism**. Unequal incorporation into the national frame is not only a matter of political administration or media prestige; it can also be traced in which food languages become legible beyond their home regions. When a Manila-centered or Tagalog-mediated term becomes ordinary, it can appear neutral or universal. When an Ilocano, Visayan, or Bangsamoro food term remains regionally marked, it may be heard as local, ethnic, or specialized even if it names a long-standing and internally sophisticated culinary world. Lexical hierarchy thus parallels regional hierarchy. Some vocabularies are heard as nationally transparent; others remain audible as difference.

At the same time, food words preserve the evidence of **contact without total assimilation**. Borrowing from Hokkien into Tagalog, or the persistence of multiple names for closely related soups across the archipelago, shows that Philippine culinary history is neither a story of pure local continuity nor one of complete linguistic homogenization. Instead, it is a stratified field in which words move, settle, transform, and remain unevenly distributed. This is why linguistic evidence is so valuable to food studies: it captures both the movement of influence and the limits of its integration.

The broader implication is that the study of Philippine cuisine should treat culinary vocabulary as more than supporting detail. Food words are not merely labels attached after the fact. They are part of the social making of cuisine itself. They reveal where trade mattered, where regions retained classificatory autonomy, where the capital normalized borrowed forms, and where linguistic diversity still resists national flattening. To study Philippine food through language is therefore to see more clearly how **creolization**, **hegemony**, and regional unevenness operate together.

# 5. Food, Society, and Political Order

If the previous sections argued that Philippine cuisine is historically layered and regionally uneven, this section asks what cuisine **does** socially and politically. Food is not only a record of contact or a set of regional flavor profiles. It is also a medium through which communities define belonging, reproduce labor relations, mark moral boundaries, and seek public recognition. In the Philippine case, cuisine often works simultaneously as identity marker, economic practice, domestic pedagogy, and political claim. This is why food cannot be confined to the domain of “culture” in a narrow sense. It belongs equally to the analysis of **hegemony**, because dominant tastes and culinary norms help naturalize social hierarchy, and to **internal colonialism**, because not all food worlds enter the national frame on equal terms. Regional cuisines are shaped not only by local ecology but by the unequal value assigned to those ecologies within the nation.

One of the most important sociological points is that food organizes **belonging at multiple scales**. A dish can stand for family continuity, village reputation, ethnolinguistic identity, regional pride, minority visibility, or state-backed heritage. In this sense, the politics of food in the Philippines is not reducible to nationalism. The same cuisine may function differently depending on context: as intimate memory within the household, as livelihood in the market, as regional brand in tourism, or as public proof of legitimacy in interreligious and interregional space. This multiscalar quality is particularly visible when one compares the Manila–Kapampangan core to Bicolano or Maranao/Bangsamoro food worlds. Manila- and Pampanga-centered cuisines more easily become “Filipino” in the national sense, while Bicolano cuisine often appears as distinctive regional heritage and Maranao/Bangsamoro cuisine often appears under the signs of halal, minority identity, or autonomy. The social life of food is therefore inseparable from unequal visibility.

A second sociological point concerns **the social reproduction of cuisine**. Cuisines do not persist by themselves. They are transmitted through domestic labor, apprenticeship, small business, memory, and embodied routine. This makes food especially revealing for the study of gender, kinship, and household economy. At the same time, the public prestige of cuisine often obscures the labor that sustains it. A region may be celebrated for its culinary identity while the women, family enterprises, and everyday kitchen work that maintain that identity remain less visible. Here food becomes a useful site for thinking about the relation between symbolic prestige and material reproduction. The prestige of cuisine is social; the labor of cuisine is social too, but recognized differently.

A third political point concerns **religion and regulated commensality**. In Muslim Filipino contexts, especially Maranao and broader Bangsamoro settings, food is not simply a style or flavor profile but part of an explicitly moral order. Halal is not merely dietary preference. It structures trust, lawful ingestion, and the social conditions under which people can eat together. In this sense, the politics of food extends beyond prestige or taste into questions of purity, boundary, and lawful belonging. This makes southern Muslim foodways analytically distinct from cuisines that are mainly sorted by classed taste or regional branding. The issue is not just whether a cuisine is admired or stigmatized, but whether it can be shared at all, and on what terms. That shift is crucial for understanding why Maranao and Bangsamoro cuisine can function not merely as regional heritage, but as a public language of minority presence and political legitimacy.

## 5.1. Cuisine as identity, region, and belonging

Cuisine in the Philippines frequently functions as a shorthand for **regional identity**. Public-facing accounts of Philippine food regularly emphasize that each region “proudly showcases local dishes” carrying identity, nostalgia, and community, which reflects a wider tendency to treat regional cuisine as an emblem of place rather than just a collection of recipes. This framing matters because it shows that food is one of the most ordinary ways in which belonging is made visible. Regional identity is not only spoken or administered; it is cooked, served, and recognized. A cuisine can stand for a region precisely because repeated ingredients, methods, and flavor combinations condense a longer history of geography, labor, and memory into something sensorially graspable.

Yet regional belonging through food is not politically innocent. Once cuisine becomes a marker of place, it also becomes part of the struggle over which places count as central to the nation. In this respect, the Manila–Tagalog–Kapampangan axis has had a clear advantage. Manila-linked dishes and Kapampangan prestige cooking have often circulated upward into the national canon, while other cuisines have remained more explicitly marked as regional. This is a matter of **hegemony**: some regional foods are nationalized, while others remain regionalized. The difference is not inherent in the food itself. It depends on media reach, historical centrality, colonial prestige, and the degree to which a cuisine can be aligned with dominant norms of festivity, refinement, or domestic respectability. Kapampangan cuisine is especially instructive here because scholarship on it explicitly links food to politics, citizenship, and moral discourse rather than treating it as a merely aesthetic tradition.

The Bicol case makes the identity question clearer from another angle. The Albay study found that local cuisines helped shape Bicol culture and identity, and that dishes such as pinangat, longanisa, and marcasotes strengthened preferences for coconut milk, spicy food, and sweet delicacies that affirm Bicol cultural identity. This is important because it shows that regional identity is not simply attached to a famous flagship dish. It is reproduced through patterned preference and shared sensory habit. Bicolano identity, in this sense, is not only discursively asserted; it is materially and repeatedly enacted through cuisine.

This brings us back to **creolization** in a regional key. Philippine regional cuisines are not pure survivals from a precolonial past. They are historically layered formations that have nevertheless come to signify distinctive belonging. A region’s cuisine can absorb foreign or colonial elements and still function as a powerful local identity marker. What matters is not purity of origin, but the degree to which a cuisine has been localized, normalized, and emotionally attached to a place and its people. That is why food often works so effectively as an identity sign: it condenses history into habit.

## 5.2. Gender, labor, and family transmission

The social reproduction of cuisine is inseparable from **gendered labor**. The clearest evidence in the present discussion comes from the Bicol study, which found two central themes in family food narratives: the role of women family members in income augmentation and cuisine as family legacy. The same study also showed that communities often identify families through the specific cuisine for which they are known, meaning that food is both livelihood and inherited social identity. This is sociologically significant because it reveals cuisine as a form of work and transmission, not only a cultural symbol.

The concept of **family legacy** is especially important. When a cuisine is passed across generations within one household or business, it carries more than technical knowledge. It transmits authority, memory, and reputation. Food in this context becomes a medium of kinship, where a family’s public identity may be tied to a signature product or dish. At the same time, that transmission is vulnerable to disruptions—death, debt, migration, shifts in market demand—showing that culinary continuity is always socially maintained rather than guaranteed. The Bicol material makes this explicit by describing how food businesses are continued across generations and how communities identify families by the dishes they produce.

Kapampangan cuisine illustrates a related point from a different angle. Juntado’s study shows that cooking metaphors permeate discourse on democracy, parenting, and child-rearing in Kapampangan life; the kitchen becomes a site where values are formed and social persons are “cooked.” The article explicitly frames this as “lived citizenship” and shows how culinary language enters reflections on participation, discipline, and moral development. This is analytically rich because it means food labor is not only reproductive in an economic or domestic sense; it is also pedagogical. The household kitchen helps produce social subjects.

Here the concept of **bourgeois domesticity** helps sharpen the analysis. Domestic labor is often idealized as tradition, nurture, or heritage, but those labels can hide the asymmetry of recognition. Public culinary prestige tends to accrue to cuisines and regions; private labor tends to remain gendered and undervalued. Even when women’s work is central to culinary transmission, it may be acknowledged primarily through familial obligation rather than institutional authority. The Bicol study’s emphasis on women’s role in income augmentation helps correct this imbalance by making labor visible as labor rather than simply as love or tradition.

## 5.3. Religion, halal, and minority visibility

Religion introduces a different order of food politics because it organizes cuisine not only around preference or prestige but around **lawful ingestion and moral boundary**. Tallara’s study of Maranao-Muslim food culture in Quiapo shows that Maranao food spaces in Manila are sites where Muslim identity becomes publicly visible and where food facilitates interreligious understanding. The article specifically emphasizes halal food and commensality as mechanisms through which association to food can advance interreligious understanding in Manila. This is crucial because it frames cuisine as a medium of minority presence rather than simply as regional heritage.

In this context, halal is best understood not as a niche market label but as a system of **trust and permissible commensality**. A halal food space says something about who may prepare food, how animals are slaughtered, and which forms of sharing are legitimate. This makes the social politics of Muslim cuisine distinct from the class-coded politics of metropolitan taste. The issue is not only whether a dish is admired, but whether it is lawful to ingest and socially safe to share. In theoretical terms, this aligns more with the logic of taboo and moral order than with the logic of class distinction alone.

The Bangsamoro frame scales this up from everyday minority visibility to **regional political recognition**. Official BARMM materials on culinary showcases present foods “peculiar to the Bangsamoro region” and explicitly stage dishes from Maguindanao, Tausug, and related traditions as publicly recognizable Bangsamoro heritage. This matters because food is here being used to build a more formal collective identity under conditions of autonomous governance. Cuisine becomes part of the symbolic work of regional statecraft: it represents diversity within Bangsamoro while also projecting coherence to outsiders.

Read through **internal colonialism**, this is especially significant. Southern Muslim cuisines have long been less able than Manila- and Pampanga-centered foodways to define the national norm. Yet precisely because of that subordinate positioning, halal food and Bangsamoro culinary display can operate as forms of counter-public assertion. They make visible a different culinary grammar and a different basis of belonging, one not organized by the lowland-Christian colonial center. In that sense, religion and halal do not simply add diversity to the Philippine culinary map. They reveal that the map itself is politically uneven.

# 6. Colonial and Postcolonial Theory of Philippine Food

By this point, the historical and regional analysis has shown that Philippine cuisine cannot be reduced to a neutral inventory of dishes. It is better understood as a field structured by unequal histories of contact, prestige, and incorporation. What remains is to make the theoretical stakes explicit. A colonial and postcolonial reading of Philippine food does not ask only where ingredients or cooking methods came from. It asks how some foodways acquired authority, how others were subordinated, and how culinary legitimacy became tied to empire, class, religion, and region. In this sense, food is not merely a reflection of history; it is one of the media through which historical power becomes ordinary.

Four concepts are especially useful here: **hegemony**, **creolization**, **internal colonialism**, and **bourgeois domesticity**. Hegemony helps explain how the tastes and culinary norms of the colonial and postcolonial center came to appear natural, prestigious, and nationally representative. Creolization helps explain why Philippine food cannot be understood as a passive accumulation of foreign influences, since imported forms were repeatedly localized, translated, and transformed. Internal colonialism helps explain why relations among Philippine regions are not flat or equal, especially in the long asymmetry between the lowland-Christian core and Muslim southern food worlds. Bourgeois domesticity helps explain how American-era ideals of hygiene, regulation, and household order reshaped the prestige of food and cooking in classed terms. Taken together, these concepts turn cuisine from a descriptive object into a field of social theory.

The value of this theoretical turn is that it prevents two common mistakes. The first is to romanticize Philippine cuisine as a harmonious synthesis, as if all influences blended on equal terms into a national whole. The second is to treat regional cuisines as self-contained cultural expressions with no relation to political hierarchy. Neither view is adequate. Philippine cuisine is plural, but not evenly valued. It is hybrid, but not innocently so. It is national, but only through a history in which some regions, languages, and moral orders were more able than others to define the meaning of the national. Theory is useful here not because it replaces historical detail, but because it clarifies the structure beneath the detail.

## 6.1. Hegemony and the making of the national canon

The concept of **hegemony** is central because it explains how one part of the culinary field comes to stand for the whole. In the Philippine case, the foods of the Manila–Tagalog–Kapampangan axis did not simply become visible because they were intrinsically superior or universally preferred. They became central because they were historically located near political power, media circulation, colonial prestige, and linguistic normalization. What later appeared as “Filipino cuisine” was therefore not a transparent expression of the archipelago’s total diversity, but a canon shaped disproportionately by the lowland-Christian colonial core.

This process can be seen at several levels. At the level of vocabulary, Tagalog culinary language absorbed and normalized food words through the Manila-facing commercial corridor. At the level of prestige, Kapampangan cuisine became associated with excellence, ceremony, and culinary seriousness. At the level of public visibility, fiesta-centered and urban-commercial dishes were more easily translated into national representations of Filipino food than cuisines strongly marked by minority religion, intense regional specificity, or non-metropolitan sensory worlds. The canon, then, is not a neutral summary. It is the outcome of selection, repetition, and institutional amplification.

Hegemony matters here because it works precisely by making this outcome seem natural. Once the central cuisines have been sufficiently normalized, they no longer appear as regional or historically contingent. They appear simply as Filipino. Other cuisines, by contrast, remain marked: Ilocano as northern, Bicolano as spicy and coconut-rich, Visayan as locally varied, Maranao or Bangsamoro as Muslim or halal. The hegemonic effect lies not in open exclusion alone, but in asymmetrical unmarkedness. Some cuisines become the national default; others remain audible as difference.

This does not mean the canon is fictitious. It means it is **politically produced**. A postcolonial reading of Philippine food should therefore treat the national canon as an achievement of cultural power rather than as an innocent culinary fact. The question is not merely what foods belong to the nation, but who had the historical capacity to define the nation’s palate.

## 6.2. Creolization and indigenization

If hegemony explains the uneven structure of visibility, **creolization** explains the structure of transformation. Philippine cuisine cannot be adequately described through a language of simple borrowing. Chinese noodle forms, Spanish ceremonial dishes, American domestic technologies, and older Southeast Asian food logics did not coexist as separate layers laid one on top of another. They interacted, fused, translated, and changed one another within local contexts. The result was not imitation but indigenization.

Creolization is especially important because it avoids two opposite errors. On one side lies purism, the search for an untouched native essence prior to contact. On the other side lies diffusionism, the idea that Philippine food is little more than a derivative collection of imported forms. Creolization offers a more precise account: outside forms entered the archipelago, but their meanings and uses were remade through local taste, ecology, labor, and social practice. A noodle dish became Filipino not simply because it was eaten in the Philippines, but because it entered new routines of seasoning, celebration, household cooking, and naming. A Spanish-derived festive dish became Filipino not because it retained its original European meaning, but because it was absorbed into local ritual and social life.

This is why the phrase **Malay matrix** remains useful despite its limitations. It points to a regional substrate through which later influences were refracted. Chinese and colonial inputs did not enter a vacuum. They entered already-existing sensory orders organized around rice, sourness, seafood, preserved condiments, coconut, and local ecological adaptation. Creolization therefore operated through constraint as well as creativity. Imported forms had to survive within local habits of eating and local structures of availability.

The concept also helps explain why regional cuisines can remain strongly distinctive even when they are historically hybrid. Bicolano food may be deeply local in identity while still bearing traces of wider colonial and regional exchange. Tagalog and Kapampangan cuisines may carry Chinese, Spanish, and American layers while still functioning as specifically Filipino formations. Maranao and Bangsamoro foodways may exist within the same national frame while operating through different moral and sensory logics. Creolization, in other words, is not the enemy of difference. It is one of the main ways difference is historically produced.

## 6.3. Internal colonialism and the regional periphery

The concept of **internal colonialism** is necessary because the inequalities shaping Philippine cuisine do not run only between colony and metropole. They also run within the nation itself. Some regions were incorporated into the national cultural field as centers of prestige and definition; others were incorporated as peripheries, specialties, or politically marked margins. The most important contrast here is between the lowland-Christian core and Muslim southern food worlds, though the framework also applies more broadly to the relation between metropolitan and non-metropolitan cuisines.

Internal colonialism clarifies why regional cuisine should not be romanticized as mere diversity. Diversity can exist within hierarchy. Ilocano, Bicolano, Visayan, Maranao, and Bangsamoro foodways are not simply different from the Manila-centered canon; they are differently positioned in relation to it. Some are treated as picturesque regional supplements, others as rustic survivals, and others as morally or politically separate. The issue is not only representation but power: which cuisines shape national common sense, and which cuisines are forced to appear as local exceptions?

This framework is especially illuminating for Maranao and Bangsamoro food. Here the asymmetry is not only regional but civilizational in the national imagination. Halal, Muslim commensality, and southern food practice often enter public discourse as marked difference, not as part of the unmarked norm of Filipino eating. That is precisely why Bangsamoro cuisine can be read as a **counter-canon**. It does not merely add another regional variant to the national table; it reveals that the table itself was historically organized from one dominant side.

Internal colonialism also helps explain why some culinary worlds become legible only through tourism, heritage display, or ethnographic curiosity. They are included, but under terms set elsewhere. The framework therefore pushes the analysis beyond celebration of pluralism toward critique of unequal incorporation. To say that the Philippines is culinary diverse is true but insufficient. The crucial question is how that diversity is ordered.

## 6.4. Bourgeois domesticity and modern food prestige

The concept of **bourgeois domesticity** helps explain a later but decisive transformation in Philippine food politics: the recoding of culinary prestige through modern household norms. Under American rule, food respectability became increasingly tied not only to abundance or ritual display, but to hygiene, nutritional reasoning, measured preparation, efficient housekeeping, refrigeration, and consumer technology. The ideal kitchen was orderly, clean, scientifically managed, and morally disciplined. This was not merely a shift in technique. It was a shift in the social meaning of proper eating and proper household life.

This matters because food prestige is never only about dishes. It is also about the conditions under which dishes are made and judged. Bourgeois domesticity turns the home into a site of class formation. Smells are moderated, kitchens are regulated, storage becomes technologized, and feeding the family becomes part of moral self-management. In this regime, middle-class household competence becomes a sign of advancement. Respectable food is not simply tasty food; it is food produced under the right conditions of cleanliness, efficiency, and moderation.

The impact of this shift on Philippine cuisine was substantial. Older prestige forms tied to feast, ritual, and elite hospitality did not disappear, but they were supplemented and partly recoded by newer standards of domestic order. A cuisine could now be valued not only because it was festive or ceremonially rich, but because it was compatible with the modern hygienic household. This had class effects. Foods and food practices that appeared too pungent, messy, laborious, unregulated, or sensorially overwhelming could be judged not only as lower in taste but as lower in domestic respectability. Here bourgeois domesticity intersects directly with the later discussion of disgust and class distinction.

This framework is also important for gender. The modern household ideal frequently turned women into managers of nutrition, cleanliness, and proper domestic consumption while leaving the prestige economy of cuisine intact. Culinary labor remained feminized, but now under the sign of science, morality, and middle-class aspiration. In postcolonial terms, this means that American-era domestic modernization did not simply “improve” the kitchen. It reorganized the moral terms under which food, labor, and class were judged.

Taken together, these four concepts show that Philippine cuisine is not a naturally unified tradition but a **structured field of unequal transformation**. Hegemony explains how the colonial and postcolonial center acquired the power to define the national palate. Creolization explains how imported and local forms were indigenized into new culinary formations. Internal colonialism explains why some regional cuisines entered the national frame as norms and others as marked margins. Bourgeois domesticity explains how modern class respectability reshaped food prestige through the household. The advantage of this theoretical vocabulary is that it makes visible what purely descriptive food writing often obscures: cuisine is not only eaten history, but organized power.

# 7. Embodiment, Taboo, and Sensory Politics

The previous sections have treated Philippine cuisine as a historical formation, a regional field, and a structure of social and political hierarchy. Yet food does not operate only at the level of discourse, canon formation, or institutional visibility. It is also lived through the **body**. Cuisine is felt as heat, smell, heaviness, bitterness, smoothness, messiness, freshness, satiety, and disgust. It is learned through the hand, tongue, nose, stomach, and routine of the household long before it is fully articulated in abstract terms. A theory of Philippine food that stops at representation is therefore incomplete. To understand why some regional cuisines are admired, stigmatized, normalized, or morally bounded, one must also examine **embodiment**, **taboo**, and **sensory politics**.

This shift matters because hierarchy is often reproduced not only by explicit judgment but by trained bodily reaction. A cuisine may be described as rustic, excessive, clean, refined, too pungent, too oily, too bitter, too spicy, or too strange. Such judgments are not merely aesthetic; they encode class position, regional prejudice, religious boundary, and ideas of moral order. In this sense, food becomes a site where **Bourdieu’s** theory of distinction and **Mary Douglas’s** theory of purity and taboo intersect, though they do not collapse into one another. Bourdieu is especially useful for understanding how classed taste naturalizes disgust toward certain foods or eating styles. Douglas is especially useful for understanding how lawful and unlawful ingestion structure commensality, purity, and social boundary. Philippine regional food brings both dynamics into view.

This section argues that embodiment and sensory politics deepen the earlier account of colonial and postcolonial food order in three ways. First, they show that culinary hierarchy is not only symbolic but bodily trained. Second, they clarify that not all food boundaries are of the same kind: some are organized by **disgust and class distinction**, others by **taboo and moral regulation**. Third, they reveal that cuisine persists not only because it is publicly celebrated as heritage, but because it is sedimented in craving, memory, bodily discipline, and affective attachment. Regional food survives not only through discourse, but through repetition in the body.

## 7.1. Taste, disgust, and class distinction

The first question is how food becomes a medium of **class distinction**. In Bourdieu’s terms, taste is never merely private preference. It is socially formed and socially ranked. Certain foods, textures, smells, and manners of eating come to signify refinement, while others become associated with excess, roughness, vulgarity, or backwardness. In the Philippine case, this is especially useful for understanding how regional foods are sorted within national life. Foods that are strongly fermented, bitter, offal-heavy, pungent, oily, smoky, or visually “messy” may be deeply valued within one local culinary world yet judged negatively through metropolitan or middle-class sensibilities.

What matters here is that **disgust is socialized**. It is not simply the body’s spontaneous rejection of what is biologically dangerous. It is often the result of training within particular regimes of domestic order, public respectability, and sensory discipline. Colonial and postcolonial hierarchies sharpened this process. As argued in the previous section, **bourgeois domesticity** elevated ideals of cleanliness, regulation, moderation, and proper household management. These ideals do not remain in the kitchen as abstract norms; they shape what bodies learn to perceive as acceptable. A cuisine that is heavily aromatic, visibly greasy, strongly fermented, or associated with hand-contact may therefore be judged not only as different but as lower in classed respectability.

This is one place where regional food becomes politically charged. Ilocano, Bicolano, Kapampangan, and other regional foodways can be admired as heritage in one register while still being subject to sensory ranking in another. The same dish can be celebrated as authentic yet quietly marked as too strong, too heavy, too peasant, too rich, or too provincial for everyday middle-class respectability. In that sense, the politics of taste is not only about praise; it is also about the management of **distance**. A cuisine may be loved as regional color but resisted as a normative standard of everyday refinement. This is how **hegemony** works at the level of the senses: dominant tastes become unmarked and natural, while others remain marked even when admired.

Kapampangan cuisine is especially revealing here. It has long been associated with prestige and abundance, yet that prestige is inseparable from moral discourse about excess, refinement, and proper cultivation. The very fact that Kapampangan cuisine can be discussed in terms of excellence, seriousness, and civility shows that classed sensory judgment is central to its public role. What Bourdieu helps us see is that such judgments are not merely culinary. They are judgments about social worth disguised as judgments about taste.

## 7.2. Halal, taboo, and moral boundary-making

If disgust and class distinction organize one axis of food politics, **taboo and lawful ingestion** organize another. Here Mary Douglas is more useful than Bourdieu, because the key issue is not prestige but **moral order**. Douglas’s insight is that food rules help establish boundaries between what is pure and impure, lawful and unlawful, inside and outside the social body. Applied to Philippine regional food, this is most clearly visible in **Maranao and broader Bangsamoro halal foodways**.

Halal is not adequately understood as a flavor preference or a market niche. It is a system of discipline governing what may enter the body, how food must be prepared, who may prepare it, and under what conditions commensality is acceptable. This means that food rules in Muslim Filipino contexts are not simply symbolic markers of identity; they are practical structures of trust and moral classification. A halal kitchen is not only a place where certain dishes are cooked. It is a space where the boundary between lawful and unlawful ingestion is maintained. Food becomes an instrument of ethical order.

This distinction is important because it prevents the flattening of all food difference into one model. A Christian or metropolitan observer may interpret halal refusal as mere fussiness, conservatism, or cultural preference, but analytically that is imprecise. The issue is not that certain foods are socially beneath the eater, but that they are not morally permissible. That is why the contrast between **disgust/class** and **halal/taboo** must be kept clear. In the first case, the question is often, “What kind of person would eat this?” In the second, it is, “May this be eaten at all?” The first is about hierarchy; the second about lawful boundary.

This also has political consequences. In Manila and other predominantly Christian urban spaces, halal foodways render Muslim presence visible as a distinct moral order. Food here does not merely signal regional origin; it stages a claim to legitimate space, lawful practice, and minority recognition. In that sense, Maranao and Bangsamoro cuisine function not only as cultural forms but as embodied counter-publics. They make it possible to inhabit the city under different terms of ingestion and commensality. That is why **internal colonialism** remains relevant here: Muslim food worlds are not only different from the lowland-Christian mainstream, but historically subordinated by it and therefore compelled to defend their boundaries more explicitly.

## 7.3. Sensory memory and affective belonging

Cuisine persists not only because it is regulated or ranked, but because it is **remembered in the body**. One of the limits of purely discursive accounts of food is that they can understate the role of craving, comfort, repetition, and embodied attachment. A regional cuisine survives because it is not just spoken about as heritage; it is desired, missed, and bodily recognized. The smell of a broth, the heaviness of coconut milk, the bite of sourness, the heat of chili, the saltiness of preserved fish, or the texture of a familiar wrapper can operate as forms of **affective recall**. Food, in this sense, is one of the most immediate ways in which belonging is felt rather than simply declared.

This has particular importance in a country marked by migration, mobility, and uneven integration. Regional cuisines travel with people, but they do not travel only as named dishes. They travel as sensory expectations and bodily routines. A migrant or urbanized subject may retain attachment to a regional food world because the body continues to recognize that world as home. This is especially important for understanding why cuisine can be such a powerful medium of regional identity even when language weakens or formal ritual changes. The body often remembers what discourse no longer fully organizes.

Affect also complicates class and taboo. The same food that is stigmatized in one context can function as consolation, dignity, or intimate continuity in another. A pungent or strongly regional dish may be read by outsiders as excessive or embarrassing, yet by insiders as the very substance of comfort and recognition. A halal practice may be interpreted from outside as restrictive, yet from within as ethically grounding and socially secure. Sensory memory therefore resists any analysis that reduces food to external ranking alone. Bodies do not merely absorb hierarchy; they also carry attachment, resistance, and alternative moral worlds.

In theoretical terms, this means that cuisine should be understood as an **affective infrastructure** as well as a symbolic system. It binds families, regions, and minorities not only through abstract identity but through repeated sensory reinforcement. What is inherited is not only the recipe, but the bodily expectation of what a proper meal should feel like. This deepens the earlier argument about family transmission: the continuity of cuisine depends not only on technical knowledge, but on the persistence of affective habit.

## 7.4. Toward a plural model of Philippine cuisine

A sensory and embodied perspective brings the article to a broader conclusion. Philippine cuisine cannot be adequately understood as a single national repertoire with regional embellishments. Nor can it be fully explained as a mosaic of equal diversities. What the analysis of embodiment, taboo, and sensory politics shows is that the Philippine culinary field is organized by **multiple regimes of legitimacy**. Some foods are normalized through classed taste and national hegemony. Some are elevated through prestige and ceremony. Some are bounded through halal and taboo. Some persist through affective memory and embodied loyalty even when they remain marginal to the national canon.

This calls for a **plural model of Philippine cuisine**. Such a model does not deny the existence of national culinary forms, but it refuses to treat them as the natural center from which all other cuisines radiate. Instead, it recognizes multiple centers, overlapping histories, and competing normative orders. Manila and Pampanga may remain crucial to the making of the national canon, but they do not exhaust the culinary logic of the archipelago. Ilocano, Bicolano, Visayan, Maranao, and Bangsamoro food worlds are not simply regional add-ons. They are distinct sensory, moral, and social orders that complicate what “Filipino cuisine” can mean.

At the theoretical level, this means keeping **hegemony** and **creolization** together with **embodiment**, **distinction**, and **taboo**. Hegemony explains why some cuisines acquire national authority. Creolization explains why culinary forms are transformed rather than simply borrowed. Bourdieu helps explain how classed taste organizes admiration and disgust. Douglas helps explain how commensality is bounded by purity and law. Internal colonialism explains why these processes are unevenly distributed across regions and moral communities. A plural model of Philippine cuisine must therefore be historical, regional, bodily, and political all at once.

The advantage of such a model is that it moves beyond both celebratory multiculturalism and flattening nationalism. It allows Philippine food to be understood as a field of **unequal but interconnected food worlds**—worlds that are eaten, felt, inherited, and contested. This, ultimately, is why cuisine matters so much in the Philippine context: it condenses history, hierarchy, identity, morality, and memory into everyday acts of ingestion.
