## Neo-Classical Name Aesthetics in the Sinosphere:

## Regulatory Constraints, Semantic Fashion, and Phonological “Poeticness” Across Korean, Chinese, Japanese, and Vietnamese Naming Systems

**Author:** (as submitted)  
**Affiliation:** Department of Linguistics, (specialty: comparative onomastics & Sinosphere historical phonology)  
**Target venue:***Journal of East Asian Onomastics & Cultural Phonology* (niche specialty review)

### Abstract

Recent decades have witnessed convergent “neo-classical” naming fashions across East Asia: Korean given names clustering around syllables such as **준/서/하/윤**, Mainland Chinese names featuring characters such as **梓/轩/涵/睿**, and Japanese names exhibiting kanji choices and readings that foreground nature imagery, elegance, and affective warmth. These patterns prompt a comparative question: do they reflect a broader Sinosphere aesthetic cycle driven by shared classical lexical reservoirs, or are they locally determined by orthographic regimes, state registration constraints, and language-specific phonological aesthetics? This review synthesizes (i) South Korea’s court-delimited inventory of registrable name Hanja and its downstream consequences for name-element choice; (ii) empirically grounded time-series inflection points in Korean registry-derived rankings; (iii) the partial but suggestive alignment with Mainland Chinese “top character” reporting and large-scale historical name-character frequency resources; (iv) Japan’s distinctive feedback loop between kanji-reading creativity (きらきらネーム) and recent legal/administrative constraints via family register furigana requirements; and (v) Vietnam’s parallel semantic cycling in Sino-Vietnamese morphemes despite script shift to quốc ngữ. Methodologically, we propose an integrated framework combining semantic-field classifiers (Virtue/Nature/Intellect/Beauty/Affect), change-point detection on element shares, and phonotactic/sonority predictors of perceived “poeticness.” We argue that cross-Sinosphere convergence is best modeled not as a single synchronized wave, but as a family of oscillations shaped by a shared prestige lexicon interacting with divergent constraints in orthography, bureaucracy, and phonology.

**Keywords:** onomastics; Sinosphere; Sino-Korean; kanji readings; semantic change; phonological aesthetics; change-point detection

## 1\. Introduction: why “Korean Hanja names sound more poetic”

The perception that Korean Hanja-based names sound “more poetic” than many contemporary Chinese given names is a serious empirical problem masquerading as a vibe. “Poeticness” here is not merely a property of meaning; it is jointly produced by (i) the *selection of elements* (character/syllable choices and their semantic fields), (ii) the *phonological surface form* in modern speech, and (iii) the *orthographic and administrative ecology* that filters which options are socially and legally viable.

Two empirical anchors motivate this review. First, South Korea exhibits extremely prominent “fashion clusters” visible in registry-derived rankings, with names like **서준, 서윤, 하준, 하윤** reaching striking counts in mid-2010s (e.g., 2014) and changing thereafter. Second, contemporary Chinese newborn-name reporting (where available) highlights a set of high-frequency characters such as **梓** and **涵**, suggestive of a parallel, though not identical, semantic turn toward nature imagery, refinement, and positive affect.

The central thesis developed below is that cross-country similarity emerges from a shared classical semantic reservoir, but the *felt poetics* —especially for Korean—arises from the interaction between (a) a bounded legal inventory of registrable Hanja and (b) the phonotactic aesthetics of modern Korean two-syllable given names, which often favor open syllables and sonorant codas that yield a “legato” auditory profile.

## 2\. Data and historiography: what counts as evidence in naming-fashion research

### 2.1 Korea: NameChart as near-registry observational data

For South Korea, a crucial advantage is the availability of year-indexed rankings with counts. For example, NameChart’s 2014 overall list shows **서준 4,218**, **서윤 3,798**, **하준 3,028**, **하윤 2,979**, capturing the mid-2010s crest of a cluster centered on 준/윤/서/하. Comparable 2025 lists show a different distribution (e.g., overall: **도윤 2,131**, **이현 1,997**, **서윤 1,916**, **하준 1,688**, etc.), consistent with post-peak diffusion and demographic decline effects.

### 2.2 Korea: the legal inventory of name Hanja as a selection filter

South Korea constrains registrable “commonly used” Hanja for names via Supreme Court–defined tables, consolidated in the widely circulated **인명용한자표** PDF (which explicitly includes base educational Hanja and appendices for additional/variant forms). This constraint is not an abstract policy footnote: it constitutes a language-external selection mechanism shaping which semantic and graphic resources are available to parents and name-consultants.

### 2.3 China: partial annual reporting and long-run historical character-frequency resources

Contemporary national annual “top-character” lists (as disseminated in public reporting) provide only intermittent, short-run series, limiting change-point inference from annual rankings alone. However, a complementary resource exists for historical modeling: the **ChineseNames** database (R package) reports nationwide frequencies of **2,614** given-name characters (1930–2008), covering a very large population base—useful for generational cohort analysis and decade-binned semantic shifts.

### 2.4 Japan: corporate survey data, new open datasets, and a shifting legal-administrative regime

Japan’s baby-name trends are frequently reported through the Meiji Yasuda survey and related summaries. Recent work by Ogihara provides a structured dataset of “common writings and readings” for Japanese baby names (2019–2024), explicitly documenting consistent survey collection by Meiji Yasuda and improving reproducibility for academic analysis.  
A major recent shock to the Japanese naming ecosystem is the legal/administrative move to require official furigana readings in the family register **戸籍(こせき)**, partly to manage the social-administrative costs of extreme reading creativity (きらきらネーム).

### 2.5 Vietnam: semantic cycling without logographs

Vietnam’s orthographic shift to quốc ngữ removes logographic constraints but retains Sino-Vietnamese morphemic prestige as a naming substrate. Contemporary sociolinguistic work emphasizes modernization-linked loosening of gendered middle-name conventions (e.g., declines in “Thị” and “Văn” usage) and the sociocultural predictors (education, urban residence) of naming style.

## 3\. Korean naming culture: Hanja as optional orthography, compulsory prestige

### 3.1 The sociolinguistic position of Hanja in contemporary Korea

In modern South Korea, given names are typically written in Hangul in everyday usage, but Hanja remains a prestige-bearing substrate used in formal registration, name interpretation, and intergenerational explanation. This creates a distinctive semiotic configuration: the spoken form is Hangul-phonological; the “meaning guarantee” is supplied by a legally bounded, culturally elevated Hanja mapping.

### 3.2 Constraint-induced convergence: why a bounded Hanja inventory can increase “poetic density”

Because the name-Hanja inventory is selected and maintained for registrability and conventional usage (and includes explicit appendices for additional and permitted variant forms), it tends to overrepresent culturally “safe” semantic fields: virtue, elegance, nature, auspiciousness, and cultivated intellect—precisely the fields that naïve listeners tend to gloss as “poetic.”

This is a structural point: a bounded inventory tends to compress the semantic search space toward socially valued regions. The result is higher average “poeticness per syllable” even when the phonological form is simple (two syllables), because many syllables map to multiple Hanja with refined connotations (e.g., 하 → 夏/河/霞-type nature or atmospheric imagery; 준 → 俊/峻-style “talent/lofty” imagery, depending on mapping conventions).

### 3.3 Empirical signature: mid-2010s takeoff and later diffusion

The NameChart 2014 distribution illustrates how a small number of names dominated with very high counts, especially those anchored in 준/윤 clusters. By 2025, the ranking still contains those motifs but with lower concentration and different leaders, consistent with diffusion (more distinct names share the mass) and demographic decline.

## 4\. China: “neo-classical” characters as modernity-compatible refinement

Chinese newborn naming fashions reported in the late 2010s–early 2020s frequently highlight characters such as **梓** and **涵**, often combined into two-character given names with nature and cultivated-affect semantics. While annual “top character” tables are not consistently available in an official, longitudinal format via a single stable repository (a methodological limitation), the existence of the large historical character-frequency database (1930–2008) indicates that Chinese naming is amenable to cohort-based semantic analysis, enabling the testing of whether “neo-classical” motifs represent a reversion, a novel recombination, or a continuity of prestige semantics across political-economic eras.

A key comparative point: because Hanzi remains the everyday script, Chinese naming fashion is publicly legible as character choice, and therefore subject to explicit discourse about “vulgar homogeneity,” “internet names,” and “overused characters,” which may accelerate counter-fashion dynamics. Korea, by contrast, often experiences a layer of semiotic opacity (Hangul surface with optional Hanja disambiguation), potentially reducing overt character-level stigma while preserving semantic prestige.

---

## 5\. Japan: kanji-reading creativity, the きらきらネーム problem, and registry feedback

Japan’s naming system is uniquely fertile ground for a semiotic tension: kanji provide semantic and aesthetic resources, but readings are flexible enough to enable “creative” pronunciations. The label **きらきらネーム** (“glittery names”) captures a class of names characterized by unusual readings, pop-culture references, and orthographic/phonological play.

The recent policy move to require official furigana readings in the family register **戸籍(こせき)** constitutes a strong selection pressure toward “generally accepted” readings, introduced to reduce administrative burdens and confusability in institutional contexts. This change plausibly marks a structural breakpoint after which the naming ecosystem shifts: not necessarily reducing “poetic kanji,” but pruning extreme reading creativity and redirecting fashion toward legible neo-classical aesthetics (nature/beauty/affect) with conventional readings.

Japan’s 2024 reporting illustrates the thematic direction: names such as **陽翔(はると)** and **凪(なぎ)** foreground nature/sky/meteorological calm as symbolic resources. Meanwhile, recent open datasets (2019–2024) enable finer-grained testing of whether semantic fields or reading patterns shifted year-by-year, beyond media summaries.

---

## 6\. Vietnam: semantic prestige cycles without character constraints

Vietnam supplies a crucial counterfactual: a Sinosphere-influenced naming culture operating in a non-logographic orthography. Script shift does not erase the prestige of Sino-Vietnamese morphemes; instead, it relocates the “neo-classical” dynamic into lexical choice and gender-structure conventions.

Recent sociolinguistic work emphasizes shifting gender marking and modernization-linked flexibility, including decline in stereotyped middle-name markers (e.g., “Thị,” “Văn”), with predictors such as education level and urban residence. The Vietnam case supports the proposition that “neo-classical” cycles can persist as *semantic-morphemic fashion*, even when the orthographic channel for logographic aesthetics is removed.

## 7\. A methodological synthesis: from “poetic” impressions to testable models

This section consolidates the computational and experimental program implicit in our discussion.

### 7.1 Semantic-field classifier: Virtue / Nature / Intellect / Beauty / Affect

**Goal:** quantify how the semantic composition of fashionable name elements changes by year, and compare across countries.

**Unit of analysis**

- Korea: Hangul syllables in popular given names, mapped to distributions over permitted Hanja candidates (because syllable→Hanja is many-to-many under legal constraints).
- China: characters in given names; use short-run annual top lists where available + cohort databases for long-run trends.
- Japan: kanji writings and readings in survey datasets (e.g., 2019–2024).
- Vietnam: Sino-Vietnamese morphemes and name-structure tokens (middle-name conventions).

**Classifier construction**

- Dictionary-and-radical cues for hanzi/kanji (e.g., 氵/水-related → Nature; 心/忄 → Affect; 女/玉/糸 → Beauty; 言/文/学/智 → Intellect; 德/仁/礼 → Virtue).
- For Korea: compute each syllable’s bucket distribution by aggregating the buckets of its permitted Hanja options from the Supreme Court table and appendices.

**Outputs**

- Per year: semantic-bucket shares (birth-weighted where counts exist, e.g., NameChart).
- Cross-country comparability: report both raw shares and z-scored deviations from each country’s baseline to control for systemic differences.

### 7.2 Change-point detection: “fashion regimes” and inflection years

**Goal:** identify years where the semantic mix or specific element shares (e.g., 준, 윤; 梓, 涵; nature-themed kanji) shift significantly.

- For Korea, year-by-year counts enable robust change-point models (e.g., PELT, Bayesian piecewise linear models) on syllable shares derived from NameChart series (e.g., mid-2010s spike visible in 2014 vs 2025).
- For Japan, the 2019–2024 dataset supports change-point analysis on semantic and reading variables, with attention to policy shocks around furigana registration requirements (戸籍(こせき) readings).
- For China, annual lists are short-run; cohort-based change-point detection (by birth decade) is more statistically defensible using the ChineseNames 1930–2008 resource.

### 7.3 Phonological and phonotactic aesthetics: open syllables, sonorant codas, and perceived “poeticness”

**Core hypothesis (testable):** name elements in the Nature (and sometimes Affect/Beauty) buckets disproportionately exhibit phonological profiles that listeners rate as “poetic,” such as:

- higher open-syllable rates,
- higher sonorant-final rates (nasal/liquid codas),
- smoother vowel sequences (less “staccato” alternation),
- in tonal languages, smoother realized F0 trajectories (after sandhi).

**Korean phonotactic leverage**  
Modern Korean coda neutralization yields a small set of permissible coda consonant categories, making “sonorant vs non-sonorant closure” tractable as a feature. Academic discussions of Korean phonological processes explicitly note coda neutralization and restricted coda inventories.

**Mandarin leverage**  
Standard Mandarin has highly restricted codas and tone-bearing syllables; thus, “open vs nasal-final vs rhotic” plus tone-sequence smoothness can be quantified.

**Experimental design (minimal publishable)**

- Stimuli: matched sets of (i) fashionable “neo-classical” names, (ii) frequency-matched controls, (iii) phonology-matched pseudo-names; presented in audio-only and text-only conditions.
- Ratings: poetic/literary, euphony, elegance, softness; plus familiarity and “modern vs traditional.”
- Analysis: mixed-effects (or Bayesian hierarchical) models predicting ratings from phonotactic predictors and semantic bucket, with random effects for participant and item.

This design can distinguish whether “poetic” is primarily driven by (a) meaning and orthographic aura (character aesthetics), (b) phonological mouthfeel, or (c) their interaction.

## 8\. Reframing “Korean names feel more literary”: a comparative explanation

The perceived superior “poeticness” of Korean Hanja names relative to many Chinese names (as felt by some observers) is best treated as an emergent outcome of three structural asymmetries:

1. **Opacity-as-protection:** Hangul surface forms obscure the exact character choice, reducing direct stigma against overused specific characters while maintaining access to refined meanings through Hanja mapping when desired.
2. **Constraint-as-curation:** a court-maintained set of registrable Hanja acts like a cultural sieve, increasing the prior probability that available options cluster in high-prestige semantic regions.
3. **Phonotactic legato:** two-syllable Korean given names often realize smooth phonotactics (open syllables and sonorant codas), plausibly biasing auditory aesthetic judgments; this can be tested directly using the methods above.

China, where character choice is fully legible and publicly contestable, may cycle faster into counter-fashion and “overused character” critique; Japan’s reading flexibility invites a different form of contestation (きらきらネーム), now increasingly regulated via 戸籍(こせき) reading requirements. Vietnam demonstrates that even absent logographs, the semantic cycle persists through Sino-Vietnamese lexicon fashion and shifting gendered name structure.

---

## 9\. Conclusion: a family of cycles, not a single synchronized wave

The evidence supports a broader Sinosphere pattern: modern naming repeatedly re-activates a classical semantic palette (Nature, Beauty, Affect, cultivated Virtue/Intellect) while negotiating modern pressures—uniqueness, administrative legibility, and cross-cultural portability. Yet the cycles are not synchronized; they are locally phase-shifted by script ecology and policy constraints. Korea’s court-delimited Hanja inventory and Hangul surface opacity produce high semantic prestige with stable phonological simplicity; China’s fully legible character economy fosters faster public critique and churn; Japan’s kanji-reading flexibility creates an additional axis of creativity now subject to new registry constraints; Vietnam’s cycle survives as lexical-semantic fashion rather than orthographic play.

The methodological program outlined here—semantic-field classifiers, change-point detection, and phonotactic/sonority-based aesthetic modeling—offers a coherent empirical route from “this feels poetic” to testable comparative claims.
