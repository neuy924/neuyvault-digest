## Abstract

This article provides a comprehensive genealogical survey of queer theory from its pre-history in the homophile movements of the 1950s through its founding moment in the early 1990s to its current frontiers in trans-crip theory, queer AI studies, and Global South epistemologies. Rather than presenting the field’s development as linear intellectual progress, the article argues that queer theory has been organised from its beginning by three recurring structural tensions — between identity-based politics and anti-identity critique, between textualist methodology and embodied knowledge, and between queer futurity and anti-futurity — that return in new forms in each generation precisely because they reflect genuine contradictions in the political conditions of queer life that theory cannot resolve, only reformulate. The article traces the field’s development across eight periods: the homophile and liberationist movements and the feminist sex wars that created the conditions for queer theory’s emergence; the founding moment of 1990–1993 in which Foucault’s retroactive foundation, Sedgwick’s *Epistemology of the Closet*, and Butler’s *Gender Trouble* established the canonical framework; the consolidation of the 1990s through the AIDS crisis, debates about lesbian and bisexual inclusion, and the anti-social turn; the critique of normativity in the early 2000s through Edelman’s anti-futurity, Duggan’s homonormativity, the emergence of trans studies, and Ahmed’s phenomenological turn; the second generation’s insertion of race and empire through Puar’s homonationalism, Muñoz’s queer futurity, Snorton’s fungibility, Haritaworn’s necropolitics, Ferguson’s queer of colour critique, and Berlant’s cruel optimism; the multiplication and expansion of the 2010s through trans studies’ consolidation, crip theory’s encounter with queer, and Global South scholarship; the current landscape of trans necropolitics, trans-crip theory, queer AI studies, and non-Western epistemological pressures; and the field’s recurring fault lines as the structural features that define its ongoing intellectual identity. The article concludes by naming three significant gaps — the embodied epistemology gap, the philosophical depth gap in engagement with non-Western traditions, and the clinical care gap — as the most urgent directions for the field’s future development.

## Section I — Before Queer Theory: The Conditions of Emergence

### 1.1 — The Homophile Movements and Liberal Rights

Queer theory did not emerge from nowhere. It emerged from the specific political and intellectual formations that preceded it — formations whose assumptions it would systematically contest, but whose concerns it continued to address. Understanding the pre-history of the field is not merely a matter of historical completeness. It is necessary for understanding why the founding texts took the specific theoretical positions they did, what problems they were responding to, and why certain tensions were built into the framework from the beginning.

The homophile organisations of the 1950s — the Mattachine Society (founded 1950, Los Angeles) and the Daughters of Bilitis (founded 1955, San Francisco) — were the first sustained political organisations in the United States to organise specifically around homosexual identity. Their political strategy was explicitly assimilationist: homosexuals were a legitimate minority deserving civil rights, like other minorities; the goal was tolerance and equal treatment within existing social structures, not the transformation of those structures. The Mattachine Society’s early publications insisted on the respectability of homosexual people, their capacity for stable relationships, their contributions to society. The Daughters of Bilitis’s journal *The Ladder* addressed lesbian women in terms that emphasised their femininity, their normalcy, their distance from the butch/femme cultures that heterosexual society found threatening.

This political strategy rested on specific theoretical assumptions that would become queer theory’s primary targets. It assumed that homosexual identity was stable and coherent — that there was a homosexual population with definable characteristics that could be distinguished from the heterosexual majority. It assumed that this identity was analogous to racial or religious identity — a characteristic of a minority group that did not fundamentally threaten the social order and that could therefore be accommodated within liberal frameworks of rights and tolerance. And it assumed that the problem was one of recognition and inclusion: if homosexual people could demonstrate their fundamental similarity to heterosexual people, they would be granted equal treatment.

These assumptions were not simply wrong. They reflected the specific political conditions of 1950s America — the Cold War climate in which homosexuality was officially classified as a security threat and a mental illness, in which the penalties for visibility were severe, and in which the primary political task was survival. The homophile organisations operated under conditions that made assimilationism not merely a political choice but a condition of possibility. What queer theory would later identify as the limitations of this framework — its investment in identity, its acceptance of the terms of normalcy, its silence on race and class — were, in the historical context of their emergence, also the conditions that made political organisation possible at all.

### 1.2 — Gay Liberation, Stonewall, and the Radicalisation of Identity

The 1969 Stonewall uprising — in which patrons of the Stonewall Inn in New York City, predominantly working-class, racially diverse, and including significant numbers of trans women and drag queens, resisted a police raid — marks the conventional beginning of the modern LGBT rights movement. The Gay Liberation Front that emerged in Stonewall’s wake operated in a radically different political register from the homophile organisations. Drawing on Black Power, second-wave feminism, anti-Vietnam War activism, and the New Left, gay liberation argued that homosexuality was not a minority characteristic to be tolerated but a challenge to the entire heterosexual social order — that the liberation of gay people required the transformation of society, not accommodation within it.

The liberationist framework produced several theoretical moves that would be important for queer theory’s later development. It politicised identity rather than seeking to depoliticise it: rather than arguing that homosexual people were fundamentally similar to heterosexual people and deserved equal treatment, gay liberation argued that homosexuality represented a different and superior relationship to sexuality, pleasure, and political life. It connected sexual liberation to other liberation movements, making the argument — which queer theory would later complicate and critique — that the liberation of sexuality was inseparable from the liberation of race, class, and gender. And it introduced the critique of heterosexuality as an institution — not merely as a personal preference but as a social structure organised around specific power relations — that would become central to the feminist theory of the 1970s and 1980s.

But gay liberation retained the identity politics that the homophile movements had established, even as it radicalised it. The “gay” of gay liberation was a collective political identity grounded in shared sexual desire and shared political struggle — a we that could be mobilised, that could march, that could demand recognition. The theoretical problems this identity posed — its implicit whiteness, its male-centredness, its assumption of coherence across class and race — were not yet visible as theoretical problems. They became visible only as the movements of the 1970s developed and fractured.

### 1.3 — Feminist Theory and the Sex Wars

The feminist theoretical interventions of the late 1970s and 1980s were the immediate intellectual preconditions for queer theory’s emergence. Two developments were particularly consequential.

Adrienne Rich’s “Compulsory Heterosexuality and Lesbian Existence” (1980) introduced a concept that would be transformative: heterosexuality not as a natural orientation but as a compelled institution — a social structure maintained through specific practices of coercion, co-optation, and the erasure of alternatives. Rich’s argument was addressed to feminist theory as much as to the study of sexuality: she was arguing that the feminist analysis of women’s oppression could not be adequate if it did not account for the ways in which compulsory heterosexuality enforced women’s dependence on men and made lesbian existence systematically invisible. The concept of compulsory heterosexuality — the idea that heterosexuality was produced and enforced rather than naturally given — became one of the most important conceptual building blocks for Butler’s heterosexual matrix.

The feminist “sex wars” of the late 1970s and early 1980s — the conflict between anti-pornography feminists (Andrea Dworkin, Catherine MacKinnon) and pro-sex feminists (Gayle Rubin, Pat Califia, Carole Vance) — generated the conditions for queer theory’s emergence in a more direct way. Gayle Rubin’s “Thinking Sex: Notes for a Radical Theory of the Politics of Sexuality” (1984) directly established the case for a theory of sexuality autonomous from feminist theory of gender. Rubin argued that sexuality had its own political economy, its own hierarchies, its own forms of persecution — and that these could not be adequately understood through the lens of gender oppression alone. A feminist theory of sexuality that treated all non-reproductive, non-normative sexuality as inherently oppressive was not, Rubin argued, a theory of sexual liberation. It was a theory of sexual regulation in feminist dress.

Rubin’s argument established two moves that queer theory would inherit: first, that sexuality required its own theoretical apparatus, not reducible to gender; and second, that the political valence of sexual practices was not determined by their content but by the social structures — law, medicine, religion, popular culture — through which they were evaluated and regulated. These moves directly anticipate Foucault’s analysis of the production of sexuality through discourse and power — which Rubin explicitly engaged — and they established the intellectual agenda that the founding texts of 1990–1993 would pursue.

## Section II — The Founding Moment: 1990–1993

### 2.1 — Foucault’s Retroactive Foundation

Michel Foucault did not found queer theory. *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1* was published in French in 1976 and in English translation in 1978 — more than a decade before queer theory acquired its name. When it appeared, it was received primarily as a contribution to intellectual history and the history of ideas, not as a theoretical framework for a nascent political movement. Foucault was not present at the 1990 conference where Teresa de Lauretis coined “queer theory.” He died in 1984, of AIDS-related complications, before the field he is now considered to have founded existed as a field.

He became retroactively foundational because the emerging field needed precisely what his book provided: a way of thinking about sexuality as a domain produced by power-knowledge relations rather than as a natural fact that power merely regulated. The repressive hypothesis — the assumption that sexuality was a pre-political force that modern society suppressed and that liberation required releasing — was, Foucault argued, not a description of what had happened but a mechanism of the very power it claimed to describe. Victorian society did not repress sexuality. It produced an explosion of discourse about it — through medicine, psychiatry, demography, education, and the confessional — and in doing so produced the very subjects whose sexuality was now a domain of knowledge, management, and intervention.

The most consequential claim: “The sodomite had been a temporary aberration; the homosexual was now a species.” Before the nineteenth century’s medicalisation of sexuality, sodomy was an act — prohibited, sinful, potentially legally punishable, but an act that any person might commit. From the 1870s onward, a new figure emerged in Western European and North American medical and legal discourse: not a person who had committed an act but a person whose entire nature was constituted by a specific desire. The homosexual was not discovered as a type of person who had always existed; the homosexual was produced as a type of person by the specific discursive practices — the clinical interview, the psychiatric case study, the sexological taxonomy — that named, classified, and thereby constituted their object.

This argument provided queer theory with its methodological inheritance: the genealogical method, the analysis of discourse as constitutive rather than merely descriptive, the critique of the naturalness of sexuality as a domain. It also provided the field’s limits, which are the limits of the Foucauldian apparatus itself. Foucault’s genealogy is a genealogy of Western European — specifically French and North Atlantic — modernity. The biopolitical apparatus he describes, the confessional technology he traces, the medicalisation of sexuality he documents: these are historically specific formations whose colonial dimensions were largely invisible in Foucault’s account. When queer theory took Foucault as its methodological foundation, it inherited not only the power of his analytical apparatus but the blindnesses constitutive of it — above all, the absence of race and colonialism from the analysis of how sexuality was produced as a domain.

### 2.2 — Sedgwick’s *Epistemology of the Closet* (1990)

The publication of *Epistemology of the Closet* in 1990 marks the moment the emerging field acquired its first canonical text and, in the same year, its name. Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick’s central argument had the ambition and the precision that founding texts require: the homo/heterosexual binary is not the private concern of gay and lesbian people but “the defining structure for gay oppression in this century” and simultaneously a structure whose analysis illuminates virtually every aspect of modern Western culture.

The closet, in Sedgwick’s analysis, is not primarily a hiding place — not a private space of concealment that gay people inhabit while waiting for the courage or the opportunity to come out. It is an **epistemological structure**: a specific organisation of the relationship between knowledge and ignorance, disclosure and concealment, the said and the unsaid, that operates at the level of culture as well as individual psychology. The person who is in the closet is positioned within a specific epistemological relationship — to themselves, to others who may or may not know, to the public norms of disclosure — that cannot be resolved by disclosure. “Coming out,” Sedgwick observes, “is never a once-and-for-all event but a series of new closets.” Every new context, every new institution, every new relationship requires the renegotiation of knowledge and ignorance on terms that the closet structure itself sets.

The text’s methodological contribution was as significant as its theoretical one. Sedgwick demonstrated that close reading of literary and cultural texts — drawing on a tradition from Henry James to Marcel Proust to Herman Melville — could illuminate the epistemological structures through which sexuality was organised in modern Western culture. This was not merely the application of a new theory to existing literary texts. It was the demonstration that the canonical literary texts of Western modernity were themselves organised by the homo/hetero binary, that they could not be read adequately without attention to the management of sexual knowledge, and that the closet’s structure was embedded in the formal properties of literary form as much as in the explicit content of literary representation.

What *Epistemology of the Closet* could not yet do — and what its publication in 1990, before the consolidation of the queer of colour critique, meant it did not need to — was account adequately for race, for gender as a domain distinct from sexuality, or for the cultural specificity of the closet as an epistemological structure. These limitations were not visible as limitations in 1990. They became the generative sites of the field’s subsequent development.

### 2.3 — Butler’s *Gender Trouble* (1990) and *Bodies That Matter* (1993)

Judith Butler’s *Gender Trouble: Feminism and the Subversion of Identity* (1990) is the founding text of queer theory in the strictest sense — the text whose argument established the field’s central theoretical claim, whose conceptual vocabulary shaped everything that came after it, and whose limits defined the agenda for subsequent decades of theoretical work.

The theory of gender performativity is the argument’s core. It does not claim that gender is a performance — a role consciously adopted, a costume worn and potentially discarded at will. It claims that gender is **performative**: constituted through the repeated, regulated, and citational practices that appear to express it. There is no prior gendered nature that performance expresses. Gender is effect, not cause — produced by the very acts that appear to be its expression. “In imitating gender, drag implicitly reveals the imitative structure of gender itself — as well as its contingency.” The drag queen does not merely imitate femininity. She reveals, through the explicitness of her imitation, that all gendered performance is imitation — that femininity as practised by women who do not understand themselves as imitating is equally citational, equally the product of regulatory norms rather than the expression of a natural essence.

The **heterosexual matrix** — Butler’s name for the grid through which bodies, genders, and desires are rendered coherent within the dominant cultural framework — is the structure that makes gender performativity politically consequential. To be legible as a person requires the alignment of body, gender, and desire in a specific configuration: female body, feminine gender, desire for men; male body, masculine gender, desire for women. Any deviation from this alignment produces **abjection** — the condition of being expelled from the domain of the recognisably human, positioned as the constitutive outside of the normative subject. Queerness, in this analysis, is not the simple opposite of heteronormativity. It is the constitutive outside that heteronormativity requires in order to define its inside.

*Bodies That Matter* (1993) extended the argument in two directions. It developed the materiality of the body — arguing against a reading of performativity that treated the body as infinitely plastic, demonstrating that bodies acquire their materiality through specific citational processes that are not freely chosen but compelled. And it began, for the first time in Butler’s work, to address race as a co-constitutive axis of the heterosexual matrix — an address that Butler herself acknowledged as incomplete, as failing to do justice to the specificity of racial formation as a constitutive dimension of the normative subject rather than an additional variable.

The founding of queer theory in 1990 was also, significantly, a founding in a specific political moment: the height of the AIDS crisis in the United States, the emergence of ACT UP (AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power) in 1987, and the intensification of anti-gay political backlash. The theory was not produced in a political vacuum. Its emphasis on the violence of normative gender, on the abjection of bodies that deviate from the heterosexual matrix, and on the political urgency of making queer life visible and grievable — all of these reflect the specific political conditions of their production. This connection between theory and political crisis will be a recurring feature of the field’s development.

### 2.4 — The Naming of the Field and Queer Nation

Teresa de Lauretis coined “queer theory” as the title of a conference she organised at the University of California, Santa Cruz, in February 1990 — the same year that *Gender Trouble* and *Epistemology of the Closet* were published. The coincidence is not accidental. De Lauretis was responding to the convergence of a set of theoretical interventions and political developments that suggested the emergence of something new — a critical analytics adequate to the specificity of sexual politics in the 1990s, neither reducible to feminism’s analysis of gender nor to the gay liberationist politics of identity.

De Lauretis subsequently repudiated the term she had coined. By 1994, she had publicly declared that “queer theory” had been “taken up by those theorists and activists who are most interested in sexual dissidence” and had thereby lost the critical edge she had intended — becoming, in her view, another identity category rather than a critical analytics. The field that claimed her coinage developed without her endorsement.

Simultaneously, Queer Nation — formed in New York in 1990, an offshoot of ACT UP — had adopted “queer” as a defiant self-designation, a reclamation of a term that had been used as a slur. “We’re here, we’re queer, get used to it” became the slogan of a political movement that embraced visibility, confrontation, and the refusal of assimilation. The academic field of queer theory and the political movement’s adoption of “queer” developed in parallel, in complex relation to each other, never fully coinciding.

This founding ambivalence — “queer” as a critical analytics that resists identity categorisation, and “queer” as a political identity that mobilises around shared vulnerability — was built into the field from its first moment. It has not been resolved. Every subsequent theoretical debate in the field can be understood, in part, as a different way of negotiating this founding tension: between the anti-identitarian critical impulse that the theoretical project requires, and the identitarian political impulse that the conditions of queer life generate.

## Section III — Expanding and Consolidating: The 1990s

### 3.1 — AIDS, ACT UP, and the Politics of Mourning

The AIDS crisis was not merely a backdrop to queer theory’s development in the 1990s. It was a direct generator of the field’s most important theoretical commitments — a political emergency that made certain theoretical questions urgent in ways that purely academic agendas would not have produced.

By 1990, AIDS had killed tens of thousands of people in the United States, the majority of them gay and bisexual men and intravenous drug users, a disproportionate number of them people of colour. The Reagan and Bush administrations had responded with systematic neglect. The mainstream media had largely ignored the epidemic until it appeared to threaten heterosexual people. The medical establishment had moved slowly, impeded by homophobia, by the social stigma attached to the communities most affected, and by the structural inadequacies of a healthcare system that had no apparatus for the kind of rapid mobilisation the crisis required.

ACT UP (AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power), founded in New York in 1987, responded with a model of political activism that would influence queer theory’s understanding of political practice: direct action, confrontational visibility, the deliberate disruption of the normalcy that was enabling neglect, and the insistence on the grievability of AIDS deaths — on the demand that the deaths of gay men, of drug users, of people of colour, of people who had been declared socially expendable, should be publicly acknowledged, publicly mourned, and publicly protested.

Douglas Crimp’s edited volume *AIDS: Cultural Analysis / Cultural Activism* (1988) and his essay “Mourning and Militancy” (1989) established the theoretical coordinates of this political practice: the argument that mourning and political militancy were not opposites but were necessarily entangled, that the refusal to acknowledge AIDS deaths was an act of political violence, and that the insistence on public grief was a form of political resistance. Crimp’s work prefigured Butler’s later development of **grievability** — the concept that whose deaths count as deaths, whose losses count as losses, is a political question with life-and-death consequences.

The AIDS crisis made visible what the founding texts had undertheorised: the specific vulnerability of racialised gay men, of trans women — particularly trans women of colour who were among the most affected populations and among the least visible in the political response — and of sex workers whose survival strategies placed them at elevated risk and whose stigmatisation placed them outside the frame of public sympathy. The whiteness of the canonical queer subject, visible to the communities most severely affected by AIDS, was not yet visible as a theoretical problem in the founding texts. The AIDS crisis began to make it visible.

### 3.2 — Lesbian and Bisexual Inclusion and the Limits of “Queer”

The emergence of “queer theory” as a field in the early 1990s immediately raised the question of who it was for. The founding texts — Sedgwick’s *Epistemology of the Closet*, Butler’s *Gender Trouble* — addressed sexuality and gender in ways that were not gender-specific in their explicit theoretical claims, but whose examples, whose cultural references, and whose implicit subjects were predominantly male. Lesbian experience and theory were either absent or assimilated into a gender-neutral “queer” that, critics argued, was actually organised around male homosexuality.

Teresa de Lauretis’s later work on lesbian sexuality developed precisely this critique: the field she had named was not adequately engaging the specificity of lesbian desire and lesbian representation. Elizabeth Grosz’s *Space, Time, and Perversion* (1995) developed a theory of lesbian sexuality drawing on Deleuzian philosophy that argued for the irreducibility of lesbian desire to the framework of the heterosexual matrix — that lesbian sexuality was not simply the mirror image of heterosexuality with different objects but a differently organised relationship to bodies and pleasures. Judith Roof’s work on narrative and sexuality argued that the structures of narrative itself — the movement toward resolution, the management of desire through plot — were organised around heterosexuality in ways that made the representation of lesbian desire formally, not merely thematically, difficult.

The question of bisexuality was raised with particular force by Clare Hemmings, whose work throughout the 1990s and 2000s argued that bisexual theory could not be adequately addressed by queer theory’s foundational framework: the homo/hetero binary that Sedgwick had made central to the field did not adequately account for bisexual desire, which exceeded the binary rather than inverting it. The assimilation of bisexuality into “queer” — along with everything else that exceeded heterosexual normativity — was a theoretical convenience that obscured the specific political and experiential situation of bisexual people.

Trans theory’s relationship to queer theory in the 1990s was more complex still. Sandy Stone’s “The Empire Strikes Back: A Posttranssexual Manifesto” (1991) is often cited as the founding text of transgender studies, and its opening moves were both a critique of the feminist transphobia of Janice Raymond’s *The Transsexual Empire* (1979) and an implicit critique of a queer theory that had used transsexuality as an example — Butler’s reading of drag performance as revealing the imitative structure of gender — without engaging trans people as subjects of their own theoretical production. Stone’s manifesto called for trans people to speak in their own voices rather than being spoken for by theorists who had different investments in what their lives meant.

### 3.3 — The Anti-Social Turn: Leo Bersani and the Embrace of Negativity

The anti-social tendency in queer theory — the argument that queerness should embrace rather than domesticate its genuinely disruptive potential — has its clearest articulation before Edelman in the work of Leo Bersani. His essay “Is the Rectum a Grave?” (1987), published in the *October* special issue on AIDS, was a provocation that took as its starting point the conservative fantasy of the gay male as a figure of sexual excess and death — and then asked what would happen if queers embraced rather than refused this fantasy.

Bersani’s argument was not simply a reclamation of gay male sexuality’s transgressive potential. It was a philosophical argument about the relationship between sexuality and the self. Sexual pleasure, Bersani argued, particularly in its most intense forms, involves a shattering of the self — a dissolution of the boundaries of the coherent, socially functional subject. This is what heterosexual culture finds threatening about male homosexuality, and what homophobic discourse has always associated with femininity: the willingness to be, in Bersani’s terms, “a woman.” The rectum is a grave — not literally, not as homophobic discourse claimed, but in the sense that intense sexual experience, when it shatters the self, constitutes a kind of death of the coherent subject.

*Homos* (1995) developed this argument into a critique of queer theory’s assimilationist tendencies. The field’s investment in the legibility and recognition of queer subjects within liberal frameworks of rights and inclusion — an investment that Bersani identified in Butler’s emphasis on intelligibility as a precondition for political life — was, he argued, a betrayal of the genuinely anti-social potential that homosexuality carried. This argument directly prefigured Edelman’s *No Future* (2004), and its racialized blind spots — the figure of the shattered self as unmarked white male, the lack of attention to the differential consequences of anti-sociality for racialised queer subjects — were present in Bersani’s work before Edelman made them visible through the critique of anti-futurity.

### 3.4 — Michael Warner and Queer Publicness

Michael Warner’s theoretical contributions to the field in the 1990s developed along a different axis from the anti-social turn. His edited volume *Fear of a Queer Planet* (1993) gathered the field’s emerging practitioners around the question of queer politics in a broader sense: not merely the politics of sexual identity but the politics of sexual culture, public space, and social norms.

Warner’s most important theoretical contribution was the concept of the **counterpublic** — developed most fully in his collaboration with Lauren Berlant in the essay “Sex in Public” (1998), which appeared in the same year as Warner’s book *The Trouble with Normal*. A counterpublic is a public formed around a form of address, a set of concerns, and a mode of discourse that is not simply a subset of the dominant public but constitutes itself partly through its difference from and opposition to it. Queer counterpublics — the bar, the bathhouse, the underground performance space, the zine, the specific forms of public sex that Warner documented in his analysis of New York’s sexual culture — were not merely private spaces or subcultural niches. They were sites of alternative public life, organised around forms of intimacy, pleasure, and sociality that the dominant public sphere pathologised and excluded.

“Sex in Public” developed this argument into an explicit critique of what Berlant and Warner called the **intimate public sphere** of American culture: the way in which heterosexual domesticity — the nuclear family, the couple, the home — had been established as the only legitimate form of public intimacy, with all other forms of intimate life required to remain private or to be constituted as threats to public order. The queer counterpublic was not simply a refuge from this normativity. It was a site of resistance to it — a demonstration that other forms of life were possible, that intimacy could be organised differently, that the demand for privatisation and domestication was not simply natural but politically enforced. This argument anticipated the homonormativity critique of the 2000s: the question of whether mainstream LGBT politics was embracing the intimate public sphere rather than challenging it.

## Section IV — The Critique of Normativity: Early 2000s

### 4.1 — Edelman’s *No Future* (2004) and Anti-Futurity

Lee Edelman’s *No Future: Queer Theory and the Death Drive* is the most theoretically provocative text of the 2000s and the point at which the anti-normative commitments of queer theory reached their logical extreme. It is also the text that most directly forced the field to confront the racial epistemology of its own theoretical commitments — not by acknowledging that epistemology but by prompting others to name it.

The argument proceeds from the observation that political discourse — across the ideological spectrum — is organised around the figure of **the Child**: not actual children, but the symbolic Child who represents innocence, reproductive futurity, and the social investment in a normative future. Every political position appeals to the future we are building for our children. To threaten the Child — to be associated with the destruction of childhood innocence, with sexual danger, with the refusal of reproductive normativity — is to be positioned as the enemy of society itself. And queerness, in Edelman’s analysis, has always been assigned exactly this position: the queer as the threat to the Child, the enemy of the future.

Edelman’s response to this positioning is shocking in its directness: rather than denying the association or claiming a place within the normative future, he argues that queers should embrace their assigned position. The death drive — Lacan’s term for the force in the psyche that works against the pleasure principle, against social reproduction, against the Symbolic Order’s demand for coherent subjecthood — is, in Edelman’s reading, the truth of queerness. **Sinthomosexuality** — his portmanteau of Lacan’s *sinthome* and homosexuality — is the queer subject’s embrace of the death drive, the refusal of redemption into the social order, the insistence on jouissance over the promise of a normative future.

The argument’s strengths are considerable. It refuses the naïveté of assimilationist politics with theoretical rigour. It identifies the specific political mechanism — the figure of the Child — through which normative politics excludes queer life. And it names, with unusual precision, the bind in which queer politics has historically found itself: the demand to demonstrate that gay people are just like straight people, just as invested in the future, just as capable of participating in the reproductive social order — a demand that requires the disavowal of everything that makes queerness threatening.

But the argument’s racialized blindnesses were immediately apparent to readers for whom the question of futurity was not a theoretical choice but a material necessity. It is a specific kind of subject who can afford to embrace the death drive: a subject whose present is comfortable enough to permit the aestheticisation of negativity, whose life is not already being destroyed by premature death, whose political options include the gesture of refusal. For queers of colour, trans women of colour, queer people navigating poverty and state violence, the refusal of the future is not a theoretical position. It is a description of existing conditions imposed from outside. Muñoz’s *Cruising Utopia* (2009) would make this critique systematically; it was visible to many readers of *No Future* before Muñoz articulated it.

### 4.2 — Homonormativity: Lisa Duggan and the Critique of Assimilation

Lisa Duggan’s essay “The New Homonormativity: The Sexual Politics of Neoliberalism” (2002) named a political formation that the field’s theoretical debates had been circling without naming. Homonormativity is a politics that does not contest dominant heteronormative assumptions and institutions but upholds and sustains them while promising the possibility of a demobilised gay constituency and a privatised, depoliticised gay culture anchored in domesticity and consumption. Marriage equality, military inclusion, adoption rights, the right to serve openly in the armed forces — the agenda that mainstream LGBT organisations pursued in the 2000s and 2010s — was, in Duggan’s analysis, not liberation but assimilation: the integration of (some) gay people into the normative social order at the cost of abandoning the critique of that order and the most marginalised members of queer communities.

Homonormativity named the way in which the victories of mainstream LGBT politics were organised around the inclusion of normatively constituted gay subjects — white, middle-class, domestically coupled, economically productive — while rendering invisible and politically expendable those who could not or would not conform to this normativity: trans people, queers of colour, sex workers, people in poverty, those whose sexual and relational lives exceeded the couple form. The gay couple seeking the right to marry was not the representative of queer liberation. It was the representative of homonormativity: the claim to rights within the normative order, purchased at the price of the critique of that order.

The concept became one of the field’s most widely used — and most debated. Its critics argued that Duggan’s analysis was insufficiently attentive to the real material benefits that legal recognition provided to gay and lesbian people — that the critique of marriage equality as homonormative was easier to sustain from a position of privilege than from the position of someone whose partner’s immigration status or hospital visitation rights or inheritance were at stake. The debate between critique of homonormativity and recognition of its material stakes was never fully resolved; it was extended and complicated by Puar’s homonationalism, which showed how homonormativity operated not only at the domestic but at the international level.

### 4.3 — Trans Studies Emerges as a Distinct Field

Trans studies’ emergence as a field distinct from queer theory — though in ongoing dialogue with it — was one of the most significant intellectual developments of the early 2000s. Sandy Stone’s “The Empire Strikes Back: A Posttranssexual Manifesto” (1991) had established the field’s founding problematic: the demand that trans people be the subjects rather than the objects of theoretical production, that their lives be engaged on their own terms rather than as examples for theories developed elsewhere.

Susan Stryker’s foundational work — including her essay “My Words to Victor Frankenstein Above the Village of Chamounix: Performing Transgender Rage” (1994), which became a touchstone for the field’s affective politics, and her co-editing (with Stephen Whittle) of the *Transgender Studies Reader* (2006) — established the institutional infrastructure and the theoretical agenda of trans studies as a named field. The *Transgender Studies Reader*‘s comprehensive survey of the field’s development made visible the extent to which trans theory had been developing in parallel with queer theory, often in response to it, and often without adequate acknowledgment from queer theory’s canonical texts.

The relationship between queer theory and trans studies was immediately complex and productive. Queer theory had, in Butler’s work and elsewhere, used transsexuality and gender transition as theoretical examples — drag as revealing the imitative structure of gender, the transgendered body as challenging the naturalness of the sex/gender distinction — without adequately engaging the specificity of trans experience, trans embodiment, or trans political life. Trans studies challenged queer theory to account for the materiality of bodily transition, for the political stakes of medical authority over trans bodies, and for the difference between using trans lives as theoretical examples and engaging trans people as agents of their own theorisation.

The challenge generated productive friction. Trans scholars working within queer theory’s framework — including J. Jack Halberstam’s *Female Masculinity* (1998) and *In a Queer Time and Place* (2005) — demonstrated that trans experience could be theorised within queer theory’s conceptual apparatus while challenging that apparatus to expand. But trans scholars working from specifically trans perspectives — Stone, Stryker, and increasingly Aren Aizura, Toby Beauchamp, and others — insisted that the expansion was not sufficient: that trans studies required its own theoretical frameworks, not merely the application of existing queer theory to trans lives.

### 4.4 — Ahmed’s *Queer Phenomenology* (2006) and the Affective Turn

Sara Ahmed’s *Queer Phenomenology: Orientations, Objects, Others* (2006) opened a new methodological register for the field at precisely the moment when the anti-social/utopian debate threatened to exhaust the theoretical energy that had been produced by the initial encounter with Foucault, Sedgwick, and Butler.

Ahmed’s approach was phenomenological rather than genealogical or psychoanalytic. Sexual orientation, she argued, could be understood literally as **orientation** — the direction of the body toward certain objects and away from others. Phenomenology’s analysis of how bodies take up space, how they feel at home or out of place in specific configurations of the world, how they reach toward some objects and recoil from others, provided a framework for understanding sexuality as a bodily orientation in the world rather than merely a psychic structure or a discursive production.

Heterosexuality, in this framework, was a **straightening**: the process by which bodies are oriented toward reproductive objects, toward certain futures, toward the specific spatial and temporal configurations that reproductive normativity requires. Queerness was a **disorientation**: not merely a different orientation but the experience of losing one’s footing, of finding oneself out of place in the configurations that dominant culture takes to be natural. And rather than mourning this disorientation — rather than seeking to reorient toward recognisable objects and legible futures — Ahmed asked what perceptions and relations became possible from the disoriented position.

This was not a rejection of Butler or Foucault but a supplement: an account of what was happening at the level of embodied experience that the discursive-genealogical apparatus could not fully capture. Ahmed’s work connected to the broader **affective turn** that was reshaping the field in the mid-2000s — Sedgwick’s late engagement with Silvan Tomkins (*Touching Feeling*, 2003), Ann Cvetkovich’s work on depression and trauma (*An Archive of Feelings*, 2003), and the growing literature on shame (David Halperin and Valerie Traub’s *Gay Shame*, 2009) as a queer affect with specifically political dimensions. Feeling was becoming one of the field’s primary theoretical objects, and the methodological approaches adequate to feeling — phenomenology, affect theory, attention to the texture of ordinary life — were beginning to supplement the genealogical and deconstructive methods that the founding texts had established.

## Section V — Race, Empire, and the Second Generation: Mid-2000s to 2010s

### 5.1 — Puar’s Homonationalism (2007)

Jasbir Puar’s *Terrorist Assemblages: Homonationalism in Queer Times* (2007) named a mechanism that queer theory had been entirely failing to theorise: **the state’s use of gay rights as imperial currency**. The argument emerged from the specific historical context of the years following September 11, 2001, in which Western governments — particularly the United States and Israel — developed a practice of citing their domestic LGBT rights frameworks as evidence of civilisational superiority, a marker of liberal modernity to be contrasted with the presumed barbarism of Muslim-majority societies.

This was not liberation. It was the conscription of the gay body into the service of racism and imperialism: the cause of LGBT rights deployed to legitimate military occupation and drone warfare, the celebration of gay visibility in Tel Aviv used to obscure the conditions of Palestinian life under occupation — what Puar and others called **pinkwashing**. The homonational subject — the assimilated, normatively coupled, middle-class, predominantly white gay or lesbian person whose queerness had been domesticated enough to serve nationalist purposes — was not the representative of queer liberation. They were the representative of a new formation in which some queer subjects were included in the nation precisely so that others could be more thoroughly excluded and targeted.

Puar’s theoretical apparatus drew on Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari’s concept of the **assemblage** — a shifting configuration of bodies, affects, technologies, discourses, and power relations — rather than on the identity categories that intersectionality theory used. The assemblage framework allowed Puar to track how the same body could be simultaneously protected (as a gay citizen), targeted (as racially coded), and commodified (as a consumer) without requiring that these positions be understood as the intersection of separate identity categories. It was a more dynamic, more processual account of how power operated on and through bodies than either the intersectional or the performative frameworks could provide.

*The Right to Maim* (2017) extended this analysis through the concept of **debilitation** — a mode of power distinct from both Foucault’s biopower (making live) and Achille Mbembe’s necropolitics (making die). Debilitation is the deliberate impairing of life below the threshold of death: the production of populations that are not killed but rendered non-functional, dependent, economically useless, and surveyable. This was, Puar argued, not a failure of biopolitical power but one of its primary operations — visible in Israeli policy toward Palestinians, in the management of chronic illness and disability under neoliberal healthcare regimes, and in the systematic production of precarious life among racialised and economically marginalised populations.

### 5.2 — Muñoz’s *Cruising Utopia* (2009) and Queer Futurity

José Esteban Muñoz’s *Cruising Utopia: The Then and There of Queer Futurity* (2009) is the most important counter-text to Edelman’s *No Future* and the most successful integration of queer of colour critique with the field’s theoretical apparatus. It is also, since Muñoz’s death at fifty in 2013, a text haunted by its own unfinishedness — a quality that gives its arguments about queer futurity an additional pathos.

The book opens with its central proposition: “Queerness is not yet here. Queerness is an ideality. Put another way, we are not yet queer.” This is a direct counter to Edelman: queerness is not what must be refused but what must be imagined into being. Not the normative future of reproductive futurism, and not the anti-social embrace of the death drive, but a **third temporal orientation** — the not-yet, the horizon, the concrete utopia that is anticipated in present practice without being fully realized anywhere.

Muñoz drew on Ernst Bloch’s concept of **concrete utopia** — as distinct from abstract utopia, which is mere wishful thinking divorced from present conditions — to develop his account of queer cultural practice as the site where utopian content is carried and transmitted. The ballroom scene, Frank O’Hara’s poetry, Kevin Aviance’s voguing, the underground performance spaces of New York’s queer scene: these are not merely entertainments or subcultural niches. They are sites where the queer ephemeral archive carries traces of a different world — where social arrangements are rehearsed that do not yet exist anywhere in fully realized form, and whose value lies precisely in their anticipatory character.

The racial epistemology of Muñoz’s argument was its most significant political contribution. Anti-futurity is a luxury — available only to those whose present is liveable enough to permit the aestheticisation of negativity. For queers of colour, trans women of colour, and queer people navigating poverty and state violence, the present is frequently not survivable, and the future is not a normative trap but a material necessity. The argument did not merely supplement Edelman’s with a different cultural politics. It demonstrated that the theoretical position of anti-futurity was racially situated — that it was possible as a theoretical position only from specific subject positions that the theory itself had failed to acknowledge.

### 5.3 — Snorton, Haritaworn, Ferguson: Completing the Critique

Three texts completed the project that the field’s founding generation had left unfinished: the demonstration that race and colonialism were constitutive of the heterosexual matrix rather than supplementary to it.

C. Riley Snorton’s *Black on Both Sides: A Racial History of Trans Identity* (2017) approached the problem from the direction of medical history. The concept of **fungibility** — the treatment of Black bodies under slavery as interchangeable, available for use, exploitable without remainder — described a specific property of the enslaved body that enabled its use as material for the medical experimentation and surgical development that produced the techniques enabling mid-twentieth century trans medicine. Christine Jorgensen’s celebrated 1952 transition, the procedures that made it possible, the medical knowledge that enabled it: these were built on the exploitation of Black bodies subsequently erased from the history they had enabled. Trans identity was not only gendered — it was racially constituted, and the constitution had a specific history of extraction and erasure.

Jin Haritaworn’s *Queer Necropolitics* (co-edited with Adi Kuntsman and Silvia Posocco, 2014) brought the necropolitics framework — Achille Mbembe’s extension of Foucault’s biopolitics to account for the sovereign decision about who lives and who dies, always already racial in its operation — to bear on queer life. **Queer necropolitics** named the differential distribution of queer life and queer death along racial, colonial, and class lines: the process by which the inclusion of some queers in the national body politic was achieved through the reinforced disposability of others. Haritaworn’s work on Berlin’s queer scene demonstrated how the city’s celebrated queer culture was constituted through the racialisation and exclusion of Turkish-German and other migrant communities — how queer liberalism and racial nationalism were not opposites but co-constitutive formations.

Roderick Ferguson’s *Aberrations in Black: Toward a Queer of Color Critique* (2004) provided the methodological framework for all of this work: **queer of colour critique** as a named methodology, the intersectional analysis of how race, gender, sexuality, and class produce differential subject positions, with particular attention to how those formations correspond with and diverge from nationalist ideals. Ferguson’s central demonstration — that American sociology had produced normative models of Black life that were simultaneously racialising and heterosexualising — showed how academic disciplines participated in the production of racial-sexual norms that queer theory’s canonical texts had failed to interrogate.

### 5.4 — Berlant’s *Cruel Optimism* (2011) and the Politics of the Ordinary

Lauren Berlant’s *Cruel Optimism* redirected the field’s attention from the exceptional — the drag queen, the anti-social gesture, the utopian performance — to the **ordinary**: to what most people actually do, which is neither refuse nor transcend but persist. This redirection was both methodological and political. It was methodological because it insisted on taking seriously the actual practices of people navigating conditions they did not choose, rather than the idealised political subjects of both assimilationist and anti-social theory. It was political because it named the specific mechanism — cruel optimism — through which the normative order reproduced itself not through force or ideology alone but through the subject’s own attachment to the promises that the normative order extended and systematically failed to keep.

A relation of cruel optimism exists when something you desire is actually an obstacle to your flourishing. The good life promise — upward mobility, romantic love, bodily health, political representation — had structurally broken down for large portions of the population under neoliberal restructuring. But attachment to the promise persisted, because the promise was not merely a desired outcome but the **structure of desiring itself**: the framework within which the subject’s investment in the future was organised. To relinquish the promise was not simply to accept a disappointment. It was to lose the orientation toward the future that made the present legible.

The **impasse** — the temporal structure in which the present cannot resolve itself through the usual means — and **lateral agency** — the ordinary human capacity to manage, to improvise, to find small sustainments within conditions that are not changing — became the field’s most politically honest account of what most queer (and non-queer) life under contemporary capitalism actually looks like. Berlant’s refusal to idealise either the subject or the political possibilities available to them, their insistence on attending to the texture of ordinary life with the same analytical rigour that the field had applied to exceptional cases, made *Cruel Optimism* one of the most politically significant theoretical texts of the decade.

## Section VI — Expansion and Multiplication: The 2010s

### 6.1 — Trans Studies Consolidates and Challenges Queer Theory

The 2010s saw trans studies move from emerging to established — from a field that was developing its theoretical apparatus in relation to queer theory, often reactively, to a field with its own canonical texts, its own journals, and its own research agenda, in productive but sometimes tense dialogue with queer theory.

Dean Spade’s *Normal Life: Administrative Violence, Critical Trans Politics, and the Limits of Law* (2011) applied queer of colour critique and Foucauldian biopolitics to trans legal activism — arguing that legal recognition strategies, however important in their immediate effects, often entrenched the administrative systems (welfare bureaucracies, prison systems, healthcare gatekeeping) that produced the most severe harm to the most marginalised trans people. Spade’s work bridged the theoretical generation of Puar and Ferguson with the specific political conditions of trans life in the neoliberal United States, and it offered one of the field’s most sustained accounts of how abstract theoretical commitments (the critique of homonormativity, the analysis of necropolitics) translated into specific political strategies (mutual aid, decarceration, healthcare access through community clinics).

Jules Gill-Peterson’s *Histories of the Transgender Child* (2018) provided the historical grounding that trans studies had lacked: a careful genealogy of how the medical category of transsexuality was produced in relation to intersex conditions and the management of “gender deviance” in childhood, drawing on archival research at Johns Hopkins and other medical institutions. Gill-Peterson’s account showed how the medical management of trans and intersex children was organised around the production of normative binary sex — and how Black and brown children had been subjected to more aggressive and more experimental interventions than white children, their bodies serving as the testing ground for techniques subsequently applied to white patients.

C. Riley Snorton and Jin Haritaworn’s “Trans Necropolitics: A Transnational Reflection on Violence, Death, and the Trans of Color Afterlife” (2013) — published before Snorton’s monograph — established trans necropolitics as a specific theoretical framework within the broader necropolitics literature: the differential exposure of trans people of colour to premature death, to state violence, to the specific forms of ungrievability that Haritaworn’s work had identified. Trans women of colour were rendered ungrievable in life and only mourned as symbols after death — without the structural conditions of their deaths being addressed or the political work required to transform those conditions being undertaken.

### 6.2 — Crip Theory and the Disability Studies Encounter

The encounter between queer theory and disability studies — crystallised in Robert McRuer’s *Crip Theory: Cultural Signs of Queerness and Disability* (2006) — generated one of the decade’s most generative theoretical conjunctions. McRuer’s central move was to identify **compulsory able-bodiedness** as structurally analogous to compulsory heterosexuality: both were normative systems that produced their subjects through the compelled performance of normalcy, both required the abjection of deviant bodies and the exclusion of those who could not or would not perform the required norm, and both operated through the naturalisation of a specific bodily standard that was in fact historically produced and politically enforced.

Alison Kafer’s *Feminist, Queer, Crip* (2013) extended the conjunction in a different direction — less interested in the structural analogy between heteronormativity and able-bodiedness and more interested in what disabled people’s actual experiences of time, futurity, and political possibility could offer to queer theory’s debates about temporality and hope. The “curative imaginary” — the assumption that disability is a condition to be overcome, that the disabled body is always on the way toward the normative body it is not yet — was, Kafer argued, the disability studies equivalent of reproductive futurism: a temporal ideology that located the value of disabled lives in their potential to become something else.

The crip-queer conjunction came into fullest expression in J. Logan Smilges’s *Crip Negativity* (2023), which brought together the negativity tradition in queer theory (Edelman, Bersani, Love’s “feeling backward”) with crip theory’s engagement with disability as a site of political, affective, and ontological knowledge. Smilges argued that crip negativity — the refusal of the curative narrative, the embrace of the disabled body’s “wrong” relationship to normative time and capacity — was not merely analogous to queer negativity but specifically located within and constituted by it: the two forms of negativity were co-constitutive in the specific bodies and lives that most acutely embodied both.

Jasbir Puar’s debilitation concept connected the crip-queer conjunction to the necropolitics and colonial violence literatures: debilitation as a mode of colonial and neoliberal power, the production of disabled and debilitated populations as a feature rather than a bug of the systems being analysed. The encounter between crip theory, queer necropolitics, and colonial studies produced one of the decade’s most theoretically rich research programmes — though one whose implications for political strategy remained actively contested.

### 6.3 — Global South and Decolonial Queer Scholarship

The 2010s saw sustained pressure within the field to move beyond the Anglo-American axis — and not merely through the addition of non-Western case studies to frameworks developed elsewhere, but through the development of distinct theoretical frameworks grounded in non-Western intellectual traditions and non-Western political conditions.

Martin Manalansan IV’s *Global Divas* (2003), Evelyn Blackwood’s *Falling into the Lesbi World* (2010), and Ara Wilson’s *The Intimate Economies of Bangkok* (2004) had established Southeast Asian queer formations as theoretical resources rather than merely empirical examples. The bakla, the tomboi, and the tom/dee formations were organised around gender role rather than object choice — a structural difference from the Western homo/hetero binary that was not merely cultural variation but a different theoretical architecture for thinking about sexuality, desire, and gender transgression.

Joseph Massad’s *Desiring Arabs* (2007) made the most provocative claim: that the Gay International — the network of Western and Westernised LGBT organisations and activists — was not extending rights to populations that already existed as gay and lesbian subjects but was producing new identity formations where local formations had been differently organised, in doing so simultaneously enabling new forms of political organisation and foreclosing the specific forms of resistance and survival that local formations had developed. The controversy this argument generated — particularly the critique from Arab queer activists who pointed to the persecution they faced before and independently of Western gay rights discourse — illuminated a fundamental tension in Global South queer theory between decolonial critique and the immediate political needs of communities facing violence.

Peter Jackson’s scholarship on Thai sexual culture — documenting the karmic framework for understanding kathoey gender crossing, the colonial import of Western sexological categories through Thai medical education, and the resulting “double taxonomy” in which kathoey were simultaneously understood through karmic and medical-psychological frameworks — provided the most sustained and methodologically careful account of how colonial sedimentation transformed a specific Buddhist-majority culture’s approach to gender and sexuality. Jackson’s concept of Thailand as “tolerant but unaccepting” — a society that accommodated kathoey visibility without offering genuine affirmation — was a theoretically significant contribution to the field’s understanding of how tolerance and acceptance were different political formations, and how the former could function to contain the latter.

### 6.4 — Queer Temporalities and Affective Queer Studies

Elizabeth Freeman’s *Time Binds: Queer Temporalities, Queer Histories* (2010) and the broader queer temporality literature extended and complicated the Edelman-Muñoz debate about futurity into a sustained theoretical engagement with how time itself was organised through heteronormativity — what Freeman called **chrononormativity**: the use of time as a technique for the production of normative subjects.

Chrononormativity was the synchronisation of bodies with the social: the organisation of the life course — childhood, adolescence, adult coupling, reproduction, middle age, retirement, death — into a sequence that naturalised reproductive normativity and marginalised those whose lives did not follow it. Queer life was characterised by a different relationship to time — what Halberstam’s *In a Queer Time and Place* (2005) had called “queer time”: organised around pleasure, friendship, and community rather than around the reproductive life course, and therefore illegible to the chrononormative frameworks that dominated both public policy and everyday social life.

Freeman’s contribution was to add the temporal dimension to the queer archive: the concept of **temporal drag**, the experience of being caught in a different time from the present, of inhabiting histories that dominant culture had declared over, of maintaining attachments to the past that chrononormativity required one to relinquish. Queer historiography — the writing of queer history — was itself a form of temporal queerness: the insistence on the relevance of past queer lives to present queer politics, the refusal to accept that history moved in a straight line from repression to liberation.

The affective turn deepened in the 2010s into a sustained engagement with the specific feelings of queer life. Ann Cvetkovich’s *Depression: A Public Feeling* (2012) argued that depression among queer people was not a medical condition requiring clinical management but a **political feeling** that indexed structural conditions: the chronic low-level violence of heteronormative culture, the psychic cost of invisibility and misrecognition, the specific form of exhaustion produced by the persistent requirement of negotiating one’s existence in contexts not designed for it. The pathologisation of this depression through clinical frameworks was itself a form of depoliticisation: the transformation of structural suffering into individual disorder.

## Section VII — The Current Landscape: 2020–2026

### 7.1 — Trans Rights Under Attack and the Theoretical Response

The current political moment is defined, in relation to queer and trans theory, by intensifying backlash and the theoretical responses it has generated. The 2020s have seen legislative attacks on trans healthcare (particularly for minors) proliferate across the United States, the United Kingdom, and multiple European countries. The UK’s Cass Review (2024) and its subsequent impact on NHS trans healthcare provision; the 2025 US executive orders eliminating federal recognition of gender identity; the UK Supreme Court ruling (2025) defining “woman” and “sex” in biological terms for the purposes of the Equality Act; Hungarian bans on pride events; the proliferation of bathroom bills and anti-trans sports legislation across American states — these constitute not merely a political backlash but a **juridification of sex**: the deployment of legal authority to enforce binary biological sex categories in ways that directly affect the lives, healthcare access, and legal recognition of trans and gender-nonconforming people.

The theoretical responses have been rapid and in some cases theoretically significant. The concept of **enforced legibility** — the way in which hostile states weaponise the demand for categorical clarity, requiring bodies to be unambiguously legible as male or female within binary frameworks as a condition of access to healthcare, legal protection, and public space — has emerged as a theoretically precise account of the mechanism through which the current backlash operates. It extends Foucault’s analysis of the production of sexuality through medical and legal discourse into the specific contemporary configuration of trans bodies as targets of administrative and juridical violence.

The trans necropolitics literature has intensified in response to the increase in violence against trans people — particularly trans women of colour — that the backlash has both reflected and produced. The demand for public mourning that Butler’s work established in the AIDS context, and that Haritaworn developed in the trans context, has become more urgent as the number of ungrievable trans deaths increases. The annual Transgender Day of Remembrance — documenting trans people killed each year, disproportionately trans women of colour in the Global South — is both a political practice of public mourning and a site of ongoing theoretical engagement with the politics of grievability.

### 7.2 — Trans-Crip Theory: The Most Generative Current Synthesis

The conjunction of trans studies and disability studies is arguably the most theoretically productive site in contemporary queer studies. J. Logan Smilges’s *Crip Negativity* (2023) — bringing together crip theory’s engagement with disability and queer theory’s negativity tradition — generated immediate and sustained engagement. The concept of **queercrip joy** — not as an opposite or replacement for pain but as simultaneous and co-constitutive with it, a specific affective formation that inhabits the ground of ongoing suffering without being defined entirely by it — extended the crip and queer literatures on affect in ways that have been widely taken up.

The concept of **gender euphoria** has emerged as a theoretically significant object in the trans-crip conjunction: the specific affective experience of gender-affirming moments — of being seen and named correctly, of inhabiting one’s body in a way that aligns with one’s sense of self — as a counterpart to the more extensively theorised gender dysphoria. The theoretical engagement with euphoria as a positive affective experience has been partially shaped by the need to respond to a clinical and political landscape in which trans healthcare is being restricted on the grounds that gender dysphoria can be managed without transition: taking gender euphoria seriously as a distinct and irreducible affective experience challenges the framework that treats transition as merely the treatment of dysphoria rather than the enabling of flourishing.

Alex Luscombe and others have developed methodological frameworks for what they call **trans-crip methodology** — approaches to research design, data collection, and analysis that are adequate to the overlapping and intersecting experiences of trans and disabled people without assimilating those experiences to either a single trans identity or a single disabled identity. These methodological developments have been among the most practically consequential contributions of the current theoretical moment.

### 7.3 — Queer AI Studies: An Emergent Field

The intersection of queer theory and artificial intelligence is one of the fastest-growing areas in the current theoretical landscape — and one of the most genuinely novel, in the sense that it addresses a technology that did not exist when the field was founded and whose implications could not have been anticipated by its founding texts.

The QueerInAI workshop — held annually in conjunction with major machine learning conferences — has been the primary institutional site for the development of this research programme. The workshop’s output has addressed: how large language models encode and reproduce binary and essentialist assumptions about gender and sexuality; how facial recognition and other classification systems misidentify gender-nonconforming people; how the training data on which AI systems depend overrepresents normative subjects while underrepresenting queer, trans, and non-Western populations; and how AI-assisted hiring, housing, and healthcare systems amplify existing discriminatory patterns in ways that are both less visible and more pervasive than human discrimination.

Theoretically, the engagement with AI has drawn productively on Puar’s assemblage framework — the critique of how identity categories are reified by computational systems that require stable, classifiable inputs — and on Foucault’s analysis of the production of subjects through classification systems. The AI system that assigns a gender to a face, or that classifies a relationship as romantic or non-romantic, or that allocates health risk scores by demographic profile, is producing subjects in the Foucauldian sense: constituting them through the very act of classification. The queer critique of this production is not merely that it misclassifies individuals but that the classificatory apparatus itself is a form of normative power, producing the categories it appears merely to observe.

**Data Resurgence** frameworks — drawing on Indigenous data sovereignty movements, decolonial data practices, and queer community-based research models — have proposed alternative approaches to data collection and analysis that refuse the extractive logic of mainstream AI training: practices that keep data in the control of the communities it concerns, that are accountable to those communities rather than to the commercial or research interests of AI developers, and that refuse the demand for the kind of categorical legibility that classification systems require.

### 7.4 — The Global South and Non-Western Epistemological Pressures

The pressure to move beyond the Anglo-American axis has intensified in the 2020s, both theoretically and institutionally. Southern Queeroscopic Interactionism (SQIT), developed by Apryl Williams and colleagues and published in 2026, synthesises Black Queer Theory, Queer of Color Critique, Two Souths Theory, Critical Race Theory, Intersectionality, and Symbolic Interactionism into a framework that reconceptualises the South — the American South, the Global South, and other marginalised spatial formations — not as a monolithic region but as a layered landscape of intersecting geographies, cultural productions, and racialised gendered economies of meaning. SQIT is significant not merely as a regional framework but as a demonstration of what it means to develop an indigenous theoretical methodology rather than applying metropolitan frameworks to local cases.

Buddhist queer theory has remained underdeveloped, despite the resources — documented in the completed manuscript that this article complements — that Madhyamaka philosophy, the doctrine of anattā, and the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*‘s teaching on gender emptiness provide. The political landscape of Buddhist-majority societies is complex: in Thailand, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, and Myanmar, Buddhist institutions have often been aligned with nationalist and heteronormative political formations, while Buddhist activists and scholars have developed arguments for queer-affirming readings of canonical Buddhist teaching. The gap between these two positions — institutional Buddhism’s colonial-era absorption of Victorian sexual ethics, and the canonical texts’ relative silence on same-sex desire and their sophisticated analysis of gender emptiness — has not yet been bridged by scholarship adequate to both dimensions.

African queer studies, Latin American queer theory, and MENA queer scholarship have all developed significantly in the 2020s — generating indigenous theoretical frameworks that refuse both the universalisation of Western queer theory and the appeal to a pre-colonial authenticity that romantic nationalism would offer. The growth of queer African philosophy, including the engagement with Ubuntu ethics and its implications for thinking about sexual and gender difference in communal rather than individualist frameworks, represents one of the most significant current expansions of the field’s theoretical resources.

## Section VIII — The Field’s Recurring Fault Lines

### 8.1 — Identity vs. Anti-Identity: The Unresolved Tension

The tension between queer theory as a critique of identity categories and queer politics as the organisation of communities around shared identity has never been resolved in the field’s history — and the failure to resolve it is not a failure of the field’s intellectual work but a reflection of a genuine contradiction in the political conditions of queer life.

The founding moment staged this tension in its first form. De Lauretis coined “queer theory” as a critical analytics that would resist identity categorisation; Queer Nation adopted “queer” as a defiant collective identity for political mobilisation. Both impulses were genuine and politically necessary, and they pulled in different directions: the anti-identitarian impulse required that the field refuse the stabilisation of any particular queer subject as the representative of queer politics; the identitarian impulse required exactly the stabilisation that enabled collective action.

In the 1990s, the tension reappeared as the debate between Sedgwick’s minoritising and universalising readings: is the homo/hetero binary the specific concern of a minority defined by it, or does it structure everyone’s experience within the cultures it organises? The universalising reading — which queer theory adopted as its theoretical lever — was more analytically productive but also more politically ambiguous: it made queerness relevant to everyone while potentially dissolving the specific experiences and political needs of people most directly targeted by sexual and gender normativity.

In the 2000s, it reappeared as the conflict between Edelman’s anti-social embrace of the death drive (the refusal of any identitarian political subject) and Muñoz’s queer futurity (the utopian horizon toward which an identifiable queer community of colour was oriented). In the 2010s, it reappeared as the conflict between Puar’s assemblage theory (the dissolution of identity categories into shifting configurations of power) and the trans and crip movements’ insistence on the political necessity of identity-based claims. In the current moment, it appears as the tension between the Madhyamaka analysis of identity as ultimately empty and the conventional political necessity of identity-based rights claims for trans healthcare and legal recognition.

The tension will not be resolved because it reflects something that cannot be resolved at the theoretical level: the fact that identity categories are simultaneously politically necessary (they enable collective action, rights claims, and the visibility of specific forms of harm) and politically dangerous (they stabilise what should be unstable, exclude what they cannot accommodate, and are available for appropriation by the normative orders they contest). Every generation of queer theory has had to find a new way of navigating this contradiction — which is why the field’s history is not a linear progress toward greater theoretical sophistication but a spiral, returning to the same contradiction in new forms.

### 8.2 — Textualism vs. Embodiment: The Methodological Fault Line

The field’s methodological inheritance from Foucault is fundamentally discursivist: knowledge is produced through discourse, and the analysis of knowledge requires the analysis of discourses and their conditions of possibility. This inheritance has been enormously productive — the genealogy of sexuality, the analysis of the heterosexual matrix, the critique of homonationalism are all products of the discursive-genealogical apparatus. It has also produced a systematic inability to engage what bodies know through their own processes of transformation.

The challenge to textualism came first from phenomenology — Ahmed’s redirection of queer theory toward the body’s orientation in space, Grosz’s engagement with corporeal feminism, the growing literature on affect and feeling as not fully captured by discourse. The challenge intensified with trans studies’ insistence on the materiality of bodily transition — that the experience of inhabiting a transitioning body could not be adequately theorised through the analysis of the discourses that constituted the trans subject. And it has become most acute in the crip-queer conjunction and the emerging engagement with altered states: the bodies of seriously ill people, of people in meditative absorption, of kink practitioners in subspace, of mystics in states of contemplative union — these are bodies producing forms of knowledge about their own constructedness that the discursivist apparatus cannot account for.

The field has expanded its methodological repertoire in response to these challenges — incorporating phenomenological approaches, affective methodologies, attention to practice and embodied experience — without replacing or adequately supplementing the discursivist core. The result is a methodological pluralism that is productive but unresolved: different methodological approaches coexist without an adequate account of how they relate to each other, which methodological apparatus is appropriate to which questions, and what a theoretically integrated account of the body as simultaneously a site of discursive inscription and an epistemological agent would look like.

### 8.3 — Futurity vs. Anti-Futurity: The Political Fault Line

The Edelman-Muñoz debate about whether queerness should embrace or refuse the future has generated more theoretical output than any other single debate in the field’s history — and it has not been resolved, because it cannot be. The debate is not ultimately about futurity as a theoretical concept. It is about what political hope requires of subjects navigating conditions that do not support their flourishing.

Edelman’s anti-futurity is a refusal of the normative promise — a refusal to invest in the future as the dominant culture constructs it, a refusal to accept the terms on which inclusion is offered. This refusal is genuinely valuable: it names the way in which queer politics has been asked to domesticate itself, to accept the terms of reproductive futurism as the price of political recognition. But it is valuable only from a position for which the current present is liveable enough to permit the luxury of refusal — a position that is racially and economically situated in ways the theory itself does not acknowledge.

Muñoz’s concrete utopia is an insistence on the possibility of a different future — a horizon toward which present practice is oriented, where the queer archive carries traces of a world not yet realised. This insistence is also genuinely valuable: it maintains the political energy that comes from the conviction that things could be otherwise, that the present is not the limit of what is possible. But it risks becoming consolatory — a horizon that never arrives, that substitutes for political engagement rather than orienting it, that converts present suffering into the fuel for a future that may never come.

Berlant’s lateral agency, Love’s backward feeling, Halberstam’s queer art of failure, the Buddhist framework’s engaged non-attachment — each of these is a different attempt to find a third position: neither the anti-social refusal of the future nor the utopian investment in it, but some orientation toward the present and the future that enables sustained political commitment without the cruel optimism that makes commitment’s failure the dissolution of the self. That this third position has not been definitively established is not a failure of the field’s theoretical work. It is evidence that the question it is trying to answer — how to sustain queer political life under conditions that do not support it — is genuinely difficult, and that the honest acknowledgment of that difficulty is itself a theoretical achievement.
