# Beyond the Melancholic Subject: Queer Theory, Buddhist Ontology, and the Global South

## A State-of-the-Field Review

-----

## Abstract

This state-of-the-field review argues that the encounter between Anglo-American queer theory, Madhyamaka Buddhist philosophy, and the queer formations of Southeast Asia and the Global South produces theoretical resources unavailable within any of those traditions considered alone. The article proceeds through three sets of destabilising encounters with the canonical triad of Foucault, Sedgwick, and Butler. The first encounter — the queer of colour critique of Muñoz, Puar, Haritaworn, Snorton, and Berlant — demonstrates that the canon’s melancholic subject is constituted by an unmarked whiteness and an absent imperialism that reproduce racial liberalism even at the framework’s most radical iterations. The second encounter — the scholarship of Southeast Asian queer formations including the Filipino bakla, the Indonesian tomboi, and the Thai kathoey — reveals that the homo/heterosexual binary central to the canon’s architecture is a culturally specific rather than universal formation, and that across much of the Global South sexuality is organised around gender role rather than object choice. The third encounter — Madhyamaka Buddhist ontology, specifically the doctrine of anattā (non-self), Nāgārjuna’s analysis of śūnyatā (emptiness), and the Vimalakīrti Sūtra’s teaching on gender emptiness — provides a more radical ontological ground for queer theory than the psychoanalytic-phenomenological frameworks Butler, Sedgwick, and Foucault inherit. The article develops a structural parallel between Butler’s passionate attachment and the Buddhist concept of upādāna (clinging), argues that the two-truths framework (conventional and ultimate truth) resolves the political-dissolution tension that Buddhist ontology generates, and proposes the bodhisattva ideal — compassionate political action without attachment to outcome — as a model for queer political engagement adequate to the present conditions of trans healthcare denial, legal rollback, and necropolitical violence. The article concludes by identifying three genuinely open questions: the current political landscape of Buddhist LGBT organising in Southeast Asia; the systematic comparative phenomenology of altered states across Buddhist meditation, kink practice, Sufi fanāʾ, and Christian unio mystica; and the clinical and care dimensions of the synthesis, particularly for oncology patients navigating the dissolution of ordinary self-structure through serious illness.

## Section I — Introduction: The Subject Queer Theory Assumes

### 1.1 — The Paradox of Subjection

In the opening pages of *The Psychic Life of Power* (1997), Judith Butler poses a question that cuts beneath the usual coordinates of political theory: if the subject is formed by the very power it might resist — if power is not merely what presses on the subject from outside but what brings the subject into existence as a subject — then what is the basis of resistance, and what does it cost? The answer Butler develops over the course of that book is one of the most unsettling in contemporary theory: **the subject cannot simply refuse the norms that injure it because those norms are the condition of its existence as a subject**. The attachment to them is not a failure of political will or a symptom of false consciousness. It is the structure of subjecthood itself under conditions of normative power.

Butler names this mechanism “passionate attachment” — the subject’s investment in the norms that simultaneously constitute and harm it, an investment so fundamental that it precedes any particular political choice or ideological persuasion. To relinquish the norm is to risk what she calls “a certain kind of annihilation” — not physical death but the dissolution of the very framework of social recognition through which the subject exists as recognisable, as present, as a person among persons. This is why the dominated so often sustain the conditions of their domination: not because they have been deceived about their interests but because the terms of domination are also the terms of their existence.

The psychic mechanism Butler identifies as producing this structure is **melancholy**, in the specifically Freudian sense developed in “Mourning and Melancholia” (1917). The melancholic subject does not mourn its losses — it incorporates them, taking the lost object into the ego, building its interior life from the residue of what it was never permitted to have. Applied to gender and sexuality under compulsory heterosexuality, this means that normative gender identities are constituted by the melancholic incorporation of foreclosed same-sex desires — desires that were never permitted to be formed as desires, that therefore cannot be mourned as losses, and that persist as the unacknowledged interior of the very identities that deny them. “The straight man,” Butler writes with characteristic precision, “becomes his male lover through the incorporation of the lost male object.” The heterosexual matrix is not the opposite of queerness. It is constituted by the queerness it forecloses.

This account of the melancholic subject has been, rightly, one of the most generative frameworks in contemporary queer theory. It explains the intensity of homophobic and transphobic reaction — not merely as prejudice but as psychic self-defence by subjects whose identities are built on the incorporated losses that gender-nonconformity threatens to make visible. It explains why the political project of queer liberation cannot be a matter of simply telling the truth about gender’s constructedness — because the truth, in this framework, is already known at some psychic level, and the knowing of it is precisely what must be managed, suppressed, and defended against. It grounds Butler’s concept of subversive repetition — resistance through citational practice that deviates from the norm — in a psychic reality that makes such repetition both necessary and never final.

And yet. The framework’s power is also its constraint. The subject Butler theorises is a very specific kind of subject: constituted through the Lacanian Symbolic Order, organised around lack and the desire for recognition, psychically structured through the internalisation of losses that have been rendered ungrievable. This is a subject with a specific philosophical genealogy — Hegel’s dialectic of recognition (*Anerkennung*), Freud’s economy of libido and loss, Lacan’s subject of the signifier — that is neither universal nor culturally neutral. It presupposes a particular account of interiority, a particular theory of how social norms become psychic structure, and a particular ontology of the self as something that persists across time through the sedimentation of incorporated losses. The queer subject of Butler’s framework is, in the precise philosophical sense, a **melancholic subject** — and melancholy, as a structure of psychic life, is not simply a human universal. It is a historically and culturally specific way of being a subject that has been theorised from within a psychoanalytic tradition developed in a particular place and time.

This is not a dismissal of Butler’s framework. It is the beginning of a question that this article takes as its central concern: **What happens to the subject of queer theory when the psychoanalytic-phenomenological assumptions of its Anglo-American canon encounter the ontological frameworks, regional formations, and embodied practices of non-Western traditions — and what political possibilities does that encounter open?**

### 1.2 — Three Encounters That Destabilise the Canon

The question is not new. It has been posed, from different directions and with different theoretical resources, by three overlapping sets of scholarly encounter that have reshaped the field over the past three decades without yet producing a synthetic account of what they together require.

The **first encounter** came from within: the queer of colour critique developed by José Esteban Muñoz, Jasbir Puar, Jin Haritaworn, C. Riley Snorton, and Roderick Ferguson, among others, which demonstrated that the canon’s universalising claims rested on an unmarked whiteness and an absent imperialism. Muñoz’s argument that Lee Edelman’s anti-futurity — the embrace of the death drive as the authentic queer political gesture — is “a luxury afforded only by those who are already in possession of a present” pointed to a racialised subject whose present is not survivable enough to permit the aestheticisation of negativity. Puar’s theory of homonationalism showed how Western states weaponised gay rights as imperial currency, folding white assimilated gay subjects into the nation while rendering racialised, trans, and poor queer subjects disposable. Snorton’s account of fungibility traced how the trans medical archive was built on Black bodies subsequently erased from the history it enabled. These were not supplementary corrections to the canon — race added here, empire acknowledged there. They were demonstrations that the canon’s subject was racially constituted from the outset, and that any framework that began from Butler’s melancholic subject without asking whose melancholy, whose foreclosed desire, and whose ungrievable loss, had already reproduced the racial liberalism it claimed to contest.

Lauren Berlant’s *Cruel Optimism* (2011) extended this critique into the temporality of ordinary political life under neoliberalism. The good life promise — upward mobility, romantic love, political representation, bodily health — had structurally broken down for large portions of the population, but attachment to the promise persisted because relinquishing it meant relinquishing the very framework of expectation and desire that gave life its forward orientation. Berlant’s concept of the impasse — the temporal structure in which the present cannot resolve itself through the usual means, cannot move forward or backward, must be navigated laterally rather than transcended — named the political-affective condition of a subject for whom Butler’s subversive repetition is simply unavailable, not because of psychic investment in the norm but because the material conditions for even the form of agency Butler’s framework requires have been systematically dismantled. The impasse is the normalisation of emergency. It is what crisis looks like when it stops being exceptional.

The **second encounter** came from outside the Anglo-American academy: the scholarship of Southeast Asian and Global South queer formations, which revealed that the homo/hetero binary central to the canon’s architecture is culturally and historically specific rather than universal. Martin Manalansan’s ethnography of Filipino gay men in diaspora showed that the *bakla* — a gender/sexuality category organised around gender crossing and the possession of a feminine soul in a male body, not around the gender of one’s desired object — could not be mapped onto Western gay identity without erasing precisely what was most theoretically significant about it. Evelyn Blackwood’s fieldwork among *tomboi* and *femme* women in West Sumatra demonstrated that in the Indonesian formation, only the tomboi bore the weight of gender transgression — the femme partner was understood as a normal woman, her femininity intact, her social position unmarked. Ara Wilson’s analysis of the Thai *tom/dee* system showed how global capitalism reorganised local gender formations without simply Westernising them, producing new configurations that were neither traditional nor modern in any straightforward sense.

The structural finding across these diverse regional formations is consistent and theoretically significant: **across much of the Global South, sexuality is organised around gender role and gender presentation — around the axis of masculine/feminine, active/receptive — rather than around the gender of one’s desired object**. The homo/hetero binary, which the canon treats as the organising structure of modern sexuality, is one regional formation among others — and one that carries specific historical conditions of possibility (the 19th century medicalisation of sexuality in Western Europe, the invention of the homosexual as a type of person rather than a type of act) that did not occur uniformly elsewhere. The closet, too — Sedgwick’s master metaphor for the epistemological structure of modern sexuality — presupposes a subject with an interiority to hide, a disclosure to make, a secular liberal public sphere in which to make it, and a stable identity category to claim once the disclosure is complete. None of these presuppositions holds without significant qualification in the contexts Manalansan, Blackwood, and Wilson describe.

The **third encounter** is the least developed in the existing literature and constitutes the article’s most significant contribution to the field: the encounter between queer theory and Buddhist philosophical frameworks, specifically the Madhyamaka analysis of selfhood developed by Nāgārjuna and the doctrine of *anattā* (non-self) in the Theravāda tradition. This encounter has been gestured toward but not systematically developed. Peter Jackson’s meticulous scholarship on Thai sexual culture showed that the *kathoey* — the Thai gender/sexuality category most often translated as transgender or third gender — was understood through a karmic framework whose political implications were neither straightforwardly homophobic nor straightforwardly queer-affirming: the kathoey was tolerated because the Buddhist karmic framework provided a cosmological explanation for gender crossing (as the residue of past-life transgression) that simultaneously acknowledged its existence and marked it as a form of spiritual deficiency to be worked through rather than an identity to be politically affirmed. José Ignacio Cabezón’s philological scholarship demonstrated that the canonical Buddhist texts say considerably less about same-sex activity than contemporary Buddhist institutions claim, and that much of what presents as traditional Buddhist sexual ethics is in fact a colonial-era import — the Buddhist modernist absorption of Victorian Protestant sexual ethics into Theravāda moral discourse, presented as ancient doctrine. What neither Jackson nor Cabezón fully developed is the theoretical implication that runs beneath their empirical findings: **that the Buddhist philosophical analysis of the self — its processual nature, its emptiness of inherent existence, its constitution through dependent origination rather than through the Lacanian Symbolic Order — provides a fundamentally different ontological ground for thinking gender and sexuality than the psychoanalytic framework that Butler, Sedgwick, and Foucault inherit**.

These three encounters are not sequential corrections to a baseline framework, each adding what the previous left out. They are simultaneous, convergent challenges that together require not a revision of queer theory but a reorientation — a transformation of the ontological ground on which the theory stands. The first encounter shows that the canon’s subject is racially constituted. The second shows that it is culturally specific. The third shows that it is ontologically limited — that the psychoanalytic-phenomenological frameworks from which it draws presuppose a kind of subject (organised around lack, constituted through loss, desiring recognition within a Symbolic Order) whose specific characteristics have been mistaken for the universal conditions of subjecthood as such.

### 1.3 — Governing Question, Stakes, and Article Structure

The governing question this article pursues is therefore: **What happens to the subject of queer theory when these three encounters are taken seriously simultaneously — when the whiteness of the canon’s subject is acknowledged, when the culturally specific nature of the homo/hetero binary is attended to, and when the ontological assumptions of the psychoanalytic tradition are tested against Buddhist philosophical frameworks that do not share them — and what political possibilities does this compound encounter open?**

The stakes of the question are not merely academic. The present moment — in which trans healthcare is being systematically denied across multiple national contexts, in which the legal infrastructure of gender recognition is being dismantled by legislative and judicial action, in which queer and trans people of colour face the compound vulnerabilities of racial capitalism and heteropatriarchal state violence — is precisely the moment that demands theory adequate to the complexity of what is happening. A theory adequate to this moment cannot operate from a framework that was never designed to account for the racialised subject, the Global South formation, or the Buddhist practitioner navigating karmic frameworks and hostile healthcare systems simultaneously. It cannot sustain the melancholic attachment to a political framework whose limits have been demonstrated from multiple directions. And it cannot ignore the question of what it means to act politically — to fight for trans rights, to demand healthcare access, to insist on the grievability of queer lives — from a position that does not reproduce the passionate attachment whose psychic structure Butler has so precisely described, and whose political consequences Berlant has so honestly analysed.

This article argues that the encounter between queer theory, Buddhist ontology, and Global South formations produces a specific set of theoretical resources that are unavailable within any single tradition and that are urgently needed now. It does not argue that the Anglo-American canon should be discarded — Butler’s analysis of subjection and melancholy is indispensable and operates at the conventional level of political reality with full force. It does not argue that Buddhism is inherently queer-affirming — the colonial transformation of Buddhist sexual ethics into institutional homophobia is a historical fact that the article documents carefully. And it does not argue that Global South formations are more authentic than Western ones — the tomboi and the kathoey are as much products of colonial history as the Western gay subject. What it argues is that **the encounter between these traditions discloses something none of them could disclose alone**: a political ontology adequate to the present moment, in which compassionate engagement with the suffering produced by the heterosexual matrix and the trans-exclusionary state is possible without the passionate attachment to outcome that makes Butler’s melancholic subject structurally unable to sustain it.

The article proceeds as follows. Section II establishes the foundational canon — Foucault, Sedgwick, and Butler — with attention to both their analytical power and the specific philosophical commitments that subsequent sections will interrogate. Section III develops the queer of colour critique and Berlant’s theory of the impasse as the first destabilisation of the canon’s assumptions. Sections IV and V develop the Global South encounter — Southeast Asian sexual formations in Section IV, Buddhist textual and institutional frameworks in Section V — tracing both the epistemological friction produced when the canon travels to these contexts and the theoretical resources those contexts generate. Sections VI and VII develop the Buddhist philosophical apparatus — *anattā*, *śūnyatā*, the two-truths framework, and the structural parallel between *upādāna* and passionate attachment — with attention throughout to the distinction between what the texts establish, what is theoretical hypothesis, and what remains genuinely open. Section VIII brings these three encounters into synthesis through the bodhisattva model as a proposed framework for queer political engagement that resolves the tensions the preceding sections have generated. Section IX returns to the governing question and the ethical obligation that underlies it: what does theory owe to the people whose lives it describes?

One methodological commitment requires statement at the outset. This article distinguishes throughout between claims established by primary textual evidence, theoretical hypotheses advanced on the basis of structural analysis, and areas of genuine scholarly uncertainty. This distinction is not a rhetorical hedge — it is an epistemological obligation. A review article that presents as established what is in fact contested, or as contested what is in fact textually documented, does not serve the field it surveys. In a domain as politically charged as this one — where both conservative Buddhist institutions and progressive queer activists make strong claims about what “Buddhism really teaches” about sexuality and gender — the obligation to precision is also a political one.

## Section II — Foundations: The Anglo-American Canonical Triad

### 2.1 — Foucault: Power Produces Subjects

The foundational move of queer theory — the move that makes everything else possible — is Michel Foucault’s demonstration in *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1* (1976) that the subject of sexuality is not a natural entity that power represses but a produced entity that power generates. The repressive hypothesis — the assumption that sexuality is a pre-political natural force that modern Western culture suppressed and that liberation requires releasing — is not, Foucault argues, a description of what happened. It is itself a mechanism of the very power it claims to describe. Modern Western culture did not repress sexuality. It produced an explosion of discourse about it — classifying, cataloguing, and inciting sexuality through medicine, psychiatry, demography, education, and the practice of confession — and in doing so, produced the very subjects whose sexuality was now an object of knowledge and therefore of power.

The most consequential claim of this argument is historical and ontological simultaneously: the homosexual, as a type of person, was invented. “The sodomite,” Foucault writes in a formulation that has structured queer theory ever since, “had been a temporary aberration; the homosexual was now a species.” Before the nineteenth century’s medicalisation of sexuality, sodomy was an act — a sinful act, subject to legal prohibition, but an act that any person might in principle commit. From the 1870s onward, a new figure emerged: a person whose sexuality constituted their nature, whose desire defined their psychology, whose acts were expressions of a pathological type rather than departures from a universal norm. The homosexual was not discovered. The homosexual was produced — through the clinical gaze, through the psychiatric consultation, through the sexological taxonomy that required ever finer distinctions between species of desire.

This production operates through what Foucault calls *biopower* — the distinctly modern form of power that governs not by prohibiting and punishing but by administering life: managing populations, normalising bodies, optimising the biological and productive capacities of the species. Sexuality is one of the primary sites at which biopower operates, because sexuality connects the discipline of individual bodies to the regulation of populations. The heterosexual family, the reproductive couple, the hygienic bourgeois body — these are not merely cultural ideals. They are the products of a specific historical formation in which power operates through the production of norms that subjects are incited to embody, monitor in themselves, confess their deviations from, and police in others.

The productive implication for queer theory is twofold. First, if the homosexual is a produced category rather than a natural kind, then gay liberation cannot be the liberation of a pre-existing subject from the power that repressed it — there is no pre-existing subject to liberate. Liberation, if it is possible, must take a different form: not the release of a natural sexuality from artificial constraint, but the genealogical exposure of the historical conditions that produced both the category of the homosexual and the desire to be liberated from it. Second, if power produces subjects, then subjects cannot simply stand outside power and critique it from a position of uninvested rationality. They are constituted by and through the power they might resist — which means that resistance must work differently than the liberation narrative assumes.

The specific limit this framework carries into the article’s subsequent encounters is geographical and historical. Foucault’s genealogy is a genealogy of Western European — and specifically North Atlantic — modernity. The biopolitical apparatus he describes, the confessional technology he traces, the medicalisation of sexuality he documents — these are historically specific formations. The colonial dimensions of that modernity, and the specific ways in which colonial power organised sexuality and gender in the territories it administered, remain largely outside Foucault’s frame. When the canon Foucault founds travels to Southeast Asia, South Asia, or the Muslim world, it arrives in contexts where the medicalisation of sexuality occurred under colonial rather than metropolitan conditions, where the production of sexual categories was entangled with the production of racial and religious categories in ways Foucault’s apparatus was not designed to track, and where existing formations of gender and desire were not simply repressed by colonial power but transformed, layered, and partially replaced in processes whose results cannot be read off from the metropolitan history.

### 2.2 — Sedgwick: The Closet as Epistemological Structure

Where Foucault provides the genealogy of how sexuality became a domain — how the homosexual came to be produced as a type of person — Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick’s *Epistemology of the Closet* (1990) provides the structural analysis of how that production organises the whole of modern Western culture. Sedgwick’s central claim is that the homo/heterosexual binary is not simply the private concern of gay and lesbian people but “the defining structure for gay oppression in this century” and simultaneously a structure that “has the potential to disrupt many other formations of difference.” The study of homosexuality is not a minority interest. It is an investigation of the epistemological foundations of modernity.

The closet, in Sedgwick’s analysis, is not primarily a hiding place — it is a **knowledge structure**. It organises the relationship between the known and the unknown, the said and the unsaid, the public and the private, across the whole range of modern cultural production. The person who is “in the closet” is not simply concealing a fact about themselves. They are positioned within a specific epistemological relationship — to themselves, to others who may or may not know, to the public norms of disclosure — that organises their social existence in ways that cannot be resolved by disclosure. “Coming out,” Sedgwick observes, “is never a once-and-for-all event but a series of new closets.” Every new context, every new relationship, every new institution requires the renegotiation of disclosure on terms that the closet structure itself sets. The epistemology of the closet is not a problem to be solved. It is a structure to be inhabited, navigated, and, at most, partially and locally disrupted.

The intellectual ambition of Sedgwick’s project is the claim that this structure ramifies far beyond its immediate domain. She argues that “an understanding of virtually any aspect of modern Western culture must be, not merely incomplete, but damaged in its central substance to the extent that it does not incorporate a critical analysis of modern homo/heterosexual definition.” This is the **universalising** reading of homosexuality’s cultural significance — as opposed to the **minoritising** reading, which understands homosexuality as the specific concern of a minority of people defined by it. The universalising reading, which Sedgwick endorses as the more analytically productive, holds that the homo/hetero binary structures the experience of all subjects within the cultures it organises, not merely those who identify as gay or lesbian. To analyse the closet is to analyse a fundamental feature of modern Western epistemology.

What this framework provides for the article’s governing question is the analysis of how knowledge and ignorance, disclosure and concealment, function as political structures rather than merely personal predicaments. The closet is not simply a burden that individual gay people carry — it is a systematically produced ignorance that serves specific political functions, that is maintained by specific social practices, and that organises the distribution of power between those who can afford not to know and those who pay the cost of their not-knowing. This analysis will be essential when the article turns to the kathoey in Thailand or the bakla in the Philippines — not because the closet applies to those formations, but precisely because examining where it does not apply reveals with new clarity what specific historical conditions it presupposes and what political structures it reflects.

Those conditions are what this article’s second and third encounters will disclose. Sedgwick’s framework was built from Anglo-American literary texts — Henry James, Marcel Proust, Herman Melville — and from the specific socio-political organisation of sexuality in the late twentieth-century United States. The interiority it presupposes, the disclosure narrative it organises, the secular liberal public sphere in which coming out makes sense as a political act — these are not universal features of human social organisation. They are the products of a specific cultural formation. When the canon travels to contexts where the closet’s presuppositions do not hold — where there is no identity category to claim, no liberal rights framework to claim it within, and no prior understanding of sexuality as a domain of psychic depth whose disclosure is a condition of political subjecthood — what the canon illuminates by its failure to apply is as theoretically significant as what it illuminates in its proper domain.

### 2.3 — Butler: Performativity, the Heterosexual Matrix, and the Melancholic Subject

Judith Butler’s *Gender Trouble* (1990) and *Bodies That Matter* (1993) complete the foundational triad by turning Foucault’s analysis of power and Sedgwick’s analysis of the homo/hetero binary toward **gender itself** — demonstrating that not only sexuality but the binary gender structure that grounds it is a produced, contingent, historically constituted formation that has been mistaken for natural.

The theory of **gender performativity** is the framework’s most influential and most misunderstood contribution. It does not claim that gender is a performance — a role consciously adopted, a costume worn and potentially discarded. It claims that gender is **performative**: that it is constituted through the repeated, regulated, and citational practices that appear to express it. There is no prior gender that performance expresses — the performance is all the way down. “In imitating gender,” Butler writes in *Gender Trouble*, “drag implicitly reveals the imitative structure of gender itself — as well as its contingency.” The drag queen does not merely imitate femininity. She reveals, through the explicitness of her imitation, that the femininity practised by women who do not understand themselves as imitating is equally citational, equally a matter of repeating a norm whose authority derives not from a natural original it expresses but from the regulatory force of the repeated citation itself.

The political structure within which this performance operates is what Butler calls the **heterosexual matrix**: the grid through which bodies, genders, and desires are rendered coherent within the dominant cultural framework. To be legible as a person — to be recognisable, to occupy a subject position that other subjects can relate to — requires the alignment of body (*female/male*), gender (*feminine/masculine*), and desire (*toward the opposite sex*). Any deviation from this alignment produces what Butler, following Kristeva, calls **abjection** — the condition of being expelled from the domain of the recognisably human, designated as the constitutive outside of the normative subject. The abject is not simply marginalised. It is necessary — the normative subject requires its abjection to constitute itself as normative. Queerness is not the simple opposite of heteronormativity. It is the constitutive outside that heteronormativity requires in order to define its inside.

In *The Psychic Life of Power* (1997), Butler provides the psychic mechanism through which this structure is maintained: **passionate attachment** to the norms that constitute the subject, an attachment produced through the melancholic incorporation of foreclosed desires. As developed in Section I, this is the account that most directly conditions the article’s central question. The melancholic subject cannot simply refuse the heterosexual matrix because the matrix is the condition of its existence as a subject with psychic depth, social recognition, and the capacity to desire at all. Resistance must take the form of subversive repetition — citational practice that reproduces the norm while introducing deviations that gradually, structurally, shift what the norm can sustain.

This is queer theory’s most sophisticated account of both the difficulty and the possibility of political transformation. It explains the psychic investment in normative gender identity; it grounds the violence of homophobic and transphobic reaction in a structural logic rather than mere prejudice; and it provides a model of resistance that is neither voluntarist (simply choose to deviate) nor deterministic (the subject is fully captured by the norm). Its power is considerable and its political purchase, within the contexts for which it was developed, remains largely intact.

The limit this framework carries into the article’s subsequent encounters is ontological. Butler’s subject is constituted through the Lacanian Symbolic Order — through language, signification, and the specific psychic structure of a subject organised around lack, loss, and the desire for recognition. This is a subject with a specific philosophical architecture. It presupposes that the deepest level of the subject’s formation is through internalisation of social norms — that what is most intimate, most psychically fundamental, most constitutive of the subject’s sense of itself, is the product of an encounter with symbolic power. The Buddhist analysis of selfhood, which the article develops in Sections VI and VII, operates from a different ontological premise: the subject’s apparent depth and interiority are not the product of the internalisation of the Symbolic Order but a conventionally real but ultimately empty construction arising through dependent origination. This is not a trivial difference. It changes what we think we are doing when we pursue queer liberation, what we think we are liberating, and what we think liberation from the conditions of subjection could possibly mean.

That question — what liberation could mean for a subject that is ultimately empty of the inherent existence that makes passionate attachment feel like the only alternative to annihilation — is what Sections VI and VII will address. The canon’s power is established here. Its limit is marked. The encounters that follow are not its refutation but its reorientation.

## Section III — The Second Generation: Race, Empire, and the Limits of the Canon

### 3.1 — Muñoz and the Raced Horizon of Futurity

The most consequential internal critique of the Anglo-American canonical triad came not from outside the field but from scholars working within it who found that the framework’s universalising gestures repeatedly produced a specific, unmarked subject — a subject whose whiteness was invisible precisely because it was foundational. The critique did not arrive as a single polemical intervention but as a set of convergent demonstrations, each working through a specific theoretical problem to show that the canon’s blind spots were not peripheral oversights but structural features of its central arguments.

José Esteban Muñoz’s *Disidentifications: Queers of Color and the Performance of Politics* (1999) began this work at the level of political strategy. Where Butler’s theory of subversive repetition described a mode of resistance available to subjects who were interpellated by the dominant culture’s norms — who were, in some sense, addressed by those norms even if injuriously — Muñoz’s subjects occupied a more complex position. For the queer of colour performer, the dominant culture’s norms were not simply injurious citations to be repeated with a difference. They were doubly alien: the heteronormative culture offered no legible subject position, and the predominantly white gay and lesbian culture offered a position that required the erasure of racial and class formation as the price of admission. Neither assimilation nor pure counter-identification was available. What Muñoz described instead — drawing on the work of Michel Pêcheux on ideology and identification — was **disidentification**: a third mode of engaging dominant cultural scripts that neither accepts them nor simply refuses them but works *on and against* them simultaneously, recycling and rerouting their symbolic resources for uses they were not designed to serve.

The performers Muñoz analysed — drag queen Vaginal Davis, Cuban-American artist Ana Mendieta, underground filmmaker Jack Smith — were not simply subverting norms in Butler’s sense. They were doing something more specific: taking the dominant culture’s images, narratives, and symbols and making something survivable from them, something that could sustain political and affective life in the gap between the subject positions the dominant culture offered and the subject positions that queer of colour life actually required. This was Butler’s performativity raced, classed, and located in actual cultural practice rather than in the structural logic of citational chains.

But it was in *Cruising Utopia: The Then and There of Queer Futurity* (2009) that Muñoz made his most politically significant intervention in the canonical debates — a direct argument against Lee Edelman’s *No Future* (2004) that simultaneously addressed the racial epistemology of anti-futurity as a theoretical position. Edelman’s argument — that queerness should embrace the anti-social, anti-reproductive force attributed to it by the dominant culture, that the death drive was the authentic political stance for a queerness that refused the terms of reproductive futurism — was, Muñoz argued, a politics available only from a specific subject position. “No future,” as a theoretical stance, presupposed a subject for whom the present was liveable enough to reject, for whom the available political positions included the aestheticisation of negativity. For queers of colour, trans women of colour, queer migrants navigating hostile healthcare and asylum systems, the present was frequently not survivable. The future was not a normative trap. It was a material necessity.

> “Queerness is not yet here. Queerness is an ideality. Put another way, we are not yet queer. We may never touch queerness, but we can feel it as the warm illumination of a horizon imbued with potentiality.”[^1]

This is Muñoz’s counter-proposition: queerness not as an anti-social refusal of futurity but as an **unrealised horizon** — a “not-yet” that is neither the normative future of reproductive futurism nor the Edelmanian embrace of the death drive but a third temporal orientation, drawing on Ernst Bloch’s concept of concrete utopia to describe the traces of a different world latent in present cultural practice. The queer ephemeral archive — the ballroom scene, the voguing competition, the underground performance — carries utopian content precisely because it rehearses social arrangements that do not yet exist anywhere in fully realised form, that are suppressed or erased by the dominant culture, but that persist in the ephemeral practices of communities for whom the official archive has no space.

The racial epistemology here is not incidental to the theoretical argument — it is its ground. Anti-futurity can only appear as radical when the theorist’s present is already comfortable enough to permit the gesture. When the present is organised by premature death, chronic dispossession, and the systematic denial of healthcare and legal recognition, the refusal of futurity is not a political position. It is a description of existing conditions. Muñoz’s insistence that the future is queerness’s domain was simultaneously a theoretical argument about temporality and a political argument about whose experience gets to anchor the field’s most foundational assumptions.

### 3.2 — Puar, Snorton, Haritaworn: Homonationalism, Fungibility, Necropolitics

Where Muñoz’s critique operated through the question of futurity, the subsequent generation pushed further into the racialised structures of the present — asking not only whose future was being imagined but whose present was being sustained, whose death was being managed, and what the canon’s implicit whiteness had made structurally invisible.

Jasbir Puar’s *Terrorist Assemblages: Homonationalism in Queer Times* (2007) identified a specific historical development that queer theory had almost entirely failed to theorise: **the state’s use of gay rights as imperial currency**. In the years following September 11, 2001, Western governments — and specifically the United States and Israel — developed a practice of citing their domestic LGBT rights frameworks as evidence of civilisational superiority, a marker of liberal modernity to be contrasted with the presumed barbarism of Muslim-majority societies. This was not liberation. It was the **weaponisation of homosexuality**: the gay body conscripted into the service of racism and imperialism, the cause of LGBT rights deployed to legitimate military occupation, drone warfare, and the racialised surveillance of Muslim communities both at home and abroad.

The homonational subject — the assimilated, normatively coupled, middle-class, predominantly white gay or lesbian person whose queerness had been domesticated enough to serve nationalist purposes — was the product of this formation. Their inclusion in the national fantasy was purchased at the price of the disposability of those deemed unassimilable: racialised queers, trans women of colour, Muslim queers, queer migrants and asylum seekers, sex workers, and the poor. The heterosexual matrix’s constitutive outside was now being managed not through simple exclusion but through a more complex operation: the inclusion of some queers and the reinforced exclusion of others, with the included functioning as evidence of liberal progress and the excluded functioning as the sacrificial ground on which that evidence was produced.

Puar’s theoretical apparatus for analysing this development drew on Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari’s concept of the **assemblage** rather than on identity categories. Where intersectionality — the framework developed by Kimberlé Crenshaw to analyse the compounded effects of race, gender, and class — described relatively stable identity categories that compounded in determinate ways, assemblage theory described shifting configurations of bodies, affects, technologies, discourses, and power relations that produced effects no stable identity category could capture. The same body could be simultaneously protected (as a gay citizen of the United States), targeted (as racially Muslim-coded), and commodified (as a consumer in the pink economy) — not because three different systems of power were independently operating on it but because a single complex assemblage was producing all three effects simultaneously.

Puar’s *The Right to Maim: Debility, Capacity, Disability* (2017) extended this analysis through the concept of **debilitation** — a mode of power distinct from both Foucault’s biopower (making live) and Achille Mbembe’s necropolitics (making die). Debilitation is the deliberate impairing of life below the threshold of death: rendering populations non-functional, dependent, economically useless, and surveyable without killing them outright. It is, Puar argues, not a failure or excess of biopolitical power but one of its primary operations — producing the disabled, the chronically ill, the traumatised, and the economically debilitated as populations that generate specific kinds of value (pharmaceutical markets, care industries, debt regimes) while remaining contained and controllable. The concept connects the analysis of Israeli policy toward Palestinians to a broader analysis of how colonial and neoliberal power produces debility as a feature rather than a bug.

C. Riley Snorton’s *Black on Both Sides: A Racial History of Trans Identity* (2017) approached the canon’s limit from a different direction: the history of medicine and the archive. Where Puar analysed the present racialised structure of queer inclusion and exclusion, Snorton asked what historical conditions had produced the categories through which trans identity was now understood — and found that the answer required attending to the systematic erasure of Black bodies from the history that produced those categories. The concept he developed — **fungibility** — described the treatment of Black bodies under slavery as interchangeable, available for use, exploitable without remainder: a property of the enslaved body that enabled its use as material for medical experimentation, surgical development, and pharmaceutical innovation without acknowledgment, credit, or protection.

Snorton’s argument was that trans medical history — and specifically the procedures that enabled high-profile mid-twentieth century transitions — was built on this fungibility. The surgical and hormonal techniques that made Christine Jorgensen’s celebrated 1952 transition possible had been developed through procedures performed on Black bodies, enslaved and otherwise, whose contribution to the medical knowledge that enabled those techniques was systematically erased from the histories that celebrated them. This was not merely a historical injustice to be acknowledged and moved past. It was a structural feature of how trans identity had been produced as a category — as a possibility available to some bodies and not others, with the bodies on whom the enabling experiments were performed excluded from the category their suffering had made possible.

Jin Haritaworn’s *Queer Necropolitics* (co-edited with Adi Kuntsman and Silvia Posocco, 2014) brought these threads together through the theoretical framework of necropolitics — Achille Mbembe’s extension of Foucault’s biopower to account for the sovereign power to decide who lives and who dies, a power that Mbembe argued was always already racial, always already colonial in its operation. Applied to queer life, the framework produced the concept of **queer necropolitics**: the differential distribution of queer life and queer death along racial, colonial, and class lines, the process by which the inclusion of some queers in the national body politic is achieved through the reinforced disposability of others.

The argument’s sharpest edge was its implication for queer politics itself. Hate crime legislation, passed in the name of LGBT rights, increased the policing and incarceration of racialised communities. Anti-trafficking legislation, framed as protection for vulnerable women and queer people, criminalised sex work and the survival strategies of the most economically marginalised queer populations. The demand for legal recognition of same-sex relationships required presenting gay and lesbian couples as domestically normative, economically productive, and reproductively oriented — a presentation that excluded and stigmatised the non-normative formations that sustained queer life at the margins. Queer politics had become, in specific and traceable ways, a vector of the necropolitical power it claimed to resist. This was not a simple betrayal. It was the structural consequence of pursuing inclusion in a normative order without challenging the normative order’s constitutive exclusions.

Together, Puar, Snorton, and Haritaworn demonstrated that the canon’s subject was not merely implicitly white but **constituted by the very racialised exclusions it failed to theorise**. The heterosexual matrix’s constitutive outside was not only gendered and sexualised — it was racialised, colonialised, and produced through the specific forms of debilitation and death that the canon had treated as peripheral to its central concerns. Any queer theory that did not account for homonationalism, fungibility, and queer necropolitics was not merely incomplete — it was, as Ferguson had argued in *Aberrations in Black* (2004), actively reproducing the racial capitalism whose operations it claimed to contest.

### 3.3 — Berlant: The Impasse and Lateral Agency

Lauren Berlant’s *Cruel Optimism* (2011) brought a different critical pressure to bear on the canon’s framework — not through the analysis of racial structure but through the phenomenology of **ordinary political life under neoliberalism**, and specifically through the temporal structure of a present that had become stuck. Where Muñoz analysed futurity and its racial epistemology, Berlant analysed the present itself — or rather, the specific quality of presentness produced when the normative narrative of the future (the good life, upward mobility, romantic fulfilment, political representation) has structurally broken down but cannot be relinquished without losing the very framework of expectation that makes the present legible as a present moving toward something.

A relation of cruel optimism exists, Berlant argues, when something you desire is actually an obstacle to your flourishing. The cruelty is structural rather than intentional: the good life promise — the normative package of upward mobility, romantic love, bodily health, and political participation that post-war Western societies offered as the horizon of ordinary aspiration — had been systematically undermined by the neoliberal restructuring of labour markets, housing, healthcare, and political representation. The promise no longer delivered what it promised. But attachment to the promise persisted, because the promise was not merely a desired outcome — it was the **structure of desiring itself**, the framework within which the subject’s investment in the future was organised. To relinquish the promise was not simply to accept a disappointment. It was to lose the orientation toward the future that made the present legible and the self coherent.

The temporal structure Berlant calls the **impasse** — “a holding station that doesn’t hold but opens to a destabilised and unresolved time” — is the condition produced when cruel optimism’s dynamic has worn out both the promise and the capacity to relinquish it. The impasse is not a crisis with a resolution approaching. It is the normalisation of crisis: the permanent present in which emergency is no longer exceptional but the baseline condition of large portions of the population. Navigating the impasse requires not the dramatic political gestures of either Edelman’s death drive or Muñoz’s utopian horizon but what Berlant calls **lateral agency** — the ordinary human capacity to manage, to improvise, to find small pleasures and small sustainments within conditions that are not changing, to move sideways through a present that will not resolve itself upward or forward.

The political and ethical implications of this framework are demanding. Lateral agency is not resignation, but it is not heroism either. It is what most people actually do — and taking it seriously as a form of life, as the actual practice of most subjects in the impasse, requires abandoning the frameworks of political theory that address an idealised subject capable of sovereign choice, rational deliberation, and strategic action. It requires, as Berlant puts it, attending to “how people navigate their way into the future in whatever time-space they have available to them, given the constraints under which they work.”

What this framework provides for the article’s governing question is a bridge — both political and theoretical — between the queer of colour critique developed in Sections 3.1 and 3.2 and the Buddhist philosophical analysis that Sections VI and VII will develop. The impasse, read through the Buddhist framework, is the structure of *dukkha* — the pervasive unsatisfactoriness inherent in all conditioned phenomena, including the good life promise. Lateral agency is the conventional navigation of *upādāna* (clinging) without yet seeing through it: the subject managing its attachments without recognising their ultimately conditioned and therefore potentially dissolvable nature. Berlant’s analysis is the most politically honest account of ordinary queer life available in the canon — more honest than Edelman’s aestheticisation of negativity, more honest than Muñoz’s sustained orientation toward a horizon that keeps receding. Its honesty is also its limit: it describes the impasse with extraordinary precision but does not — cannot, within its theoretical framework — provide a way through it that does not simply reproduce the lateral management of conditions that are not changing.

That the Buddhist framework might provide what Berlant cannot — not a solution to the impasse, since no conventional outcome can resolve the ultimate problem, but a **different relationship to the impasse**, one that does not require either the cruel optimism of attachment to the good life promise or the lateral management of its perpetual non-arrival — is one of the central theoretical proposals that Sections VI and VII will develop. The impasse, from the Madhyamaka perspective, is what samsara looks like when you are inside it. The path through it is not a better promise. It is the recognition, progressive and partial and always incomplete, that the subject whose good life was at stake was never the ultimately real entity that passionate attachment treated it as being. That recognition changes nothing about the conventional political struggle — and it changes everything about the psychic relationship to its outcomes.

## Section IV — Global South Formations I: Southeast Asian Sexualities and the Failure of the Binary

### 4.1 — The Bakla, Tomboi, and Tom: Gender Role Over Object Choice

The homo/heterosexual binary — the organising principle of the Anglo-American canonical triad, the axis around which the closet structures modern Western culture, the grid through which the heterosexual matrix renders bodies and desires intelligible or abject — is not a universal feature of human sexual organisation. It is a historically specific formation, produced through the medicalisation of sexuality in nineteenth-century Western Europe, and it travels badly. This is not merely a sociological observation about cultural variation. It is a theoretical claim with structural consequences: if the binary does not organise sexuality in many of the world’s social formations, then a queer theory built on its analysis cannot claim the universality its foundational texts implicitly assert.

The Southeast Asian scholarship has demonstrated this with particular precision, because the regional formations it analyses are not simply different from the Western model in their surface features while sharing its deep structure. They are organised around a **different axis entirely** — one that the Western framework consistently misreads when it encounters it.

Martin Manalansan IV’s *Global Divas: Filipino Gay Men in the Diaspora* (2003) is the founding text of this demonstration in English-language scholarship. Manalansan’s ethnographic work with Filipino gay men in New York City’s Jackson Heights neighbourhood is organised around the figure of the **bakla** — a gender/sexuality category that has been translated into English as “gay man,” “homosexual,” “transvestite,” and “effeminate man” at different historical moments, and that is misrepresented by each of these translations. The bakla is not defined by the gender of their desired objects — by same-sex desire in the Western sense — but by **gender crossing**: the possession of a *kaluluwa* (soul) understood as feminine, residing in a body assigned male at birth, producing a specific set of social roles, aesthetic practices, and forms of relation that constitute a recognised, if ambivalently positioned, social formation within Filipino culture.

This distinction — between sexuality organised around the gender of the desired object and sexuality organised around the gender of the desiring subject — is the structural key to understanding why the Western framework consistently misreads Southeast Asian formations. In the Western homo/hetero binary, what defines the homosexual is the gender of their partner: a man who desires men is gay regardless of how he presents, how he identifies, or what role he occupies in sexual practice. In the bakla formation, what defines the subject’s position is their own gender — their femininity, their crossing, their occupation of a social role that is neither normatively male nor the feminine assigned to women but a third position with specific cultural content. The gender of the bakla’s partners is, in the local framework, largely irrelevant to their definition as bakla. A masculine man who is the bakla’s sexual partner is typically not understood as gay, queer, or gender-transgressive — he is simply a man.

Manalansan’s most theoretically significant contribution is not the description of the bakla formation itself — earlier scholars had noted its existence — but his analysis of what happens when the bakla migrates. In the diaspora, Filipino gay men navigate between two identity systems: the bakla formation from their place of origin, which they carry in their bodies, their femininity, their performative practices, and their social relations; and the Western gay identity system of their host country, which demands legibility in its own terms — coming out, pride, rights-claiming, homonormative coupledom — and which reads the bakla’s femininity as either drag performance or gender dysphoria rather than as the expression of a coherent alternative formation. The result is not assimilation or resistance but what Muñoz would recognise as **disidentification**: working on and against both systems simultaneously, finding ways to sustain bakla social life within a context that does not recognise its terms, producing hybrid formations that belong fully to neither.

**Swardspeak** — the vernacular language used by Filipino gay men that mixes Filipino, English, Spanish, and invented coinages in an elaborate and shifting lexicon — is one of the primary vehicles of this disidentification. Manalansan reads swardspeak not merely as a secret code or a form of in-group bonding but as a **queer archive**: a repository of collective memory, social knowledge, and forms of desire that official culture does not preserve, that cannot be reduced to the representational categories of either Filipino nationalist or Western gay identity discourse, and that constitutes a form of theoretical production in its own right. The swardspeak lexicon is a theory of desire — a system of categories, distinctions, and evaluations that organises the social world of bakla life with a precision and specificity that no imported framework could match. That it does not look like theory in the academic sense — that it is vernacular, performative, always in flux, embedded in social practice rather than written in scholarly prose — is precisely the methodological challenge this article addresses in Section 4.3.

Evelyn Blackwood’s fieldwork in West Sumatra, documented most fully in *Falling into the Lesbi World: Desire and Difference in Indonesia* (2010), reveals a parallel but structurally distinct formation. The **tomboi** — Indonesian women who adopt masculine gender presentation, take on masculine social roles, and are attracted to feminine women — and their **femme** partners (*cewek*) constitute a same-sex relational formation that is, again, organised around gender role rather than object choice. But the Indonesian formation is asymmetrical in a way that the bakla formation is not: the tomboi is the transgressive figure, the one whose gender crossing constitutes a deviation from norms, while the femme partner is typically not understood as lesbian, queer, or gender-deviant in the local framework. She is a normal woman who is in a relationship with a tomboi — her femininity is intact, her social position is unmarked, and her departure from heteronormativity is understood as a temporary or situational arrangement rather than an identity.

This asymmetry — in which the masculine partner bears the full weight of gender transgression while the feminine partner is exempted from it — is not a quirk of Indonesian culture but a structural feature of formations organised around gender role. Where the Western lesbian formation typically understands both partners as equally lesbian, equally defined by the gender of their desired objects, the tomboi/femme formation distributes transgression according to gender presentation: to present as masculine in a female body is transgressive; to present as feminine, and to be desired by a masculine partner, is not. The political implications are significant. A rights framework premised on the mutual identification of both partners as gay or lesbian will misrepresent this formation, will speak on behalf of populations whose self-understanding it distorts, and will produce legal and political strategies that serve some members of the formation while alienating others.

Blackwood also traces the historical sedimentation that produced the Indonesian formation in its current shape — specifically, the colonial layering that her fieldwork makes visible. The Dutch colonial administration introduced Western sexological categories into the Netherlands East Indies through medical education and colonial law. The term *lesbi* — clearly derived from the English “lesbian” — was introduced into Indonesian vernacular through this colonial mediation and has been subsequently transformed by the circulation of global media, NGO frameworks, and international LGBT rights discourse. What presents as the local formation is therefore always already layered — precolonial Minangkabau gender frameworks (in which matrilineal social organisation produced specific possibilities for female authority and gender flexibility), Dutch colonial sexology, postcolonial Indonesian nationalism’s heteronormative constructions of proper femininity and masculinity, and the global LGBT rights apparatus are all present simultaneously in the tomboi’s daily navigation of her social world. To read the tomboi through any single framework is to read only one layer of a deposit whose full depth requires attention to all of them.

Ara Wilson’s *The Intimate Economies of Bangkok: Tomboys, Tycoons, and Avon Ladies in the Global South* (2004) extends this analysis to Thailand’s **tom/dee** formation — structurally similar to the Indonesian tomboi/femme in its organisation around gender role rather than object choice — and adds a political-economic dimension that neither Manalansan nor Blackwood foregrounds as centrally. Wilson traces how the tom figure is not only a sexual and gender formation but a **market formation** — a specific intersection of gender presentation, consumer practice, labour market position, and urban mobility that global capitalism has organised in specific ways. The tom is associated with certain occupational categories, consumer goods, and spatial practices (the city, the market, the public sphere of non-domestic labour) that connect gender transgression to economic and geographic mobility in ways that cannot be read off from either the cultural analysis or the sexual politics literature alone.

Wilson’s critical contribution to the theoretical debate is her challenge to what she calls the **globalisation thesis** — the argument, associated most prominently with Dennis Altman, that globalisation is producing a convergence toward Western gay identity categories as the modern form of sexual self-understanding, with local formations either disappearing or being assimilated into the global gay standard. Wilson demonstrates, through her fieldwork, that this narrative dramatically underestimates both the persistence of local formations and the complexity of how they engage with globalised identity categories. The tom does not become a Western gay woman. She becomes something new — a formation that has absorbed certain elements of the globalised gay repertoire while maintaining the structural organisation around gender role rather than object choice that is the formation’s constitutive principle. The result is a hybridity that is neither traditional nor modern in any simple sense but specifically contemporary: a product of the entanglement of local formations with transnational capital, media, and rights discourse that generates possibilities and constraints the globalisation thesis cannot account for.

### 4.2 — Colonial Sedimentation and the Production of Identity

The structural finding that emerges from reading Manalansan, Blackwood, and Wilson together is not simply that Southeast Asian formations differ from Western ones. It is that **they are constituted through the same colonial processes that produced Western gay identity** — but produced differently, layered differently, and generating different formations because the colonial encounter took place in different local contexts with different precolonial gender frameworks already in place.

This is what the concept of **colonial sedimentation** — borrowed here from historical linguistics and applied to sexuality — attempts to capture. The sexual formations of contemporary Southeast Asia are not survivals of a precolonial past, nor are they simple imports from the colonial or postcolonial metropolitan centre. They are **layered deposits**: precolonial categories that organised gender and desire in ways specific to particular social formations; colonial impositions that introduced new taxonomies, legal regimes, and medical frameworks; postcolonial nationalisms that constructed heteronormative models of the proper citizen-subject; and global capitalisms that introduced new consumer practices, media representations, and rights frameworks. Each layer is present in the contemporary formation — not as distinct strata that can be clearly separated but as interpenetrating residues whose effects are simultaneous and mutually conditioning.

The genealogy of the Indonesian term *lesbi* illustrates the sedimentation process with particular clarity. Before the Dutch colonial period, the Minangkabau social formation — matrilineal, organised around female inheritance of property and authority — produced specific possibilities for female gender flexibility and same-sex intimacy that were not understood through the category of homosexuality. Dutch colonial sexology, introduced through medical education and colonial law, layered the Western taxonomy onto these formations. The English-derived *lesbi* circulated through Indonesian national media in the mid-twentieth century, producing a new category that carried both colonial and nationalist inflections. The global HIV/AIDS public health apparatus introduced the category of MSM (men who have sex with men) as a specifically non-identity-based alternative to gay identity — itself a recognition of the inadequacy of Western identity categories for the populations at risk — and thereby produced a further layer. The NGO-isation of LGBT rights in the early twenty-first century introduced the full international LGBT acronym, with its implied identity model, as the framework within which political advocacy was expected to take place.

The tomboi navigating contemporary West Sumatra inhabits all of these layers simultaneously. When she presents herself to a health clinic, she may be interpellated as MSM (if the clinic uses that framework), as *lesbi* (if the clinic uses Indonesian vernacular), or as transgender (if the clinic has been trained through international NGO frameworks that assimilate tomboi to a trans identity). Each interpellation distorts her formation in specific ways: MSM erases her gender crossing; *lesbi* assimilates her to a Western identity model organised around object choice; transgender imports a medical-psychological framework that locates gender non-conformity in a pathological interiority rather than in a social and relational formation. None of these frameworks was designed for her. All of them are available to her, and she may use different ones strategically in different contexts.

This strategic deployment of multiple, mutually inadequate frameworks is precisely what Massad’s Gay International critique — contentious and contested as it remains — was designed to illuminate. Joseph Massad’s *Desiring Arabs* (2007) argued that international LGBT rights organisations were not simply extending rights to populations that already existed in the form those organisations assumed. They were producing new identity formations — homosexuals, gays, lesbians — where local formations had organised same-sex desire and gender crossing around different principles, and in doing so were simultaneously enabling new forms of political organising and foreclosing the specific forms of resistance and survival that local formations had developed. The controversy Massad’s argument generated — and the critique from Arab queer activists who argued that his theoretical account ignored the persecution they actually faced — maps directly onto the tension between decolonial theoretical critique and the immediate needs of communities facing violence. That tension is not resolvable from within the theoretical apparatus alone. It is a political question that theory can clarify but not answer.

What the colonial sedimentation framework clarifies is that neither the globalisation thesis (local formations are being replaced by Western gay identity) nor the cultural authenticity thesis (local formations are pristine survivals to be protected from Western contamination) adequately describes the process. What actually occurs is more complex and more theoretically productive: **encounter, layering, transformation, and hybrid formation** — a process in which local and imported frameworks mutually condition each other, producing formations that are irreducibly contemporary and that require analytical tools adequate to their layered complexity.

### 4.3 — Vernacular Theory and the Limits of the Academy

The scholarship reviewed in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 operates within a specific methodological tension that is worth naming explicitly, because naming it is itself a contribution to the field. The scholarship is academic — produced in universities, published in peer-reviewed venues, addressed to readers with the theoretical formation to engage its conceptual apparatus. And yet the formations it analyses are, in important respects, **self-theorising**: they produce their own understandings of gender, desire, and social life that do not wait for academic analysis to become coherent, that circulate in vernacular rather than scholarly register, and that carry theoretical content the academy has been slow to recognise as theory.

Swardspeak is the clearest example. Manalansan’s reading of the swardspeak lexicon as a queer archive extends Muñoz’s concept of the ephemeral archive into a specifically Southeast Asian and diasporic register — but the theoretical content of swardspeak does not depend on Muñoz’s framework to be real. The distinctions swardspeak makes between types of desire, forms of relation, modes of gender presentation, and gradations of social status constitute a **working theory of bakla social life** — a system of categories that has been developed, refined, contested, and transmitted across generations of practitioners, and that enables them to navigate a social world that no imported framework maps accurately. That this theory is encoded in a vernacular language rather than in scholarly prose, that it is performed and embodied rather than written and archived, that it is always in flux rather than stabilised in a canonical text — none of these features make it less theoretical. They make it differently theoretical, and the academy’s persistent failure to recognise vernacular knowledge production as theory is a methodological limitation that reproduces the very hierarchies queer theory claims to contest.

The tomboi networks of West Sumatra constitute a parallel case. Blackwood’s ethnographic accounts describe communities that have developed, over decades, practical knowledge about how to navigate gender transgression within Minangkabau matrilineal social structures, Islamic gender norms, Indonesian state heteronormativity, and the pressures of global capitalism — knowledge about which contexts permit which forms of presentation, which relationships can be acknowledged and which must be managed with discretion, which forms of male authority can be negotiated and which must be avoided, how to sustain tomboi community life under conditions that are neither fully permissive nor fully hostile. This navigational knowledge is not merely practical. It is a theory of power — of how gender norms operate, where they are enforced and where they are flexible, what forms of agency are available within constraints that cannot be simply refused. It is produced through practice, transmitted through community, and tested against the concrete conditions of social life in ways that academic theory rarely achieves.

The kathoey’s navigation of Thailand’s karmic framework presents perhaps the most theoretically rich case. As Peter Jackson’s work documents, the karmic explanation of gender crossing — as the consequence of past-life transgression, as a condition to be worked through rather than an identity to be affirmed — is not merely an ideological imposition that karmic subjects passively internalise. It is a **working framework** that generates specific forms of social knowledge, specific strategies for managing public and private presentation, specific modes of relating to both the Buddhist institutional structures that partially include and partially exclude the kathoey and the commercial and tourist economies that provide specific livelihood possibilities. That the karmic framework marks gender crossing as spiritually deficient — that its tolerance is not the same as affirmation — does not mean that the practitioners working within it have no theoretical understanding of their situation. They have an unusually precise one, calibrated to the specific conditions of Thai social life in ways that neither the Western gay rights framework nor the Buddhist modernist framework of institutional Buddhism can match.

What the academy must do, if it is to take vernacular theory seriously as theory, is develop **methodological apparatus adequate to its recognition** — practices of reading, forms of citation, modes of accountability, and frameworks of analysis that can engage swardspeak, tomboi networks, and karmic navigation as theoretical productions without simply assimilating them to academic categories or treating them as raw material for theoretical frameworks developed elsewhere. This is not merely a methodological injunction. It is a political one. The persistent tendency of the academy — including the queer academy — to theorise *about* these communities rather than *with* them, to extract the theoretical content from vernacular practice and present it in scholarly form without adequate acknowledgment of its origin, reproduces the colonial gesture that the scholarship in this section is designed to contest.

The article does not claim to have solved this problem. It names it as the most significant methodological challenge facing the field, and it builds the recognition of vernacular theory into its own analytical commitments — in the decision to take the swardspeak lexicon as theoretical rather than merely descriptive, in the reading of karmic navigation as political knowledge rather than as religious passivity, and in the refusal to treat the tomboi formation as a cultural curiosity to be explained by frameworks that were not developed in conversation with its practitioners. That these commitments are imperfectly realised within the constraints of an academic review article is itself a feature of the problem the article is describing.

## Section V — Global South Formations II: Buddhism, Colonialism, and Sexual Ethics

### 5.1 — What the Texts Actually Say: Paṇḍaka, the Third Precept, and the Limits of Institutional Claims

The most politically consequential question at the intersection of Buddhism and sexuality is not a theological question. It is a philological one: **what do canonical Buddhist texts actually say about gender and sexuality**, as distinct from what Buddhist commentarial traditions have interpreted them to say, what Buddhist modernist reformers have claimed they say, and what contemporary Buddhist institutions assert they have always said? These are three different things, and the conflation of them — the treatment of institutional Buddhist homophobia as though it expressed the timeless teachings of the Buddha — is the most consequential misrepresentation in contemporary Buddhist discourse on sexuality.

The methodological distinction matters politically as well as philologically. Conservative Buddhist institutions in Thailand, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Singapore, and elsewhere routinely invoke canonical authority for positions whose actual textual basis is far thinner than those institutions claim. Progressive Buddhist activists and scholars who challenge those positions need the philological record on their side — not because textual authority should be the final arbiter of ethical questions, but because the claim that Buddhism has always and unambiguously condemned same-sex desire or gender non-conformity is historically false, and demonstrating its falsity matters for the communities whose lives are shaped by that claim.

The foundational philological work in English on this question is Leonard Zwilling’s essay “Homosexuality as Seen in Indian Buddhist Texts,” published in José Ignacio Cabezón’s edited volume *Buddhism, Sexuality, and Gender* (1992). Zwilling’s contribution is primarily methodological: he demonstrates that the term **paṇḍaka** — the category of person excluded from monastic ordination in the Pāli Vinaya and therefore the primary textual basis for claims about Buddhism’s position on sexual and gender non-conformity — is not a stable category whose meaning can be assumed from its appearance in a single text. It is a cluster of distinct categories that later commentators collapsed into an apparently unified class, and the collapse itself is the source of most contemporary misrepresentation.

The paṇḍaka appears in the Mahāvagga section of the Vinaya Piṭaka, in the list of persons ineligible to receive the *upasampadā* — the full ordination as a monk. The list is notably heterogeneous: it includes persons who have committed the most severe moral transgressions (parricide, harming a Buddha, causing a schism in the Saṅgha), persons with specific physical characteristics, and the paṇḍaka. This heterogeneity is itself significant: the list is not a moral taxonomy, organising ineligibles by the gravity of their offence. It is a practical register, and the criteria for inclusion are various — severe moral transgression in some cases, physical characteristics in others, and in the case of the paṇḍaka, a classification that is never fully explained in the canonical text itself.

The reason given in texts that do offer one — and not all do — is that the paṇḍaka is understood as incapable of maintaining the celibacy that monastic life requires, or as constituting a disruptive presence in the monastic community. This is a **practical rather than moral** exclusion. The paṇḍaka is not excluded because their desires are sinful in some absolute sense but because the monastic community is constituted by celibacy, and the paṇḍaka is understood — through assumptions about their desires and behaviour — as incompatible with that constitution. A celibate paṇḍaka who maintained monastic discipline would, on the logic of the texts, present no difficulty. The exclusion is circumstantial, not categorical.

Later commentarial literature — particularly Vasubandhu’s *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya* (4th–5th century CE) and Buddhaghosa’s commentaries on the Pāli Vinaya — developed a taxonomy of five types of paṇḍaka that introduced considerably more specificity than the canonical texts contain. Of these five types, only one — the *āsittapaṇḍaka*, understood as a person who seeks sexual gratification through specific acts with men — clearly involves what might be described as same-sex behaviour. The others involve voyeurism, periodic status, intersex embodiment, and congenital status. The collapse of this heterogeneous set into a single category, and the subsequent equation of that category with “homosexuality” as a modern identity, is a commentarial and ultimately colonial operation rather than a canonical one.

The **third precept** — *kāmesumicchācāra*, the prohibition on sexual misconduct — is the other primary textual basis for institutional Buddhist claims about same-sex activity, and it presents a similar pattern. The precept is one of the Five Precepts binding on lay Buddhists, but its content is not defined in the precept itself. What counts as sexual misconduct is determined by commentarial literature, and that literature is considerably less unanimous on the question of same-sex activity than contemporary Buddhist institutions typically acknowledge. Peter Harvey’s comprehensive survey *An Introduction to Buddhist Ethics* (2000) documents that same-sex activity is not explicitly identified as *kāmesumicchācāra* in the canonical texts, that the commentarial definitions focus on harm, consent, and the violation of specific social relationships rather than on the gender of one’s partner, and that the commentarial tradition’s engagement with same-sex activity is less consistent and less restrictive than institutional Buddhist discourse suggests.[^2]

Cabezón’s *Sexuality in Classical South Asian Buddhism* (2017) — the most comprehensive English-language treatment of the primary sources on this question — confirms and extends these findings through engagement with Sanskrit as well as Pāli sources, and through careful attention to the Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya preserved in Tibetan translation. His central methodological contribution is the insistence on distinguishing three levels of Buddhist teaching that are routinely conflated: the canonical texts themselves, the commentarial tradition that elaborated those texts across centuries of institutional Buddhist life, and the contemporary institutional positions that claim canonical authority for interpretations whose actual basis is primarily commentarial. When a Thai monk asserts that Buddhism forbids homosexuality, he is drawing on the third level while claiming the authority of the first — and the claim to that authority is not, in most cases, philologically defensible.

The single most important canonical Buddhist resource for a queer-affirmative reading of the tradition is not in the Vinaya at all but in the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* — one of the most celebrated texts in the Mahāyāna Buddhist canon, deeply influential in Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese Buddhism. Chapter Seven of the sūtra contains an exchange between the monk Śāriputra and a goddess who has long inhabited the room of the layman Vimalakīrti. The goddess, in the course of a philosophical demonstration of the doctrine of emptiness, transforms Śāriputra into a woman and herself into his male form, then asks him why he is not changing back. When Śāriputra reports that he does not know how to effect the transformation, the goddess observes: “If Śāriputra could change out of this female body, then all women could change likewise. Just as Śāriputra is not really a woman but appears in a woman’s form, all women appear in women’s forms but are not really women. Therefore, the Buddha said, ‘All phenomena are neither male nor female.’”[^3]

The philosophical content of this exchange is precise and consequential. The goddess is not making a claim about social construction in Butler’s sense — she is not arguing that gender has no original, that performance is all the way down. She is making a Madhyamaka argument about **ontological emptiness**: gender characteristics are ultimately empty of inherent existence, they are conventional designations applied to phenomena that do not possess fixed essences, and the apparent naturalness of gendered embodiment is a product of conventional designation rather than of ultimate reality. The political implications of this argument differ from Butler’s deconstruction in ways that Section VI will develop in detail. What matters here is that this teaching is canonical — it appears in one of the most widely studied and revered texts in the Mahāyāna tradition — and that its implications for the naturalness of binary gender are at least as far-reaching as anything in the Western queer theoretical archive.

### 5.2 — Buddhist Modernism and the Colonial Import of Victorian Sexual Ethics

Understanding the gap between what canonical Buddhist texts say and what contemporary Buddhist institutions claim they say requires engaging with the history of **Buddhist modernism** — the reformist movement that transformed Buddhism across Asia in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, largely through a process of engaging, appropriating, and in certain respects absorbing the intellectual and moral frameworks of Protestant Christianity and Western rationalism.

David McMahan’s *The Making of Buddhist Modernism* (2008) is the essential historical account of this transformation. McMahan traces how the Buddhist reform movements that emerged in Sri Lanka, Burma, Thailand, Japan, and elsewhere in response to colonial pressure and missionary challenge produced a new kind of Buddhism: one that emphasised textual authority over ritual practice, rational ethics over cosmological belief, individual spiritual development over communal ceremony, and the compatibility of Buddhism with modern science over the metaphysical claims of traditional Buddhist cosmology. This new Buddhism was, in important respects, shaped in the image of the Protestant Christianity it was arguing against — adopting Protestant forms of organisation, Protestant modes of textual authority, and Protestant ethical emphases while insisting on Buddhism’s superiority to and independence from Christian influence.

The sexual ethics of Buddhist modernism is a specific and consequential instance of this broader transformation. The Victorian era sexual norms that Protestant Christianity brought to Asia through missionary activity and colonial administration — the emphasis on monogamy, the stigmatisation of prostitution, the pathologisation of same-sex desire, the construction of female sexuality as a domain of danger requiring containment — were absorbed into Buddhist modernist ethics and presented, retroactively, as the authentic expression of Buddhist teaching. The mechanisms of absorption varied by context: in some cases, Buddhist modernist reformers explicitly engaged with Christian moral arguments and developed Buddhist equivalents; in others, colonial legal frameworks imposed Christian-derived sexual norms that Buddhist institutions then incorporated into their own ethical discourse without acknowledging the colonial origin of those norms.

The criminalisation of “unnatural offences” across British colonial Southeast and South Asia is the most consequential legal instance of this process. The relevant provisions — typically Section 377 of colonial penal codes, modelled on English law — were imposed by British colonial administrations across India, Burma, Malaya, Singapore, Sri Lanka, and other territories. They were not derived from Buddhist, Hindu, or Islamic legal traditions in those territories. They were British colonial law, and their persistence in postcolonial legal systems as “traditional values” is a specific form of colonial sedimentation whose origin is routinely misrepresented in contemporary debates about LGBT rights in Asia. When Singaporean politicians defend Section 377A of the Penal Code as reflecting Asian values, or when Burmese Buddhist nationalists invoke traditional morality in their campaigns against LGBT rights, they are defending colonial law while claiming indigenous authenticity — and the philological and historical record makes this misrepresentation traceable and challengeable.

Peter Jackson’s scholarship on Thai sexual culture illuminates the specific form that Buddhist modernism’s transformation of sexual ethics took in Thailand — a country that was never formally colonised but that underwent extensive Westernisation through its own choice as a strategy of modernisation and diplomatic survival. Jackson documents how the Thai Buddhist institutional framework absorbed Western sexological categories through Thai medical education in the mid-twentieth century, producing a transformation in the understanding of the *kathoey* from a cosmological category (a karmic condition, the consequence of past-life gender transgression) to a psychological and medical one (a gender identity disorder, potentially treatable through medical intervention). The kathoey was not simply renamed — they were reconceptualised, their social position renegotiated, their relationship to the Buddhist institutional framework reconfigured, through an encounter with Western sexology that took place within the broader project of Thai modernisation.

The result was what Jackson calls the **double taxonomy**: the kathoey is now understood simultaneously through the old karmic framework (a condition produced by past-life transgression, to be managed through merit accumulation and spiritual practice) and the new medical-psychological framework (a gender identity condition, amenable to hormonal and surgical intervention, understood through the conceptual apparatus of Western trans medicine). These frameworks produce different subject positions, different political possibilities, and different relationships to the Buddhist institutional structures that govern social life in Thailand. A subject interpellated through the karmic framework is embedded in a specific moral cosmology that provides both explanation and social positioning; a subject interpellated through the medical framework is a patient in need of treatment, or potentially an identity with rights claims. Most kathoey navigate between both frameworks depending on context — another form of the strategic disidentification that Manalansan identified in the bakla diaspora.

The political implication that follows from Jackson’s analysis is the same conclusion that follows from Zwilling’s and Cabezón’s philological work from a different direction: **the distinction between canonical Buddhist teaching and colonial-era institutional accretion is essential for any political engagement with Buddhist discourse on sexuality and gender**. Buddhist institutions that invoke canonical authority for homophobic and transphobic positions are, in most cases, invoking commentarial and modernist frameworks whose authority is far weaker than claimed. This does not resolve the political question of what Buddhist communities should do — that is a normative question that exceeds the philological — but it does remove the false constraint of a canonical consensus that does not, on examination, exist.

### 5.3 — Queer Buddhist Activism and the Strategic Use of Tradition

Contemporary LGBT activism within Buddhist-majority societies has developed a set of strategic responses to the institutional framework that Jackson and McMahan’s scholarship illuminates — responses that are themselves theoretically productive, not merely politically expedient, because they require working through the specific resources and constraints of Buddhist philosophical traditions rather than simply importing Western rights frameworks.

Jackson’s later work documents three primary strategies that Thai LGBT activists have deployed in relation to the karmic framework. The first is **karmic reinterpretation**: arguing, on the basis of Buddhist cosmological reasoning, that the kathoey’s gender crossing is not evidence of karmic deficit but of karmic advancement — that a being who has lived as both male and female across multiple lifetimes has accumulated wisdom and compassion that make them spiritually distinguished rather than spiritually deficient. This is a revaluation that operates entirely within the Buddhist framework, invoking the same cosmological logic to different political effect. It does not challenge the karmic explanation of gender crossing — it accepts that framework while contesting the negative valence that has been attached to it.

The second strategy is the invocation of **Buddhist compassion ethics**: arguing that the core Buddhist values of *mettā* (loving-kindness) and *karuṇā* (compassion) require acceptance and affirmation of gender-diverse people, regardless of the cosmological status of their gender crossing. This strategy appeals to the ethical rather than the cosmological dimension of Buddhist teaching and has found resonance with Buddhist teachers in Thailand and beyond who are willing to affirm the value and dignity of LGBT lives without necessarily resolving the cosmological question. The Engaged Buddhism movement, associated with figures such as Sulak Sivaraksa in Thailand and Thich Nhat Hanh in the Vietnamese tradition, has developed precisely this kind of social ethics — extending Buddhist compassion principles into explicit engagement with political structures of oppression — though the specific application to LGBT issues has been uneven and contested within those movements.

The third strategy is the importation of **Western rights discourse** through NGO infrastructure — the adoption of the international LGBT rights framework as the vocabulary and conceptual apparatus of local political advocacy. This strategy has the advantage of connecting local activism to global resources, solidarity networks, and legal frameworks. It has the disadvantage that Manalansan and Massad both illuminate from their different directions: it requires presenting the local formation — the kathoey, the tomboi, the bakla — in terms legible to the international LGBT rights framework, which means either assimilating the formation to Western identity categories or accepting the framework’s marginalisation of formations that cannot be so assimilated.

The tension between these three strategies is not resolvable by theoretical clarification — it is a political tension that reflects genuine disagreements about what kind of recognition is desirable, at what cost, on whose terms, and with what relationship to the traditional frameworks that have simultaneously sustained and constrained gender-diverse lives in Buddhist-majority societies. What the theoretical analysis can contribute is precision about what is actually at stake in each strategy: what the karmic reinterpretation preserves and what it concedes; what the compassion argument achieves and what it cannot address; what the rights framework enables and what it forecloses.

One dimension of what the rights framework forecloses deserves explicit attention here, because it bears directly on the article’s central theoretical question. The Western rights framework for LGBT people is premised on **identity**: on the claim that LGBT people constitute a recognisable identity group with stable characteristics, inherent dignity, and rights that follow from that dignity. This is the framework that underlies the claim that homosexuality is a natural variant of human sexuality, that trans identity is a legitimate self-understanding rather than a pathology, that gender diversity deserves legal protection rather than medical correction. These are important claims with real political purchase.

But the Buddhist philosophical framework — specifically the Madhyamaka analysis of selfhood that Section VI will develop — operates from a different ontological premise. The claim that LGBT people have an identity that deserves rights presupposes that identity categories have the kind of stable, inherent existence that the Madhyamaka analysis denies to all phenomena. From the perspective of the two-truths framework, LGBT identity has conventional reality — it is a conventionally valid designation with real political and ethical implications at the conventional level — but it lacks ultimate reality. This is not a reason to abandon the rights framework: conventional reality is real, and the suffering produced by its violation requires conventional political response. But it does mean that the relationship between Buddhist philosophical analysis and the Western rights framework is more complex than simple compatibility or incompatibility, and that Buddhist activists who use the rights framework while remaining committed to Buddhist philosophical principles are navigating a genuine ontological tension rather than a merely strategic one.

That tension — between the conventional political necessity of identity-based rights claims and the ultimate emptiness of the identity categories on which those claims rest — is what Sections VI, VII, and VIII will address directly. It is the theoretical heart of the article’s governing question, and its emergence at the intersection of Buddhist philosophy and queer politics in contemporary Southeast Asia is the specific historical and regional grounding from which the article’s philosophical claims draw their political urgency.

## Section VI — Buddhist Ontology I: Anattā, Śūnyatā, and the Empty Subject

### 6.1 — Anattā: The Precise Claim

The transition from the regional and historical analysis of Sections IV and V to the philosophical analysis that this section develops requires a preliminary methodological clarification. The argument that Buddhist ontology provides resources for queer theory does not depend on the claim that Buddhist-majority cultures are more affirming of gender diversity than Western ones — Section V has already demonstrated that this claim is historically false. It does not depend on the claim that Buddhist philosophy and queer theory are naturally compatible — the preceding analysis has shown that their relationship is complex, contested, and inflected by colonial history in ways that simple compatibility narratives obscure. It depends, rather, on a more specific and philosophically modest claim: that the Buddhist analysis of the nature of the self — its processual character, its conventional reality, and its ultimate emptiness of inherent existence — provides a different and more radical ontological ground for thinking about gender and sexuality than the psychoanalytic-phenomenological frameworks that the Anglo-American canonical triad inherits. This is a philosophical argument, and it must be made with philosophical precision.

The foundational text is the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta* — the Discourse on the Characteristic of Non-Self — preserved in the Saṃyutta Nikāya (SN 22.59) and understood in the Theravāda tradition as the second discourse delivered by the historical Buddha after his enlightenment. The argument the sutta makes is simultaneously logical and phenomenological: it proceeds through the examination of each of the five aggregates (*khandhas*) that comprise what is conventionally designated a person — form (*rūpa*), feeling (*vedanā*), perception (*saññā*), mental formations (*saṅkhāra*), and consciousness (*viññāṇa*) — and demonstrates of each that it is not the self.

The demonstration has a specific logical structure that is worth attending to carefully, because it is frequently misread as a simple assertion that the self does not exist. The argument is not “there is no self” — that would be the nihilist position that the Buddha explicitly rejected as a misunderstanding of the doctrine. The argument is: **if form (or feeling, or perception, and so on) were the self, then form would be fully under our control — we could determine what form we have, how it functions, how it changes — but since form is not fully under our control, it cannot be the self we mean when we say “I.”** The argument is diagnostic rather than simply assertoric: it asks what we mean by “self” — permanent, substantially real, independent, fully our own — and demonstrates that none of the components of what we actually are satisfies those criteria.

The conclusion is not annihilation but reorientation: the aggregates are real in a conventional sense — they arise, they function, they produce experience and action, they have causal efficacy — but none of them is the self, and the whole composed of them, while conventionally designated as a person, does not possess the kind of inherent, independent, self-standing existence that ordinary experience assumes and that produces the clinging and suffering the Buddhist path is designed to address. The self, in the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*‘s analysis, has **conventional reality** (*saṃvṛti-satya*) but not **ultimate reality** (*paramārtha-satya*) — a distinction that the Madhyamaka tradition developed into the two-truths framework that is the philosophical apparatus most directly relevant to the article’s governing argument.

The distinction must be stated precisely because it is frequently collapsed in both directions. Against the nihilist collapse — the reading of anattā as “there is no self, therefore nothing matters, ethics dissolves, political action is pointless” — the two-truths framework insists that conventional reality is genuinely real at its own level, that conventional persons with conventional suffering require conventional political response, and that the recognition of ultimate emptiness does not negate but reorients conventional engagement. Against the assimilationist collapse — the reading of anattā as equivalent to Butler’s constructivism, as simply another way of saying that the self is socially constructed — the two-truths framework insists that the Buddhist claim is more radical than constructivism: it is not that the self is constructed rather than natural (the constructed self is still real, still a self, still organically whole), but that the self, however constructed, is ultimately empty of the inherent existence that both the natural self and the constructed self assume.

This is the philosophical distinction on which the article’s central argument depends, and it deserves a concrete illustration. Butler’s performativity demonstrates that gender has no original — there is no prior gendered nature that performance expresses; performance is all the way down. This is a powerful deconstruction of gender’s naturalness. But the subject who performs in Butler’s account is still a subject with psychic depth, with incorporated losses, with a constitutive outside that defines it, with a Symbolic Order that structures its desires. The construction goes deep, but it stops somewhere — at the Lacanian subject of the signifier, at the psychic structure produced through melancholic incorporation, at the body as a surface of inscription for social norms. Butler’s constructed subject is still, in a philosophically specific sense, a substantial subject: it has determinate characteristics, it persists across time through the sedimentation of its citational practices, it has a constitutive interiority even if that interiority is produced rather than given.

The Buddhist analysis says something more radical: that subject, however produced, is ultimately empty of the inherent self-existence that its production makes it appear to have. The melancholic incorporation of foreclosed desires, the passionate attachment to the norms that constitute the subject, the psychic depth built from accumulated losses — all of this is conventionally real. It arises. It has causal efficacy. It produces suffering. But it does not have the inherent, self-standing existence that makes passionate attachment feel like the only alternative to annihilation. The subject constituted through subjection is a conventional designation for a stream of conditioned arising, and that stream — unlike the Lacanian subject — is not organised around an irreducible lack. It is organised around **karma**: the causal residues of past mental, verbal, and physical actions that condition arising without constituting a fixed essence.

### 6.2 — Nāgārjuna’s Śūnyatā: Dependent Origination as Emptiness

The philosophical apparatus that most rigorously develops the anattā analysis into a comprehensive ontology of emptiness is the Madhyamaka school, founded by Nāgārjuna in the second century CE and systematised by his successor Candrakīrti in the seventh. Nāgārjuna’s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* — Root Verses on the Middle Way — is the primary text of this tradition, and its twenty-seventh chapter constitutes one of the most sustained arguments against essentialist ontology in any philosophical tradition.

The key formulation is *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* 24.18, the verse in which Nāgārjuna identifies three things as equivalent:

> *yat pratītyasamutpannaṃ śūnyatāṃ tāṃ pracakṣmahe* /
> *sā prajñaptir upādāya pratipat saiva madhyamā* //

“Whatever arises dependently — that we call emptiness. That is a dependent designation, and that itself is the middle way.”

Three terms are here identified as equivalent or mutually implying: **dependent origination** (*pratītyasamutpāda*), **emptiness** (*śūnyatā*), and **conventional designation** (*prajñapti*). To say that something arises dependently is to say it is empty; to say it is empty is to say it exists as a conventional designation rather than through its own inherent nature; to say it is a conventional designation is to say it arises dependently. The middle way that this equivalence constitutes is the path between the two extremes that Nāgārjuna rejects: the extreme of essentialism (things have inherent, fixed, self-standing natures) and the extreme of nihilism (things have no reality at all).

What this means for the subject constituted through Butler’s heterosexual matrix is philosophically precise. The matrix is real — conventionally, dependently, as a complex of social practices, normative discourses, legal structures, medical regimes, and psychic formations that condition the arising of gendered and sexual subjects. It is empty — ultimately, it lacks the inherent self-existence that would make it necessary rather than contingent, natural rather than produced, fixed rather than subject to transformation through the conditions that sustain it. And it is a conventional designation — the name “heterosexual matrix” picks out a real pattern in the conventional world without that pattern having a self-standing reality independent of the conditions through which it is constituted and sustained.

The political implication of this analysis is not that the matrix can be wished away by recognising its emptiness. Conventional reality is genuinely real at the conventional level, and the suffering it produces requires conventional response. But the recognition of ultimate emptiness does change **how one acts in relation to the conventional** — it removes the ontological necessity that makes the matrix appear as a fixed, permanent, and immovable feature of reality, and it removes the basis for the passionate attachment (*upādāna*) to the norms that constitute the subject, the attachment that makes those norms appear to the melancholic subject as the only alternative to annihilation.

Two textual clarifications are necessary at this point, and the epistemic discipline the article has established throughout requires stating them explicitly.

**First clarification:** The two-truths framework as it appears in Nāgārjuna’s text and as it was elaborated by Candrakīrti in the *Madhyamakāvatāra* and the *Prasannapadā* is a philosophical analysis with specific technical content that is not fully captured by any summary, including this article’s. The Madhyamaka tradition has generated centuries of sophisticated philosophical debate about the precise relationship between the two truths, the status of conventional reality, the nature of the conventional subject, and the relationship between conventional and ultimate analysis — debates that are not settled and that this article cannot adjudicate. What this article uses is the broad architecture of the two-truths framework — the distinction between conventional and ultimate validity, and the relationship between dependent origination, emptiness, and conventional designation — without claiming to resolve the internal debates of the Madhyamaka tradition.

**Second clarification:** The application of Madhyamaka analysis to the categories of gender and sexuality — specifically the claim that “gender is empty of inherent existence” — is a theoretical move that goes beyond what Nāgārjuna’s text itself addresses. Nāgārjuna’s arguments are about the nature of phenomena in general, not about gender specifically. The application to gender is an inference from the general analysis, and while the article holds that inference to be philosophically sound, it is flagging it as an inference rather than a direct textual claim.

The most important adjacent text for this application is the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*‘s goddess episode — not a Madhyamaka philosophical text in the technical sense but a narrative instantiation of Madhyamaka insight that directly addresses gender. When the goddess observes that “all phenomena are neither male nor female,” she is making the application of the Madhyamaka analysis to gender that this section is developing — and doing so with canonical authority that the theoretical inference alone does not possess. The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* provides the textual ground; the Madhyamaka philosophical apparatus provides the theoretical framework for understanding what the goddess’s observation means.

### 6.3 — The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* and the Emptiness of Gender

The encounter between Śāriputra and the goddess in Chapter Seven of the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* is, in the context of this article’s governing argument, the most important passage in the Buddhist canonical literature. Its importance is not merely that it addresses gender — though the scarcity of canonical Buddhist engagements with gender that are not organised around either the exclusion or the spiritual subordination of women makes the passage’s affirmative philosophical content notable. Its importance is that it makes the application of the Madhyamaka analysis to gender in a specifically narrative, embodied, and demonstrative mode that conveys what a purely philosophical argument cannot: the experiential dimension of recognising gender’s emptiness.

The narrative context is essential to the argument. Śāriputra is one of the Buddha’s two chief disciples, renowned for his mastery of the Abhidharma — the systematic philosophical analysis of the canonical teachings. He is, in the sūtra’s intellectual hierarchy, as well-qualified an interlocutor as the tradition can produce. When the goddess transforms him — when he finds himself inhabiting a female body not through any choice or practice but through the demonstration of another’s insight — the phenomenological point is made directly: the body one inhabits is not a fixed natural given but a conventional formation that can, under conditions of sufficient insight into its emptiness, be otherwise.

Śāriputra’s inability to change back is the philosophical crux. If the female body he now inhabits were constituted by a fixed, inherent female nature — if the femininity of the body were an intrinsic property of the physical form rather than a conventional designation — then insight into that nature should provide the basis for transformation. But there is no such inherent female nature to be found, which means there is no fixed handle by which the transformation can be grasped. The goddess makes this explicit: “I’ve been here for twelve years,” she tells Śāriputra, “and have looked for the innate characteristics of the female sex and haven’t been able to find them.” The search for inherent gender characteristics is the search for what is not there — and the recognition that it is not there is itself the liberating insight.

The political difference between this analysis and Butler’s deconstruction has been flagged in Section 5.1 and deserves more careful development here. Butler’s performativity demonstrates that gender has no original: there is no prediscursive natural sex that gender then expresses, no prior gendered nature that performance represents, only the citational chain of performances that produces the illusion of an original. The political consequence of this demonstration is agonistic: the citational chain is real and its normative force is real, and resistance must take the form of subversive repetition — repeating the norm with a difference sufficient to expose its contingency without falling outside the order of recognition that the norm organises. The struggle is ongoing, never final, always vulnerable to recuperation by the normative order’s self-renaturalising tendency.

The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*‘s analysis makes a different kind of claim. Gender does not merely lack an original — it lacks **inherent existence**. The distinction matters because inherent existence is a stronger claim than origination: something can be fully constructed (lacking any original) while still possessing a kind of settled, functional reality that is not easily disturbed by the recognition of its constructedness. Butler’s subject — constituted through subjection, organised around incorporated losses, maintained by passionate attachment to the norms that constitute it — is exactly this: fully constructed but settled, psychically real, and resistant to transformation by the mere recognition of its constructedness, because the recognition does not dissolve the psychic investment that sustains the construction.

The Buddhist analysis addresses this resistance at a different level. The recognition that gender is ultimately empty of inherent existence is not merely an intellectual insight — it is, in the Buddhist framework, a transformative realisation (*bodhi*) whose effects on the subject’s relationship to its own construction are more fundamental than any intellectual critique can produce. This is why the goddess does not simply argue that gender is constructed: she enacts the recognition of gender’s emptiness by transforming Śāriputra’s embodiment and thereby demonstrating what the recognition produces. The argument is embodied, not merely propositional.

This distinction between intellectual understanding and transformative realisation is crucial for the article’s political argument, and it connects directly to the altered states analysis that Section VII will develop. If the recognition of gender’s ultimate emptiness requires transformative realisation rather than mere intellectual critique — if insight of this kind is produced through specific practices of embodied attention rather than through genealogical deconstruction — then the forms of knowledge and practice that can sustain queer political life are not limited to the textualist-deconstructive repertoire that the canon’s Foucauldian inheritance has provided. Buddhist meditative practice, as Section VII will argue, produces a specific form of embodied knowledge about the constructed nature of the subject and its characteristics — including its gender — that neither the canon nor its queer of colour critical extension can account for.

What the canonical Buddhist literature makes available, in summary, is the following set of resources for queer theory. The anattā doctrine provides an analysis of the subject’s conventional reality and ultimate emptiness that goes beyond Butler’s constructivism without abandoning it. The Madhyamaka two-truths framework provides the philosophical apparatus for holding conventional political engagement and ultimate ontological critique in productive tension, without either dissolving the conventional into the ultimate (nihilism) or elevating the conventional to ultimate status (essentialism). The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*‘s narrative demonstration provides canonical authority for the application of the emptiness analysis to gender specifically, and in a mode — embodied, demonstrative, experiential — that a purely philosophical argument cannot replicate. Together, these resources constitute not an alternative to the queer theoretical framework the article has developed across its preceding sections but a more radical ontological ground on which that framework can stand — a ground that does not require the melancholic subject’s passionate attachment to the conditions of its existence, because it shows that the subject to whom those conditions appear as necessary was never the ultimately real entity that the attachment treated it as being.

The limit of this analysis — the point at which the philosophical precision of the Buddhist framework runs up against its own inability to determine what follows politically — is the question of what it means to act from the recognition of emptiness. That is the question Sections VII and VIII address: not the abstract philosophical question but the concrete political one. What does queer liberation look like when the subject to be liberated is ultimately empty? What does the struggle for trans healthcare access, for legal recognition, for the right to mourn queer deaths publicly mean when the identity that grounds those claims is conventionally real and ultimately empty simultaneously? The bodhisattva model that Section VIII will propose is this article’s answer — partial, provisional, and aware of its own limitations in the way that the epistemic discipline of this analysis requires.

## Section VII — Buddhist Ontology II: Upādāna, Passionate Attachment, and Altered States

### 7.1 — Upādāna and Passionate Attachment: The Structural Parallel

Section VI established that the Buddhist subject is a stream of conditioned arising without a fixed centre of inherent existence — conventionally real, ultimately empty. This section asks a more concrete question: if the subject is ultimately empty, what sustains the apparent solidity of its conventional reality? What keeps the melancholic subject attached to the norms that injure it, even after the philosophical analysis has demonstrated that those norms are ultimately empty? The Buddhist answer is *upādāna* — clinging, grasping, appropriation — and the structural parallel between this concept and Butler’s passionate attachment is the theoretical pivot on which this article’s central argument turns.

*Upādāna* is the ninth of the twelve links of dependent origination (*dvādaśāṅga pratītyasamutpāda*) — the causal chain that, in the Buddhist analysis, generates the cycle of conditioned existence and the suffering it produces. The chain begins with *avijjā* (ignorance of the ultimate nature of phenomena) and moves through volitional formations, consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense bases, contact, feeling, and craving (*taṇhā*) before arriving at *upādāna*. The sequence matters: *upādāna* arises from craving, and craving arises from feeling — from the immediate affective response to pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral sensory and mental experience. The chain is not arbitrary. It traces a specific causal pathway from a fundamental misapprehension of reality (ignorance of emptiness) through the affective and volitional responses that misapprehension generates (craving) to the solidification of those responses into an ongoing orientation toward the world (clinging).

*Upādāna* leads to *bhava* — becoming, the compulsion to maintain and reproduce a particular mode of existence — and from becoming to *jāti*, birth into a specific form of life, and from birth to the aging and death that are its inevitable consequences. This is the Buddhist account of how ignorance becomes suffering: not through a single catastrophic fall but through a specific causal pathway in which the fundamental misapprehension of reality generates the affective responses that generate the clinging that generates the becoming that generates the specific forms of life — and their attendant sufferings — that ignorance makes appear necessary.

The Pāli analysis identifies four specific forms of *upādāna*, and the distinctions between them are philosophically important. *Kāmupādāna* is clinging to sensual pleasures. *Diṭṭhupādāna* is clinging to views and ideological positions. *Sīlabbatupādāna* is clinging to rules, rituals, and ethical observances as ends in themselves rather than as means. And *attavādupādāna* is clinging to a doctrine of self — the foundational form of clinging, the grasping at the belief that there is a substantial, permanent, independent self that must be maintained, protected, and recognised. It is this fourth form that the Buddhist analysis identifies as most fundamental: not the clinging to particular objects or pleasures, not the attachment to particular views or practices, but the prior commitment to a self for which all these clingings then become necessary.

The structural parallel with Butler’s passionate attachment is not identity but **homology** — the same causal pattern operating in different theoretical frameworks with different philosophical commitments. Butler’s passionate attachment is the subject’s investment in the norms that constitute it, an investment so fundamental that relinquishing the norm feels like the dissolution of the self. The Buddhist *attavādupādāna* is the clinging to a doctrine of self — the investment in the belief that there is a substantial self — so fundamental that the recognition of selflessness feels like annihilation. In both cases, the attachment precedes and conditions all other attachments: it is the ground on which particular clingings — to specific norms, specific identities, specific forms of social recognition — are founded. In both cases, the attachment is maintained not by explicit belief or conscious choice but by the deep structure of how the subject is organised: by the psychic architecture of the melancholic incorporation (Butler) or by the causal chain from ignorance through craving to clinging (Buddhism).

The crucial difference between the two frameworks — and it is the difference on which the article’s political argument depends — lies in the status of this attachment. In Butler’s analysis, passionate attachment is **constitutive**: the subject would not exist without the norms to which it is attached, and to release the attachment is to dissolve the subject itself. There is no subject prior to or outside the passionate attachment; the attachment and the subject are the same thing. The political consequence is that transformation must be indirect, structural, gradual — the slow accumulation of subversive repetitions that shift what the norm can sustain without requiring the subject to surrender its constitutive investment in the norm.

In the Buddhist analysis, *upādāna* is **conditioned**: it arises through a specific causal pathway (from ignorance through feeling through craving to clinging) and therefore, in principle, can cease through the cessation of its conditions. This is not a voluntarist claim — the cessation of *upādāna* is not achieved by simply deciding to stop clinging. It is achieved through the progressive cultivation of insight (*vipassanā*) that sees through the misapprehension of inherent existence that generates the craving from which clinging arises. But it is achievable in a way that Butler’s framework cannot sustain: the conventional subject does not dissolve when *upādāna* is progressively loosened — it continues to function, to act, to engage — but its relationship to its own constitution changes fundamentally. It acts without the desperate investment in self-maintenance that makes Butler’s subject structurally unable to relinquish what harms it.

This is the difference between **transformation through deconstruction** — Butler’s method, which exposes the contingency of norms without dissolving the subject’s investment in them — and **transformation through insight** — the Buddhist path, which progressively loosens the clinging that sustains the subject’s investment by revealing the emptiness of both the self that clings and the objects to which it clings. The two methods are not mutually exclusive. They operate at different levels of the same problem — the conventional level (Butler) and the ultimate level (Buddhism) — and the two-truths framework holds that both levels are real at their own register. What the Buddhist analysis adds is not the refutation of Butler’s method but the ontological reorientation that makes the subject capable of Butler’s political work without the cruel optimism that makes that work structurally self-defeating.

The connection to Berlant is now fully visible. Berlant’s impasse — the temporal structure in which the good life promise has broken down but cannot be relinquished without losing the framework of hope that makes life liveable — is the macropolitical instantiation of *upādāna*: the clinging to conditions whose inadequacy is experientially known but whose relinquishment feels like the dissolution of the self whose life was at stake. Berlant’s lateral agency — the ordinary human management of conditions that are not changing — is the conventional navigation of the impasse without the insight that would loosen the clinging. The Buddhist path through the impasse is not a better promise, not Muñoz’s utopian horizon, not Edelman’s anti-social refusal. It is the progressive loosening of the clinging that makes the impasse feel necessary — the recognition, partial and always ongoing, that the self for whom the good life was required was never the ultimately real entity that *upādāna* treated it as being.

### 7.2 — Altered States as Embodied Epistemology

The analysis of *upādāna* developed in Section 7.1 is philosophical — it operates through textual argument, conceptual analysis, and the careful drawing of distinctions. But the Buddhist tradition does not understand the loosening of clinging to be achieved primarily through philosophical argument. It is achieved through **practice** — through specific forms of embodied attention, meditative cultivation, and the cultivation of insight that are not reducible to intellectual understanding of the philosophical analysis. The *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*’s argument that the aggregates are not self is not, in the Buddhist framework, a proposition to be accepted on the basis of logical persuasion. It is a description of what is recognised, directly and experientially, through sustained meditative practice.

The meditative absorptions — the **jhānas** (Pāli) or **dhyānas** (Sanskrit) — describe a progressive series of altered states in which the ordinary structure of experience is systematically transformed. In the first jhāna, discursive thought is stilled and replaced by sustained attention and the pleasure of the unified mind. In the second, even directed thought ceases and the meditator rests in a state of inner confidence and equanimity. In the third and fourth jhānas, pleasure and discomfort are progressively superseded by a profound equanimity that is neither positive nor negative but simply present — what the texts describe as the purity of mindfulness and equanimity undisturbed by the ordinary reactive patterns of desire and aversion. In the formless absorptions that follow, even the sense of embodied presence dissolves: the meditator rests in boundless space, then boundless consciousness, then a state that the texts describe as neither perception nor non-perception, before arriving at the cessation of perception and feeling.

The phenomenological content of these states is philosophically significant for this article’s argument in a specific way. In the deeper jhānas and in the states of insight that formal meditation practice cultivates, the ordinary self-structure — the sense of being a subject observing objects, of having a perspective on the world from a specific locus, of maintaining a continuous identity across the flux of experience — is **temporarily suspended**. This is not unconsciousness. The Pāli texts are emphatic that the meditative absorptions involve a specific quality of heightened awareness rather than the simple absence of awareness. What is suspended is not awareness itself but the **structure** through which awareness is ordinarily organised: the subject-object distinction, the ongoing narrative of selfhood, the evaluative monitoring that sustains the sense of being an “I” to whom things happen.

This suspension is the experiential confirmation, in the Buddhist framework, of the philosophical analysis that Section VI developed. The recognition that the self has no inherent existence is not merely an intellectual concession made on the basis of philosophical argument — it is, in states of deep meditative absorption and insight, a **direct experiential finding**: the ordinary self-structure is discovered, through attending to it with sustained precision, to have been a construction all along. Not a fiction — the construction is real at the conventional level — but a construction, conditioned, impermanent, and without the fixed centre that ordinary experience takes it to have.

The theoretically significant question for this article is whether this form of knowledge — embodied, experiential, produced through specific practices of attention rather than through discursive analysis — has parallels in other traditions and practices, and what those parallels reveal about the epistemic resources available to queer theory. The answer to the first part of this question is yes, and the parallels are structural rather than merely metaphorical.

The altered states produced in **kink practice** — specifically the states known colloquially as subspace and described phenomenologically in terms of dissolution of ordinary ego-boundaries, suspension of the usual cognitive monitoring, heightened presence, and a profound sense of being simultaneously less self-determined and more fully alive — share a recognisable deep structure with the meditative states that the Buddhist tradition cultivates, despite being produced through entirely different means and embedded in entirely different cultural and ethical frameworks. In subspace, as in the deeper jhānas, the ordinary structure of self-maintenance is temporarily suspended: the sustained attention to sensation that kink practice cultivates, the deliberate relinquishment of ordinary decision-making authority through consensual surrender, and the specific forms of physical intensity that some practitioners describe as inducing the state, all produce a temporary dissolution of the self-monitoring, future-oriented, identity-maintaining cognitive activity that ordinarily organises experience.

The parallel extends to two other traditions. The Sufi concept of **fanāʾ** — the annihilation of the self in the divine, described by Ibn ʿArabī and Rūmī as the highest form of mystical attainment — involves the dissolution of the individual subject into the divine reality in terms that are phenomenologically resonant with both the Buddhist meditative states and the kink subspace, even as the ontological interpretation is entirely different. For Ibn ʿArabī, the dissolved subject participates in the divine reality of *waḥdat al-wujūd* — the unity of being. For the Buddhist, the dissolved subject recognises the emptiness that was always already its nature. For the kink practitioner, the dissolved subject returns, refreshed and altered, to a conventional identity that has been temporarily but genuinely suspended. The phenomenological deep structure is shared; the metaphysical interpretations diverge significantly.

The Christian tradition of **unio mystica** — the mystical union with God described by Meister Eckhart in terms of the *Seelengrund* (ground of the soul), by Teresa of Ávila in terms of the interior castle’s innermost room, by John of the Cross in terms of the dark night of the soul that precedes the dawn of union — constitutes a fourth instance of the same structural pattern: a state in which the ordinary self-structure is dissolved, suspended, or transcended in an encounter with a reality that exceeds the subject’s ordinary capacity for self-maintenance.

The epistemological claim this article is making on the basis of this structural parallel is specific and carefully limited. These states are not identical — the Buddhist meditator, the kink practitioner, the Sufi mystic, and the Christian contemplative are not having the same experience, interpreted differently. They are having different experiences, embedded in different cultural, ethical, and metaphysical frameworks, produced through different practices, and understood through different conceptual vocabularies. The parallel is structural rather than experiential: all four traditions produce states in which the ordinary construction of the self — including its gender, its social identity, its characteristic patterns of desire and aversion — becomes recognisable as construction, temporarily but genuinely, through the suspension of the self-maintenance activity that ordinarily conceals the constructedness.

The claim this article makes is that these states constitute a form of **embodied epistemological access** to the constructed nature of the subject — and therefore of gender and desire — that the queer theory canon’s textualist inheritance has no adequate framework for engaging. Butler’s genealogical critique of the heterosexual matrix operates through discourse — it demonstrates, through the analysis of texts, practices, and institutional regimes, that what appears natural is historically produced. This demonstration is politically important and intellectually rigorous. But it is limited by the epistemological assumption that its inheritance from Foucault imposes: that knowledge of the constructed nature of the subject is produced through the analysis of the discourses that constitute it. The altered states traditions suggest a different epistemological pathway: **knowledge through the temporary suspension of the construction itself**, rather than through the analysis of the discourses that sustain it.

This is not a mystical claim in the dismissive sense — not a claim that certain experiences provide access to a transcendent reality beyond the reach of ordinary reason. It is a phenomenological claim: that specific practices of embodied attention produce states of experience in which the ordinary self-structure is temporarily suspended, and that this suspension provides a specific form of direct evidence — not proof, but evidence — that the self-structure is a construction. The evidence is not available to someone who has not undergone the relevant practices, which is precisely what makes it a form of embodied knowledge rather than a propositional claim accessible to anyone through reasoning alone.

### 7.3 — The Embodied Epistemology Gap in Queer Theory

The structural parallel between Buddhist meditative states, kink subspace, Sufi fanāʾ, and Christian unio mystica points to a specific gap in the queer theoretical archive — a gap that has consequences for both the theory’s intellectual adequacy and its political reach.

Queer theory’s foundational methodological commitment is to the **body as a site of political inscription**: the body is not a natural given but a produced surface, inscribed by normative practices, disciplinary regimes, and the citational chains of gender performance. Butler’s performativity, Foucault’s genealogy of the sexed body, Puar’s assemblage theory — all of these frameworks are, in different ways, accounts of how bodies are produced, marked, and regulated by power. The body is central to queer theory’s political analysis.

But queer theory has no adequate account of what the body **knows** — of the forms of knowledge that are produced through the body’s own activities, particularly in states where the discursive constitution of the body-subject is temporarily suspended. The body as a site of inscription is the body as an object of discourse. The body in altered states is the body as an epistemological agent — as something that produces knowledge through its own processes, rather than receiving the knowledge produced through discourse about it. Queer theory has invested heavily in the former and almost entirely neglected the latter.

This neglect has a specific intellectual genealogy. The Foucauldian inheritance that structures queer theory’s methodology is fundamentally discursivist: it holds that knowledge is produced through discourse, and that the analysis of knowledge therefore requires the analysis of discourses and their conditions of possibility. This is an enormously powerful methodological commitment, and the scholarship it has produced — the genealogy of sexuality, the analysis of the heterosexual matrix, the critique of homonationalism — has been indispensable. But its power is also its limitation: it cannot easily account for forms of knowledge that are not produced through discourse, that are pre-discursive or post-discursive, that are embodied rather than textual, that are produced through specific practices of attention rather than through the analysis of representations.

Ann Ahmed’s *Queer Phenomenology* (2006) is the text in the existing literature that comes closest to bridging this gap. Ahmed’s analysis of sexual orientation as a direction of the body toward certain objects, away from others — her account of heterosexuality as a straightening, queerness as a disorientation — operates at the level of embodied phenomenology rather than discursive analysis. The queer body in Ahmed’s framework is not merely a surface inscribed by norms but a body with a specific orientation in space, a body that feels at home or out of place in specific configurations of the social world, a body whose disorientation opens specific perceptual possibilities. This is closer to the embodied epistemology that the altered states analysis requires than anything in Butler or Foucault.

But Ahmed’s phenomenological analysis, while enormously productive, operates within the framework of everyday embodied experience — the ordinary body moving through social space, encountering objects and others, feeling oriented or disoriented. It does not engage the phenomenology of the body in **extreme states** — the body in intense pain, the body in meditative absorption, the body in the specific intensity of kink practice, the body in the experience of serious illness or proximity to death. These are states in which the ordinary structures of embodied experience are not merely redirected or disoriented but fundamentally transformed, and they produce a specific form of knowledge about the constructedness of those ordinary structures that Ahmed’s phenomenology of everyday orientation cannot access.

The clinical context is the most urgent instance of this gap. Patients with serious illness — particularly those with cancer, for whom the confrontation with the body’s altered and potentially terminal condition is both inescapable and extended — routinely undergo experiences that are structurally resonant with the altered states that the Buddhist tradition describes and the kink literature documents. The dissolution of the ordinary self-structure through illness, the suspension of the future-oriented cognitive activity that organises ordinary experience, the specific quality of attention that serious illness forces — these are not merely metaphors for the Buddhist or kink experiences. They are instances of the same broad class of phenomena: states in which the ordinary construction of the self becomes recognisable as construction, not through philosophical argument or political critique but through the body’s direct encounter with its own contingency.

The theoretical implication is not that illness is a spiritual practice or that patients should be consoled by Buddhist philosophy. It is that the embodied knowledge produced in clinical contexts — by patients, by their families, and by the clinicians who attend them — constitutes a form of theoretical resource that neither queer theory nor Buddhist studies has adequately engaged. What does it mean to be a gendered, desiring subject when the body that grounds that subjecthood is radically altered by disease? What forms of identity persist and what forms dissolve when the ordinary self-maintenance activity is interrupted by the urgency of illness? What kinds of care are possible for subjects in states of radical vulnerability that do not reproduce the cruel optimism of promising a recovery that may not come, the passive nihilism of withdrawing investment from lives that are diminishing, or the lateral management of conditions that are not changing?

These are questions that the theoretical synthesis this article has been developing — queer theory, Buddhist ontology, and the phenomenology of altered states — is, uniquely, positioned to address. They are also questions that remain genuinely open: the clinical and care dimensions of the argument have been flagged throughout this article as a domain that the existing literature has not adequately explored, and the article does not claim to have resolved them here. What it claims is that the encounter between the three traditions it has developed — Anglo-American queer theory, Madhyamaka Buddhist philosophy, and the Global South formations that resist both — generates a conceptual apparatus more adequate to these questions than any of those traditions could generate alone.

The gap in queer theory’s epistemological repertoire — the absence of an account of embodied knowledge produced in states where the discursive constitution of the subject is temporarily suspended — is not a minor oversight. It is a structural feature of a field that committed early and deeply to the analysis of discourse and that has not yet fully developed the methodological resources for engaging what the body knows through its own processes of transformation. The altered states literature, the Buddhist meditative phenomenology, and the clinical experience of radical embodied change are three sites where that knowledge is produced. Developing theoretical frameworks adequate to its recognition is one of the most significant tasks facing the field in the years ahead.

## Section VIII — Synthesis: The Bodhisattva Model and Queer Political Ontology

### 8.1 — The Political-Dissolution Tension and Its Buddhist Resolution

The analysis developed across Sections VI and VII generates a tension that the article has been building toward since its opening pages and that it must now address directly: if the path through passionate attachment involves recognising the ultimate emptiness of the subject constituted through subjection — if the Buddhist analysis shows that the melancholic subject’s desperate investment in the norms that constitute it is a form of *upādāna* that can, through insight, be progressively loosened — does this recognition dissolve the basis for queer political mobilisation? If the identity categories that ground claims to trans healthcare access, legal recognition, and the right to mourn queer deaths publicly are ultimately empty of inherent existence, what grounds those claims? If the self to whom things must happen is a conventional designation for a stream of conditioned arising without a fixed centre, who is fighting for trans rights, and on whose behalf?

The question is not rhetorical. It is the objection that any politically serious reader will bring to the Buddhist philosophical analysis, and it has been anticipated in the queer theory literature most directly by the critique of Edelman’s anti-futurity: that the embrace of the death drive is a luxury available only to those with a survivable present, that the aestheticisation of dissolution is a position affordable only by those whose lives are not already being dissolved by structural violence. The Buddhist recognition of ultimate emptiness might appear to be the same gesture in a different register: another form of anti-political quietism that relinquishes the very subject positions that the most vulnerable queer and trans people require in order to survive.

The Madhyamaka tradition anticipated this objection in its own terms. Nāgārjuna’s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* Chapter 24 begins with precisely this challenge: an interlocutor argues that if all things are empty, then the Four Noble Truths, the path, and liberation itself are empty, which means nothing matters and nothing is possible. Nāgārjuna’s response — one of the most important arguments in the history of Buddhist philosophy — is that the objection confuses the two truths:

> *vyavahāraṃ anāśritya paramārtho na deśyate* /
> *paramārtham anāgamya nirvāṇaṃ nādhigamyate* //

“Without relying on convention, the ultimate cannot be taught. Without attaining the ultimate, nirvāṇa cannot be achieved.” (*Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* 24.10)[^4]

Emptiness does not dissolve the conventional. It is **disclosed through the conventional** and requires the conventional as its medium. The person is conventionally real; their suffering is conventionally real; the political structures that produce and sustain that suffering are conventionally real; the actions required to transform those structures are conventionally real and conventionally necessary. None of this is negated by the recognition that these conventional realities are ultimately empty of inherent self-existence. What is changed is not the reality of the conventional world but the **relationship of the acting subject to that world** — specifically, the *upādāna* that makes the conventional world appear as a domain of fixed necessities rather than of conditioned, interdependent, ultimately empty arising.

The political implication is precise. Trans healthcare access matters — conventionally, urgently, in ways that require immediate political response. Legal recognition of gender identity matters. The right to mourn queer deaths publicly matters. The structural violence of the heterosexual matrix matters. All of this matters at the conventional level with full force, and the recognition of ultimate emptiness does not diminish that force. What the recognition changes is the **psychic relationship of the political subject to the struggle** — the release from the *upādāna* that makes the subject’s investment in the struggle’s outcomes a form of passionate attachment whose failure produces the specific kind of suffering that Berlant calls cruel optimism: the attachment to promises that are obstacles to flourishing, the investment in normative futures that the structural conditions systematically foreclose.

The bodhisattva ideal provides the model for what political engagement looks like from this reoriented ontological ground. The bodhisattva — the being committed to the liberation of all sentient beings from suffering — does not act less urgently because of the recognition of emptiness. The urgency is, if anything, intensified: the recognition that all beings suffer under conditions of *upādāna*, that suffering is produced by the misapprehension of inherent existence that generates craving and clinging, that the conditions producing suffering are not fixed necessities but conditioned arisings that can be transformed — this recognition produces not detachment but the specific form of compassion (*karuṇā*) that is possible only when the compassionate agent is not attached to the outcome of their compassion in a way that makes their own psychic stability conditional on the suffering’s resolution.

This is the distinction that the *Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra* (Diamond Sūtra) encodes in its famous formulation: the bodhisattva vows to lead all beings to liberation and simultaneously recognises that no being has been led to liberation — not because the action is insincere or the commitment hollow, but because the being who leads, the beings who are led, and the liberation to which they are led are all ultimately empty of the inherent existence that would make the action a matter of one substantial entity transforming another. The action is conventionally real; the emptiness of all participants in the action does not negate but purifies it — removes the *upādāna* that would otherwise make the action’s success or failure the condition of the acting subject’s own psychic survival.

### 8.2 — Berlant’s Impasse Reread Through Dukkha and Anicca

The encounter between Berlant’s theory of cruel optimism and the Buddhist analysis of *upādāna* and impermanence (*anicca*) produces something more than a simple theoretical alignment. It produces a **political reorientation** of the impasse that neither framework could generate alone.

Berlant’s impasse is the temporal structure of a present that cannot resolve itself through the usual means — in which the good life promise has broken down but cannot be relinquished without losing the framework of expectation that makes the present legible as a present moving toward something. The subject in the impasse manages laterally, improvises within constraints that are not changing, finds small sustainments without transcendence. This is politically honest and phenomenologically precise. It is also, the Buddhist analysis suggests, a description of *saṃsāra* — the cycle of conditioned existence — from the perspective of someone still within it.

*Dukkha* — the first of the Buddha’s Four Noble Truths — is frequently translated as “suffering” but encompasses something more structurally specific: the pervasive unsatisfactoriness inherent in all conditioned phenomena, even pleasant ones, because they are impermanent. The *dukkha* of conditioned existence is not the same as acute suffering — it is the deeper background condition of a life organised around the pursuit of conditions that cannot provide the stability and fulfilment that *upādāna* leads the subject to seek from them. Berlant’s cruel optimism is a specific macropolitical form of *dukkha*: the attachment to a good life promise that cannot deliver what the attachment seeks from it, because no conditional promise can satisfy the fundamental clinging whose resolution requires not better conditions but the recognition of their ultimate emptiness.

*Anicca* — impermanence, the second of the Three Marks of Existence — is the direct encounter with the *dukkha* structure: the recognition that all conditioned phenomena arise and pass, that nothing maintains the stability that *upādāna* attributes to it, that the good life promise will always fail to deliver permanent satisfaction because permanence is not a feature of any conditioned phenomenon. Berlant’s impasse is the political-economic instantiation of *anicca*: the moment when the impermanence of a specific configuration of promises — post-war capitalist normativity, the good life of upward mobility and domestic security — becomes undeniable.

But Berlant’s analysis, for all its political honesty, is limited in a specific way that the Buddhist framework illuminates. Berlant describes the impasse with extraordinary precision and proposes lateral agency as the form of life it generates, but the framework cannot itself offer a way **through** the impasse — only through the conditions that produce it, through structural transformation of the political-economic formations that have broken the good life promise. This is a legitimate and important political claim. But it leaves unaddressed the question of what to do in the meantime — how to sustain political engagement across the extended duration of conditions that are not yet changing, without the cruel optimism that makes the engagement’s outcome the condition of the agent’s own psychic survival.

The Buddhist framework addresses precisely this gap. The path through the impasse is not a better promise and not the structural transformation of the conditions that produce it — though that transformation is conventionally necessary and should be pursued. The path through the impasse is the **progressive loosening of the clinging** that makes the good life promise necessary in the first place — the recognition, through the cultivation of insight, that the self for whom the good life was required was never the ultimately real entity that *upādāna* treated it as being. This does not make the good life unnecessary at the conventional level — food, shelter, healthcare, legal recognition, freedom from violence are conventionally necessary and their denial constitutes real and serious harm. But it changes the relationship of the political subject to those necessities from desperate clinging to engaged non-attachment: pursuing conventional goods with full conventional commitment, without the *upādāna* that makes their failure the dissolution of the self.

The practical political difference is not negligible. A queer activist who fights for trans healthcare access from a position of *upādāna* — whose sense of self, whose political identity, whose psychological stability are conditional on the achievement of that access — will experience the impasse differently from an activist who fights from the bodhisattva orientation: with equal commitment and equal urgency, but without the secondary suffering of attachment to outcome. The first activist is vulnerable to what Berlant calls cruel optimism’s specific injury: the damage done not only by the failure to achieve the good but by the attachment to the good’s achievement as the condition of the self’s survival. The second activist is not less politically committed — they may be more so, because their engagement is not defended by the psychic self-protection that *upādāna* generates. They are differently constituted as political subjects: acting from conventional necessity without ultimate attachment to the outcome.

### 8.3 — Three Implications for the Field

The synthesis developed across this article generates three specific implications for the field that are worth stating directly, as conclusions that follow from the argument rather than as suggestions for future work.

**The first implication** is that queer theory requires a non-Western ontological supplement — but a specific kind of supplement, not the addition of non-Western cultural content to an unchanged theoretical framework. The Buddhist philosophical framework does not merely diversify queer theory’s cultural references or expand its geographical reach. It challenges the ontological ground on which the theory stands: the psychoanalytic-phenomenological substrate that produces the melancholic subject, organises it around lack, and makes passionate attachment to the conditions of its own subordination appear as the only alternative to annihilation. The supplement the Buddhist framework offers is not additive but transformative — it provides a more radical ontological analysis of the subject’s construction that neither refutes Butler’s political analysis nor leaves it unchanged.

This has methodological implications that extend beyond the specific argument about Buddhism and queer theory. It suggests that the productive engagement between queer theory and non-Western philosophical traditions requires genuine philosophical translation — attending to the specific conceptual content of those traditions, learning their philosophical vocabulary, engaging their internal debates — rather than the extraction of cultural examples or the citation of non-Western thinkers to demonstrate the field’s diversity. The kind of engagement this article has attempted with Nāgārjuna, Candrakīrti, and the Pāli canonical literature is methodologically demanding in ways that the current training of most queer theorists does not equip them for. That methodological gap is itself a political problem: it reproduces the asymmetry between Western theoretical frameworks and non-Western traditions that queer of colour critique has exposed within the Anglo-American academy’s racial formation.

**The second implication** concerns the status of Global South queer formations in the field’s theoretical architecture. The scholarship reviewed in Sections IV and V — Manalansan’s bakla, Blackwood’s tomboi, Wilson’s tom, Jackson’s kathoey — has been produced within the academy and has contributed to the field’s theoretical development in important ways. But it has remained, in the field’s dominant self-understanding, primarily empirical: case studies that demonstrate the limits of the Western framework’s universalising claims, illustrations of the diversity of queer formations, correctives to the canon’s ethnocentrism. This article has argued that these formations are not merely correctives. They are theoretical resources in their own right — sites at which the intersection of precolonial gender frameworks, colonial sedimentation, postcolonial nationalism, global capitalism, and Buddhist (or Catholic, or Islamic) cosmological frameworks produces epistemologies that the academy must learn to engage as theory rather than as data.

The methodological corollary is the one developed in Section 4.3: the academy needs apparatus for recognising vernacular theory — swardspeak, tomboi community knowledge, karmic navigation — as theory, without assimilating it to academic categories or extracting it from the practices that produce it. This is a methodological challenge that the field has not yet adequately met, and meeting it will require changes not only in how queer theorists read and cite but in how they are trained, what institutional relationships they develop with the communities whose lives and practices they engage, and what forms of accountability they accept for the representations they produce.

**The third implication** concerns the epistemological gap in queer theory’s methodological repertoire identified in Section 7.3. The field’s textualist inheritance — its commitment to the analysis of discourse as the primary site of knowledge production about the subject and its constructions — has produced indispensable scholarship. It has also produced a systematic inability to engage the forms of knowledge produced through the body in states where the discursive constitution of the subject is temporarily suspended. Buddhist meditative practice, kink subspace, Sufi fanāʾ, Christian unio mystica, and the experience of serious illness and dying are all sites at which this kind of knowledge is produced. The field’s inability to engage these sites as epistemological resources — as sites producing knowledge about the constructedness of gender and desire through the body’s own processes rather than through the analysis of discourse about the body — is not a peripheral limitation. It is a gap at the centre of the field’s self-understanding as a theory of the body.

Addressing this gap will require developing methodological frameworks adequate to phenomenological research on extreme states of embodiment: frameworks that can engage the jhāna literature, the kink phenomenology literature, the comparative mysticism literature, and the clinical narratives of illness and dying as contributions to the understanding of how bodies know their own constructedness. This is a methodological frontier, and this article does not claim to have crossed it. It claims to have named it precisely, to have shown why crossing it matters for the field’s intellectual and political adequacy, and to have suggested the traditions — Buddhist meditative phenomenology, comparative mysticism, clinical phenomenology — from which the crossing might begin.

The bodhisattva model that this section has proposed is not a solution to the political problems that queer theory addresses. Trans people are dying. Healthcare is being denied. Legal recognition is being dismantled. These are not problems that ontological reorientation resolves. They are problems that require conventional political engagement — advocacy, litigation, mutual aid, community organising, the slow work of building the social and institutional conditions within which queer and trans lives can be sustained. The bodhisattva model is a proposal about the **psychic and ontological ground** from which that conventional engagement can be conducted — a ground that does not require the passionate attachment that produces cruel optimism, does not require the anti-social refusal that aestheticises dissolution, and does not require the lateral management that accepts the impasse as permanent.

It is, in the end, an argument about what it means to care — about how political commitment can be sustained across conditions of partial failure, slow progress, and the permanent non-arrival of the utopian horizon, without either the desperate investment in outcome that breaks down into cruel optimism or the detachment that abandons those who need the commitment most. The bodhisattva vows to return — again and again, across conditions that do not change fast enough, to beings whose suffering is real at the conventional level — not because the vow will be fulfilled in any final sense, but because the vow is what compassion looks like when it is not organised around the self’s own survival.

That is the form of queer political commitment that the encounter between queer theory, Buddhist ontology, and Global South formations has produced in this article. It is partial, provisional, and aware of its own limitations in exactly the way the article’s epistemic discipline has required. It is also, the article believes, more adequate to the conditions of queer and trans life under the current political dispensation than any of the frameworks it draws on could generate alone.

## Section IX — Conclusion: Theory’s Obligation

### 9.1 — What the Encounter Produced

This article set out to ask what happens to the subject of queer theory when the psychoanalytic-phenomenological assumptions of its Anglo-American canon encounter the ontological frameworks, regional formations, and embodied practices of non-Western traditions — and what political possibilities that encounter opens. Eight sections later, an answer is available. Not a complete answer — the article has been careful throughout to distinguish what the analysis establishes from what it proposes and what it leaves genuinely open — but a specific and consequential one.

The encounter produced a queer theoretical subject that is **processual rather than melancholic**: not constituted through the irreversible incorporation of foreclosed desires and the passionate attachment to the norms that sustain it, but arising through conditions that are themselves conditioned, conventionally real and ultimately empty, capable of transformation through insight in ways that the melancholic structure forecloses. It produced a queer political framework that is **engaged rather than agonistic or anti-social**: not the endless subversive repetition that never escapes the normative order it contests, not the aestheticised embrace of the death drive that refuses the political terrain where most queer and trans people’s lives are at stake, but the sustained conventional commitment to trans healthcare, legal recognition, and the grievability of queer lives, conducted without the *upādāna* that makes the commitment’s failure the condition of the agent’s dissolution. It produced a queer epistemology that is **embodied rather than purely textual**: not limited to the analysis of discourses that constitute the body but extended to the forms of knowledge that the body produces through its own processes of transformation, in states where the discursive constitution of the subject is temporarily suspended. And it produced a queer theory whose reach is **genuinely global rather than Anglo-American with supplements**: not the universal framework with regional case studies attached, but an analysis whose conceptual resources are drawn from Pāli canonical literature and Madhyamaka philosophy alongside Butler and Berlant, from the bakla’s swardspeak alongside Sedgwick’s epistemology of the closet, from the kathoey’s karmic navigation alongside Foucault’s genealogy of sexuality.

Whether this constitutes a new queer theoretical paradigm is not a claim this article makes. What it claims is more modest and more defensible: that the encounter it has staged between Anglo-American queer theory, Madhyamaka Buddhist philosophy, and the queer formations of Southeast Asia and the Global South produces theoretical resources that are unavailable within any of those traditions considered alone, and that those resources are relevant to the most urgent political questions facing queer and trans people in the present moment.

### 9.2 — What Theory Owes

The governing ethical question — what does theory owe to the people whose lives it describes? — is not a question that can be answered abstractly, before or outside the specific theoretical work that gives it content. The answer this article’s argument implies is multiple and concrete.

Theory owes **precision about the limits of its own assumptions**. The Anglo-American canonical triad produced its most consequential errors not through carelessness but through the confident extension of frameworks beyond their actual conditions of applicability — the universalising of the homo/hetero binary, the assumption that the closet’s epistemological structure is a universal feature of sexual modernity, the implicit whiteness of the melancholic subject. The answer to these errors is not the abandonment of rigorous theoretical work but the cultivation of a specific scholarly virtue: the capacity to mark what a framework can and cannot account for, to hold its generative power and its constitutive limits simultaneously in view. This article has attempted to practise that virtue throughout, and it names it here as a debt to the communities whose formations the framework misrepresents when it overreaches.

Theory owes **attention to vernacular theoretical production**. The swardspeak lexicon, the tomboi community’s accumulated knowledge of navigating Minangkabau gender norms and Islamic propriety, the kathoey’s practical understanding of the karmic framework’s affordances and constraints, the bakla diaspora’s management of two incommensurable identity systems — these constitute theoretical production in the strict sense: systematic knowledge about how power works, where norms are enforced and where they are flexible, what forms of agency are available within constraints that cannot simply be refused. That this production does not look like academic theory — that it is vernacular, embodied, performative, and always in flux — does not make it less theoretical. It makes it differently theoretical, and the academy’s persistent failure to recognise it as such is a form of the epistemological violence that queer theory has identified at the level of sexuality without fully acknowledging at the level of knowledge production itself.

Theory owes **honesty about what it does not know**. This article has flagged at specific junctures — in its treatment of the paṇḍaka material, in its account of the *upādāna*-passionate attachment parallel, in its comparative phenomenology of altered states — what is established by textual evidence, what is theoretical inference, and what is genuine scholarly uncertainty. This is not rhetorical hedging. It is the specific form of intellectual honesty that the political stakes of the inquiry require. When Buddhist institutions claim canonical authority for positions whose textual basis is thin, or when progressive scholars claim that Buddhism is naturally queer-affirming, or when Western queer theorists claim that the homo/hetero binary is a universal feature of human sexuality — all of these overstatements do harm to the communities on whose behalf they are nominally made. The obligation to precision is also a political obligation.

But what theory owes most concretely, and most urgently, is **engagement with the lives that are at stake in the present moment** — and those lives are not only the lives that appear in the theoretical literature’s standard examples. They include the lives of patients in oncology wards whose gender identity, sexual history, and intimate relationships are navigated within healthcare systems that were not designed to see them clearly. They include the lives of trans women in Southeast Asia who navigate karmic frameworks and hostile healthcare systems and colonial legal regimes simultaneously. They include the lives of queer asylum seekers whose claims for protection require the presentation of an identity — legible, consistent, documentable — within a legal framework that has no apparatus for the formations they actually inhabit.

For all of these people, theory’s obligation is not primarily to represent them accurately, important as accuracy is. It is to be **useful** — to generate frameworks that help understand the conditions of their lives, that identify where those conditions are produced and where they might be transformed, and that sustain the political commitment required to work toward their transformation across extended conditions of partial failure and slow progress. The test of the encounter this article has staged is not whether it satisfies the criteria of academic adequacy in the relevant disciplines — though the article has tried to meet those criteria — but whether the frameworks it develops are adequate to the lives at stake.

### 9.3 — Open Questions and Future Directions

Three genuinely open questions emerge from this article’s analysis, and naming them is itself a contribution: it specifies where the field’s work needs to go, rather than leaving the sense that the argument has been completed.

The first concerns the **political landscape of Buddhist LGBT organising in Southeast Asia** in the present moment. The scholarship this article has drawn on — Jackson’s Thailand, Blackwood’s West Sumatra, Manalansan’s Filipino diaspora — was produced across the period from the late 1980s to the early 2010s. The political landscape has transformed significantly since then, particularly in the context of the intensifying global backlash against trans and LGBT rights that the current moment represents. How are the three strategies Jackson identified — karmic reinterpretation, compassion ethics, rights discourse importation — being deployed, contested, and transformed by activists in Thailand, Indonesia, and the Philippines in 2025 and 2026, in the context of both the global backlash and the specific political configurations of their national contexts? This is primarily an empirical question requiring contemporary ethnographic and political research that this article cannot provide, and its absence is a genuine limitation.

The second concerns the **phenomenological comparison of altered states**. The structural parallel between Buddhist meditative states, kink subspace, Sufi fanāʾ, and Christian unio mystica that Section VII developed is, as the article has been careful to flag, a structural inference rather than a settled finding. What the field needs — and what does not currently exist with adequate methodological rigour — is a sustained comparative phenomenological study that holds the first-person accounts of these states alongside each other with attention to both their deep structural resonances and their specific doctrinal, cultural, and experiential differences. William James’s four marks of mystical experience remain the most widely cited framework for this comparison, but they were developed from a sample that significantly overrepresents Western Protestant and Catholic experiences. A genuinely comparative phenomenological study that draws equally on Pāli meditation manuals, kink practice phenomenology, Sufi mystical literature, and Christian contemplative accounts would constitute a significant methodological contribution — and one that has direct implications for the embodied epistemology argument this article has advanced.

The third open question is the one this article has circled throughout without fully entering: the **clinical and care dimensions** of the synthesis. The observation in Section 7.3 — that seriously ill patients, particularly those with cancer, undergo experiences that are structurally resonant with the altered states that the Buddhist tradition describes, experiences in which the ordinary self-structure becomes recognisable as construction through the body’s direct encounter with its own contingency — points toward a domain where the theoretical synthesis developed in this article has its most practically urgent application and its most significant underdevelopment.

Patients facing cancer navigate a specific form of the encounter with impermanence and self-dissolution that the Buddhist tradition has theorised and the queer theory of the subject has only begun to address. They navigate gendered bodies that are being radically altered by disease and treatment — bodies that may no longer function, look, or feel as the gender performances that constitute their identities have required. They navigate the dissolution of the future-oriented framework of hope and planning that the good life promise sustains, and the specific form of cruel optimism that medical treatment generates: the attachment to the promise of recovery, the progressive failure of that promise, the psychic difficulty of relinquishing it without losing the will to continue. They navigate the specific forms of institutional recognition and misrecognition that healthcare systems impose — the ways in which queer and trans patients are seen or not seen, named or misnamed, cared for or failed by institutions whose normative assumptions are built into their physical environments, their forms, their languages, and their clinical practices.

What theory owes to these patients — and to the clinicians who attend them — is frameworks adequate to this navigation: not the false consolation of spiritual appropriation, not the political advocacy that mistakes clinical care for a policy problem, not the philosophical abstraction that loses sight of the body in pain. The encounter between queer theory, Buddhist impermanence, and the phenomenology of altered states is, this article has argued, one of the most productive sites at which such frameworks might be developed. That development has not yet occurred at the level of rigour the stakes require. This article ends by naming that as its most significant direction forward — not as a call for future scholarship in the abstract, but as a specific obligation that the argument it has made generates for the communities whose lives gave it its urgency in the first place.

Theory’s obligation to the people whose lives it describes is not discharged by describing those lives accurately, or by advocating for their political recognition, or by developing philosophical frameworks adequate to their ontological complexity — though all of these are necessary. It is discharged, finally, by remaining in proximity to those lives: by keeping the question “adequate to what?” always in view, refusing the consolation of frameworks that are elegant in the abstract and impractical in the specific, and sustaining the capacity for the form of engagement that the bodhisattva ideal encodes — returning again, to the same conditions, with the same commitment, without the passionate attachment to resolution that would make the returning conditional on its success.
