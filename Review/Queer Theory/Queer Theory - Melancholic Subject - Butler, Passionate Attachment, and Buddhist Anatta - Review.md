# Emptying the Melancholic Subject: Judith Butler’s Passionate Attachment and the Buddhist Doctrine of Anattā

## Abstract

This essay argues that the Buddhist doctrine of *anattā* (non-self) provides a more radical ontological ground for queer theory than the psychoanalytic-phenomenological frameworks Judith Butler inherits from Lacan, Hegel, and Freud. Butler’s theory of subjection, as developed in *The Psychic Life of Power* (1997), holds that the subject is constituted through passionate attachment to the norms that simultaneously form and injure it — a melancholic structure built on foreclosed identifications and ungrievable losses. This account of the subject’s psychic investment in its own subordination is politically powerful but ontologically constrained: it presupposes a subject organised around lack, incorporated loss, and the desire for recognition within a Symbolic Order whose terms it cannot refuse without risking annihilation. The Madhyamaka Buddhist analysis of selfhood — specifically the two-truths framework distinguishing conventional (*saṃvṛti-satya*) and ultimate (*paramārtha-satya*) reality — offers a way through this constraint without bypassing Butler’s political insights. At the conventional level, Butler’s account of subjection, melancholy, and the heterosexual matrix remains fully valid and politically necessary. At the ultimate level, the Madhyamaka analysis reveals the subject constituted through subjection to be empty of inherent existence (*śūnya*) — not nothing, but a conventional designation for a stream of conditioned arising without a fixed centre of lack. This is not the dissolution of political subjecthood but its reorientation: action without the passionate attachment that produces *upādāna* (clinging), the psychic mechanism shared between Butler’s subjection and the Buddhist analysis of suffering. The essay develops this conjunction through close reading of Butler’s *The Psychic Life of Power*, Nāgārjuna’s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*‘s teaching on gender emptiness, and the Yogācāra analysis of *ālayavijñāna*. It concludes by proposing that the bodhisattva ideal — compassionate political action without clinging to outcomes — offers a model for queer political engagement that resolves the tension between Butler’s necessary politics and the Buddhist recognition of the subject’s ultimate emptiness.

## 摘要（繁體中文）

本文主張，佛教「無我」（*anattā*）之教義，為酷兒理論提供了一個比朱迪斯·巴特勒所繼承之拉康、黑格爾及佛洛伊德之精神分析—現象學框架更為根本的存有論基礎。巴特勒在《權力的精神生命》（1997）中所發展的臣服理論認為，主體乃透過對規範的熱切依附而被建構——而這些規範同時形塑並傷害主體——形成一種建立於被封閉之認同與無從哀悼之失落之上的憂鬱結構。此一關於主體在自身從屬關係中進行心理投資的論述，具有強大的政治力量，但在存有論上受到制約：它預設了一個圍繞著匱乏、內攝之失落，以及在一個其條件無法被拒絕而不冒自我消滅之風險的象徵秩序中尋求承認之欲望所組織起來的主體。中觀佛教的自我分析——特別是區分世俗諦（*saṃvṛti-satya*）與勝義諦（*paramārtha-satya*）的二諦框架——提供了一條穿越此一制約的道路，而無需繞過巴特勒的政治洞見。在世俗層面上，巴特勒關於臣服、憂鬱與異性戀矩陣的論述依然完全有效且在政治上不可或缺。在勝義層面上，中觀分析揭示了透過臣服而被建構的主體本質上是空的（*śūnya*）——並非虛無，而是對一個無固定匱乏中心之緣起流的世俗稱謂。這並非政治主體性的消解，而是其重新定向：無執著之行動——免於產生「取」（*upādāna*，執取）的熱切依附，而「取」正是巴特勒之臣服與佛教苦難分析之間共同的心理機制。本文透過細讀巴特勒的《權力的精神生命》、龍樹的《中論》、《維摩詰經》關於性別空性的教義，以及唯識學對「阿賴耶識」的分析，來發展這一結合。最後提出，菩薩理想——不執著於結果的慈悲政治行動——為酷兒政治參與提供了一個模型，能夠化解巴特勒必要之政治學與佛教對主體之究竟空性之認識之間的張力。

## I. Introduction: The Paradox of Subjection

In the opening pages of *The Psychic Life of Power*, Judith Butler poses a question that cuts to the heart of any transformative politics: if the subject is formed by the very power it might resist, what is the basis of resistance? Her answer — that the subject is constituted through a passionate attachment to the norms that subordinate it, an attachment so fundamental that to relinquish the norm is to risk the dissolution of the self — is one of the most penetrating accounts of political passivity under conditions of injury in contemporary theory.

Butler writes:

> “If, following Foucault, we understand power as forming the subject as well, as providing the very condition of its existence and the trajectory of its desire, then power is not simply what we oppose but also, in a strong sense, what we depend on for our existence and what we harbor and preserve in the beings that we are.”

This is the paradox of subjection: the subject formed through power cannot simply refuse power without refusing its own existence as a subject. The political implication is both clarifying and vertiginous. It explains why subjects defend identities that harm them, why the dominated internalise the terms of domination, and why Butler’s own concept of subversive repetition — resistance through citational practice that deviates from the norm — must be so subtle, so patient, so structural rather than voluntarist.

But the paradox also encodes a philosophical limitation that this essay proposes to address. Butler’s account of the subject — melancholic, constituted through foreclosed identifications, organised around lack and the desire for recognition — is drawn from a specific psychoanalytic and phenomenological inheritance: Lacan’s subject of the signifier, Freud’s economy of libido and loss, Hegel’s dialectic of recognition. These frameworks share an ontological commitment: **the subject is real, organised around lack, and its psychic depth is constituted through the internalisation of what it has lost or been denied.**

What Buddhist philosophy, specifically the Madhyamaka analysis of selfhood developed by Nāgārjuna and refined by Candrakīrti, offers is not a refutation of this account but a more radical ontological frame within which it can be situated and, ultimately, seen through. The subject constituted through subjection is, from the Madhyamaka perspective, conventionally real — its suffering is real, its political needs are real, its resistance is real and necessary. But it is ultimately empty of the inherent self-existence that Butler’s psychoanalytic inheritance attributes to it. It is not organised around a real lack but around a *constructed* lack — a construction that can be, through specific practices of insight, gradually recognised as such.

This distinction — between conventional validity and ultimate emptiness — is the key to what this essay proposes: not the dissolution of queer political subjecthood but its reorientation, away from the passionate attachment (*upādāna*) that makes Butler’s melancholic subject structurally unable to relinquish what harms it, and toward the compassionate action without clinging that the bodhisattva ideal describes.

The argument proceeds in five stages. Section II reconstructs Butler’s account of subjection and melancholy in *The Psychic Life of Power*. Section III develops the Madhyamaka analysis of selfhood through the two-truths framework. Section IV reads the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*’s teaching on gender emptiness as the canonical Buddhist resource for queer theory. Section V develops the *upādāna*-passionate attachment conjunction and its political implications. Section VI proposes the bodhisattva model as a resolution to the political-dissolution tension the conjunction generates.

## II. Butler’s Melancholic Subject: Subjection, Foreclosure, and Passionate Attachment

### The Reflexive Turn of Power

Butler’s account of subjection is organised around what she calls “a figure of turning” — power operates on the subject, and the subject turns back toward the power that operates on it, taking up its own subjection as the condition of its existence. This reflexive turn is not chosen; it is the structure of subjecthood itself under conditions of normative power.

The mechanism Butler identifies is **passionate attachment** — the subject’s investment in the norms that constitute it, an investment so fundamental that it precedes any particular choice or desire:

> “The subject… is compelled to repeat the norms by which it is produced, but this repetition establishes the temporality of the norm, not as a mechanical repetition, but as a compelled return.”

This attachment is not ideological mystification — it is not that the subject has been deceived about its interests. It is constitutive: the subject would not exist without the norms to which it is attached, and to refuse them entirely is to risk falling outside the order of recognition and therefore outside existence as a subject.

This explains a phenomenon that purely structural accounts of power cannot: **why subjects actively sustain the conditions of their own subordination**. The answer Butler gives is psychic rather than political: because those conditions are the condition of existing as a subject at all.

### Melancholy and Gender: The Foreclosure of Same-Sex Desire

Butler’s most powerful and most provocative argument in the book concerns **gender identity as melancholic structure**. Drawing on Freud’s “Mourning and Melancholia” (1917), she argues that the normative gender identities produced by compulsory heterosexuality are built on incorporated, ungrievable losses.

In Freud’s account, melancholia arises when a love object is lost but cannot be mourned — instead of withdrawing libido from the lost object and directing it toward new attachments (the work of mourning), the melancholic subject incorporates the lost object, taking it into the ego, identifying with it. The object is not lost but preserved within the self, and the ambivalence toward it — love and hostility — is turned inward: “the shadow of the object fell upon the ego.”

Butler’s application: compulsory heterosexuality requires not merely the renunciation of same-sex attachments but their **foreclosure** — they must be denied the status of attachments that could be mourned. Unlike heterosexual object-loss (which can be grieved and replaced), homosexual attachment must never have been had. It is foreclosed before it can be formed as a recognisable desire.

The result is that **normative heterosexual gender identity is constituted by the melancholic incorporation of foreclosed same-sex desire**:

> “The straight man becomes his male lover through the incorporation of the lost male object.”

Masculine heterosexual identity is built on the ungrievable loss of the same-sex desire that was never permitted. The grief that cannot be expressed is turned inward, and the lost object — the male lover who was never had — becomes part of the ego’s structure.

This is Butler’s most theoretically radical claim and the one most directly relevant to the present argument. **The normative subject and the queer subject are not simply opposed.** The normative subject is *constituted by* the very queerness it forecloses. Homophobia is not merely prejudice — it is **psychic self-defence** by a subject whose identity is built on the incorporated loss of the homosexuality it denies.

### The Desire for Recognition and the Althusserian Scene

Butler’s reading of Althusser’s interpellation scene illuminates a further dimension of passionate attachment: the **desire for recognition** as the force that makes subjection not merely suffered but actively embraced.

When a police officer calls “Hey, you!” and the individual turns, they become a subject through that turning — they recognise themselves as the one addressed, take up the subject position the hail assigns. Butler’s question: why does the subject turn, even when the hail is accusatory?

Her answer: because **being hailed means existing**. The desire for social existence — for recognition as a subject within the social order — is more powerful than the desire for innocence, for non-subjection, or for freedom from the norm. To refuse to turn is to risk falling outside the order of address, to cease to exist as a recognisable subject.

> “The subject does not want to fall outside the law even if it pays the price of guilt for that desire.”

This is the deepest structure of passionate attachment: it is not attachment to a particular norm or a particular form of recognition but to **the condition of being recognised at all**. The subject clings to its subjection because subjection is the condition of its social existence.

## III. The Madhyamaka Analysis: Two Truths and the Empty Subject

### Anattā and Its Precise Claim

The Buddhist doctrine of anattā (Pāli) / anātman (Sanskrit) is frequently misread as a claim that the self does not exist — a nihilism that the Buddha explicitly rejected. The precise claim of the doctrine is more subtle and more philosophically demanding:

The self has **conventional reality** (*saṃvṛti-satya*) but lacks **ultimate reality** (*paramārtha-satya*). In the Pāli *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*, the Buddha examines each of the five aggregates (*khandhas*) that comprise what we conventionally call a person — form (*rūpa*), feeling (*vedanā*), perception (*saññā*), mental formations (*saṅkhāra*), and consciousness (*viññāṇa*) — and demonstrates that none of them is the self:

> “Form is not self. If form were self, then form would not lead to affliction, and one could have it of form: ‘Let my form be thus, let my form be not thus.’ But because form is not self, form leads to affliction, and none can have it of form: ‘Let my form be thus, let my form be not thus.’”

The argument is logical and phenomenological simultaneously: examine each component of what we call the self and you will find that none of them is permanent, none of them is fully under our control, none of them is the essential “I” that we imagine ourselves to be. But the aggregates are not nothing — they arise, they function, they produce experience and action. They are conventionally real. What they lack is inherent, independent, self-standing existence.

### Nāgārjuna’s Śūnyatā: Emptiness of Inherent Existence

Nāgārjuna’s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (2nd century CE) extends the anattā analysis through the doctrine of *śūnyatā* (emptiness). Not only the self but all phenomena are empty of *svabhāva* — inherent self-existence. They arise through *pratītyasamutpāda* (dependent origination): they exist only in relation to other phenomena, never through their own intrinsic nature.

The critical passage is Chapter 24, verse 18, the definition of dependent origination as emptiness:

> *yat pratītyasamutpannaṃ śūnyatāṃ tāṃ pracakṣmahe* /
> *sā prajñaptir upādāya pratipat saiva madhyamā* //

“Whatever arises dependently, that we call emptiness. That is a dependent designation, and that itself is the middle way.”

Three things are identified as equivalent: **dependent origination**, **emptiness**, and **conventional designation** (*prajñapti*). What exists dependently exists as a conventional designation — a name applied to a relational process that has no independent existence. This is not nihilism (the thing does not exist) but a positive analysis of how things exist: dependently, relationally, without inherent self-standing being.

The two-truths framework is Nāgārjuna’s resolution of the apparent paradox: how can things be empty of inherent existence and yet function, cause effects, be the basis of ethical and political action?

> *dve satye samupāśritya buddhānāṃ dharmadeśanā* /
> *lokasaṃvṛtisatyaṃ ca satyaṃ ca paramārthataḥ* //

“The teaching of the Buddhas is based on two truths: the conventional truth of the world and the ultimate truth.” (MK 24.8)

At the conventional level, persons exist, actions have consequences, political institutions are real, and suffering requires response. At the ultimate level, those persons, actions, and institutions are empty of inherent existence — they arise dependently and have no fixed, self-standing nature.

### The Empty Subject and Butler’s Melancholic Subject

The Madhyamaka analysis of the subject provides an ontological frame that is more radical than Butler’s psychoanalytic account in a specific sense: it does not merely deconstruct the naturalness of the subject (showing it is constructed rather than given) but analyses the nature of that construction as empty of inherent existence.

Butler’s subject is constructed through citational practice, melancholic incorporation, and passionate attachment. This construction is real — conventionally real, in Madhyamaka terms. The heterosexual matrix exists; the foreclosure of same-sex desire occurs; the melancholic structure of normative gender is produced. These are conventional facts with real consequences.

But the subject thus constructed lacks ultimate reality. It is a conventional designation for a stream of conditioned arising — karma, in the Buddhist framework — without a fixed centre of lack around which it is organised. Butler’s subject is *organised around lack* (the Lacanian barred subject, the foreclosed desire, the lost object incorporated in melancholy). The Buddhist subject is not organised around lack but around **dependent arising** — it arises in relation to conditions, and its apparent solidity, including its apparent depth of psychic interiority, is a conventionally real but ultimately empty construction.

This difference matters politically. Butler’s subject *must* cling to the norms that constitute it because its existence as a subject depends on that clinging. The Buddhist analysis shows that this clinging — *upādāna* — is itself a conditioned arising, not an ontological necessity. It arises from *taṇhā* (craving) and can, through specific practices of insight, be seen through without the annihilation of the subject. What is annihilated is not the subject but the *misrecognition* of the subject as having inherent, independent existence — the misrecognition that makes passionate attachment feel like the only alternative to non-existence.

## IV. The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* and the Emptiness of Gender

### The Goddess and Śāriputra: A Canonical Teaching on Gender Emptiness

The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*, one of the most celebrated texts in the Mahāyāna Buddhist canon, contains in its seventh chapter (*“Observing Living Beings”*) the most philosophically direct Buddhist teaching on the emptiness of gender. The scene involves a goddess who has long inhabited Vimalakīrti’s room and the monk Śāriputra, one of the Buddha’s chief disciples and a master of the Abhidharma.

The goddess demonstrates her understanding of emptiness through a direct intervention in Śāriputra’s embodiment: she transforms him into a woman and herself into his male form, then asks:

> “Śāriputra, what prevents you from changing out of this female body?”

When Śāriputra replies that he does not know how to change it, the goddess continues:

> “If Śāriputra could change out of this female body, then all women could change likewise. Just as Śāriputra is not really a woman but appears in a woman’s form, all women appear in women’s forms but are not really women. Therefore, the Buddha said, ‘All phenomena are neither male nor female.’”

The argument is precise and philosophically consequential. The goddess does not argue that gender is culturally constructed in Butler’s sense — that there is no prediscursive body whose gender is then expressed. She argues that gender is **ultimately empty** — that the designation “female body” refers to no inherently female nature, that the appearance of gender is a conventional designation applied to an aggregation of phenomena that has no fixed gender essence.

This is not Butler’s deconstruction of the natural through the performance. It is the Madhyamaka analysis of the emptiness of the conventional through insight into dependent origination. Butler shows that gender has no original — performance is all the way down. The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* shows that gender has no inherent reality — conventional designation is all the way down, and conventional designations are empty of self-existence.

### The Political Difference

The political implications of this difference are significant. Butler’s deconstruction of gender’s naturalness is an **epistemological operation** — it reveals what was naturalised as constructed, thereby opening space for political contestation. The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*’s teaching is an **ontological operation** — it reveals what was conventionally designated as lacking ultimate reality, thereby opening space for a different relationship to convention itself.

The epistemological operation requires continued engagement: you must keep deconstructing, keep subverting, keep repeating with deviation, because the normative order keeps renaturalising itself. The ontological operation does not require continued deconstruction but a **change of relationship to the conventional order** — from attachment (treating conventions as ultimately real, defending them as if the self depended on them) to engagement without clinging (acting within conventions while recognising their ultimate emptiness).

This is the difference between Butler’s agonistic politics and the Buddhist post-agonistic orientation — though crucially, post-agonistic does not mean politically passive. The bodhisattva acts, and acts urgently, in the conventional world. But acts without the passionate attachment that makes Butler’s melancholic subject structurally unable to relinquish what harms it.

## V. *Upādāna* and Passionate Attachment: The Shared Mechanism

### The Twelve Links of Dependent Origination

The Buddhist analysis of *upādāna* (clinging, grasping, appropriation) occurs within the framework of the twelve links of dependent origination (*dvādaśāṅga pratītyasamutpāda*) — the causal chain that produces the cycle of suffering:

> *avijjā → saṅkhāra → viññāṇa → nāmarūpa → saḷāyatana → phassa → vedanā → taṇhā → **upādāna** → bhava → jāti → jarāmaraṇa*

Ignorance (*avijjā*) → formations (*saṅkhāra*) → consciousness → name-and-form → six sense bases → contact → feeling → **craving (*taṇhā*)** → **clinging (*upādāna*)** → becoming (*bhava*) → birth (*jāti*) → aging-and-death.

*Upādāna* arises from *taṇhā* (craving) and leads to *bhava* (becoming) — the compulsion to maintain and reproduce a particular form of existence. It is the mechanism by which craving becomes the constitution of a particular kind of subject, perpetuating itself across time.

The Pāli analysis identifies four types of clinging:

1. *Kāmupādāna* — clinging to sensual pleasures
2. *Diṭṭhupādāna* — clinging to views and ideologies
3. *Sīlabbatupādāna* — clinging to rules and rituals
4. *Attavādupādāna* — **clinging to a doctrine of self**

The fourth type is the most fundamental: the clinging to the belief that there is a self — a substantial, permanent, independent entity — that must be protected, maintained, and recognised. This clinging is the psychological equivalent of what Butler calls passionate attachment to the norms that constitute the subject.

### The Structural Parallel

Butler’s passionate attachment and Buddhist *upādāna* are not identical concepts. They arise from different theoretical frameworks, address different aspects of human experience, and carry different philosophical commitments. But they are **structurally parallel** in a way that is theoretically productive:

Both describe a mechanism by which subjects maintain the conditions of their own suffering through attachment to what constitutes them. In Butler, the subject clings to the norms of the heterosexual matrix because those norms are the condition of its existence as a recognisable subject — to release the attachment is experienced as annihilation. In Buddhist analysis, the subject clings to its self-view (*attavāda*) because the sense of being a self is the organising principle of all experience — to release the clinging is experienced as annihilation.

Both accounts identify this attachment as **the mechanism of reproduction** — it is how the normative order (Butler) and the cycle of suffering (Buddhism) perpetuate themselves. And both locate the possibility of transformation not in a subject freed from the attachment but in a changed *relationship* to the attachment — for Butler, through subversive repetition that exposes the contingency of the norm; for Buddhism, through insight (*vipassanā*) that sees the constructed nature of the self to which one clings.

The crucial difference: Butler’s account cannot provide a path through the attachment without risking the subject itself, because in her framework the subject *is* the attachment — it is constituted by and through the passionate attachment to the norms that form it. The Buddhist framework provides a path: *upādāna* is a conditioned arising, not an ontological necessity. It can be seen through, progressively loosened, without the annihilation of the conventional subject — because the conventional subject was never the ultimately real entity that passionate attachment treats it as being.

## VI. The Bodhisattva Model: Compassionate Action Without Clinging

### The Political-Dissolution Tension

The preceding argument creates an apparent political problem. If the path through passionate attachment involves recognising the ultimate emptiness of the self — and therefore of the identity categories that political action requires — does this dissolve the basis for queer political mobilisation?

The Mahāyāna response to this tension is precisely what the two-truths framework was designed to address. Nāgārjuna’s Chapter 24 of the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* anticipates the objection directly:

> *śūnyatāṃ prati te doṣā nāsmāsu prasajante* /
> *vyavahāraṃ nāśritya paramārtho na deśyate* //

“The faults you attribute to emptiness do not apply to us. Without resorting to convention, the ultimate cannot be taught.” (MK 24.10)

Emptiness does not dissolve the conventional. It is **disclosed through the conventional** and does not negate it. The political world of trans rights, healthcare access, anti-discrimination legislation, and public mourning is conventionally real. Its importance is not reduced by the recognition of ultimate emptiness — if anything, the compassion that arises from that recognition increases the urgency of conventional political engagement, because suffering is real at the conventional level and calls for response.

### The Bodhisattva: Acting Without Clinging

The **bodhisattva ideal** in Mahāyāna Buddhism provides the model for how this engagement looks: the bodhisattva vows to work for the liberation of all sentient beings and acts with complete commitment to that vow — while recognising that the beings to be liberated are ultimately empty, that the liberation being sought is ultimately empty, and that the bodhisattva who vows is ultimately empty.

This is not the cynicism of the detached observer. It is the **compassion (*karuṇā*) that arises precisely from the recognition of emptiness** — because the recognition that all beings are empty of inherent existence is simultaneously the recognition that all beings’ suffering is real at the conventional level and calls for response. The bodhisattva does not act less urgently because of emptiness recognition; they act without the clinging that generates the secondary suffering of attachment to outcomes.

The *Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra* (Diamond Sūtra) gives the classical formulation:

> *bodhisattvena mahāsattvena sarvasattvāḥ… parinirvāpayitavyāḥ… na ca kaścit sattvaḥ parinirvāpito bhavati*

“By the bodhisattva, all beings are to be led to nirvāṇa… and yet no being whatsoever has been led to nirvāṇa.”

The action is real. The outcome is sought. And the recognition of ultimate emptiness does not diminish but purifies the action — removing the passionate attachment to outcome that generates the secondary suffering of disappointment, the cruel optimism of attachment to a good life promise that the structure of *upādāna* makes structurally unavailable.

### The Resolution of Berlant’s Impasse

The conjunction of Butler and the Buddhist framework also offers a resolution to Berlant’s impasse — the temporal structure in which the good life promise has broken down but cannot be relinquished without losing the framework of hope that makes life liveable.

Berlant’s impasse is, in Buddhist terms, the structure of *dukkha* — the unsatisfactoriness inherent in all conditioned phenomena, including the good life promise. The attachment to the promise is *upādāna*. The breaking down of the promise is the conventional experience of impermanence (*anicca*). The inability to relinquish the promise without losing the framework of hope is the structure of attachment to self-view (*attavādupādāna*) — the sense that who I am is inseparable from the life I expected to have.

The Buddhist response is not to counsel despair or to endorse the promise. It is to offer a different relationship to conventional life — engagement without the *upādāna* that makes the conventional structure of promises the condition of one’s existence. This is not Edelman’s anti-futurity (refuse the promise, embrace the death drive) or Muñoz’s concrete utopia (maintain the horizon of a different promise). It is **action in the present, without the cruel optimism of attachment to outcome** — the equanimity that comes not from detachment but from the recognition that the self to whom things must happen is ultimately empty of the inherent existence that makes its need for the good life feel absolute.

## VII. Conclusion: Toward a Buddhist Queer Politics

This essay has argued for a conjunction of Butler’s psychoanalytic-political analysis and the Madhyamaka Buddhist analysis of selfhood that advances both frameworks beyond their current limits. The argument can be summarised in four theses:

**First**, Butler’s account of subjection and passionate attachment is politically indispensable. The melancholic structure of normative gender identity, the foreclosure of same-sex desire, the desire for recognition that makes the dominated actively sustain the conditions of their subordination — these analyses are correct at the conventional level and necessary for any adequate queer politics.

**Second**, Butler’s account is ontologically constrained by its psychoanalytic inheritance. The subject organised around lack, the melancholic incorporation of foreclosed desire, the passionate attachment that cannot be relinquished without risking annihilation — these presuppose a subject with more inherent existence than the Buddhist analysis can sustain.

**Third**, the Madhyamaka two-truths framework provides the ontological reorientation Butler’s politics requires. At the conventional level, all Butler’s analyses hold. At the ultimate level, the subject constituted through subjection is empty of inherent self-existence — it is a conventional designation for a stream of conditioned arising organised by *upādāna* rather than by lack, and *upādāna* is a conditioned arising that can be progressively loosened through insight without the annihilation of the conventional subject.

**Fourth**, the bodhisattva ideal provides the model for what queer politics looks like when conducted from this reoriented ontological ground: compassionate, urgent, committed to the liberation of all beings from the conventional suffering produced by the heterosexual matrix, the trans exclusionary state, and the clinical regimes that debilitate — and conducted without the passionate attachment to outcome that produces the cruel optimism, the melancholic paralysis, and the psychic investment in the conditions of one’s own subordination that Butler’s analysis so rigorously describes.

A queer politics adequate to the present moment — in which trans lives are endangered, healthcare is systematically denied, and the legal infrastructure of recognition is being dismantled — requires both the full force of Butler’s political analysis and the ontological reorientation that the Buddhist framework provides. It requires subjects who can act with complete commitment to the liberation of those who are suffering, without the *upādāna* that makes that action’s failure into the dissolution of the self that was acting.

The goddess in the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* has been in Vimalakīrti’s room for twelve years looking for the innate characteristics of the female sex and has not found them. This is not the end of politics. It is the beginning of a different kind: action without essence-attachment, engagement without inherent-existence-clinging, compassion without the cruel optimism of the melancholic subject who cannot let go of what constitutes it because what constitutes it is all it has.
