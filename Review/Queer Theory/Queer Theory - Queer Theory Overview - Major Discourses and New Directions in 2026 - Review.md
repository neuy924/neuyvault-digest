# Abstract

Queer theory in 2026 is best understood not as a unified school or a single dominant debate, but as a field of overlapping pressure zones. This review article maps the major discourse clusters currently shaping the field and argues that queer theory’s center of gravity has shifted from a relatively self-confident anti-normative critique toward a broader engagement with embodiment, backlash, governance, and survival. The article begins by revisiting anti-normativity as queer theory’s oldest live inheritance, while showing how recent scholarship has questioned its adequacy as a complete political and methodological program. It then examines the increasingly central relationship between queer theory and trans studies, focusing on how embodiment, recognition, and administrative life have become indispensable to contemporary analysis. From there, the review turns to anti-gender politics as a transnational formation, arguing that queer theory now has to explain not only norms but organized reaction, movement infrastructure, and political-economic convergence. The article next considers visibility, homonormativity, homonationalism, pinkwashing, and anti-imperial critique, emphasizing how queer inclusion can be selective, instrumentalized, and geopolitically mobile. It then analyzes the growing importance of governance, surveillance, and data infrastructures, showing how sexuality and gender are increasingly shaped by technical systems of classification, inference, moderation, and regulation. Finally, it turns to survival, care, futurity, and ecological/infrastructural precarity, arguing that queer theory’s recent mood reflects a move from transgression alone toward the problem of sustaining queer life under damaged conditions. Across these sections, the article contends that queer theory has not abandoned its canonical concerns so much as repositioned them under pressure. What defines the field in 2026 is not a new master concept, but the need to think anti-normativity, embodiment, backlash, incorporation, governance, and survival together.

# Section 1. Introduction: Why map queer theory in 2026?

Queer theory in 2026 is not best described as a unified school, a settled canon, or a single controversy unfolding under one name. It is better understood as a **field of overlapping disputes** whose objects, scales, and moods have shifted. The older image of queer theory—as primarily a project of anti-identitarian critique, anti-normativity, and textual or discursive destabilization—still matters, but it no longer exhausts the field. Recent framing essays suggest a more pressured landscape: one asks directly, “What makes theory queer? Why care?” while another locates queer studies “in and from the ruins,” signaling a discipline that is self-reflexive about its inheritance and unusually attentive to damaged institutions, political backlash, and conditions of survival.

That change in mood is not merely stylistic. It reflects a substantive reorganization of the field’s concerns. Queer theory still interrogates normativity, identity, and the production of intelligible life, but it now does so in a context shaped by anti-gender mobilization, renewed state hostility, platform governance, algorithmic classification, global rights conflict, and intensified struggles over embodiment and social reproduction. The question is no longer only how queer subjects resist norms; it is also how they are governed, counted, represented, protected, erased, monetized, and made to survive. Recent work on anti-normativity, anti-gender politics, sexual datafication, queer/trans embodiment, and queer survival all point toward this broader reconfiguration.

This article begins from that premise. Its aim is not to announce a new master concept that supersedes the old canon. Nor is it to treat “queer theory in 2026” as a clean break from the 1990s and 2000s. Rather, the task is to **map the current discourse structure of the field**: to identify the major clusters of argument, to clarify where they overlap and diverge, and to show what has become newly salient in the mid-2020s. The argument advanced here is that queer theory is presently organized less by a single theoretical center than by a series of pressure zones: anti-normativity and its limits; queer/trans relations; anti-gender backlash; homonormativity, homonationalism, and anti-imperial critique; governance, surveillance, and data infrastructures; and a growing concern with care, endurance, and survival.

The value of such a map is partly descriptive, but not only descriptive. Field-mapping is itself an intervention. To decide what counts as a “major discourse” is already to make a claim about the field’s present center of gravity. In earlier moments, queer theory could plausibly be narrated through a smaller cluster of canonical problems: performativity, anti-essentialism, heteronormativity, the closet, and the relation between sexuality and modernity. Those remain indispensable points of reference. But if one were to organize a serious account of the field in 2026 around those terms alone, one would miss how strongly current work is shaped by backlash, governance, datafication, racialized geopolitics, and the problem of living under compromised institutional conditions. The recent literature does not merely add these as topical supplements; it increasingly treats them as constitutive of what queer thought now has to explain.

A further reason for remapping the field is that the term **“queer theory” itself has become unstable in scope**. In some usages, it refers narrowly to the literary-theoretical and philosophical tradition associated with anti-normativity, performativity, and deconstruction. In others, it has widened into something closer to “queer studies,” encompassing sociology, anthropology, trans studies, media studies, science and technology studies, legal theory, political theory, critical race studies, and decolonial critique. The instability is not a defect; it is one of the field’s conditions. But it means that any contemporary review has to make its scale explicit. This article adopts a deliberately mid-range understanding. It is centered on theory, but it treats theory as inseparable from the interdisciplinary conversations through which queer concepts now travel and mutate. That is why it takes recent work on data infrastructures, anti-gender politics, and survival as part of the field’s active conceptual life rather than as merely external applications.

At stake, then, is not just a catalog of topics but a shift in **what queer theory is for**. In its more canonical self-understandings, queer theory often imagined itself as a critical practice of denaturalization: exposing the contingency of sexual and gender norms, undoing the stability of identity, and resisting the closure of social intelligibility. Those tasks remain vital. Yet the current moment has made clear that denaturalization alone does not tell us enough about how queer and trans life is administered, how anti-gender campaigns circulate transnationally, how visibility becomes marketized or securitized, or how subjects navigate worlds in which recognition may be both necessary and dangerous. The field is thus not abandoning critique; it is redistributing critique toward institutions, infrastructures, and conditions of endurance.

The sections that follow proceed from this diagnosis. They do not propose a single chronology in which one discourse replaces another. Instead, they treat the present as layered. Anti-normativity continues to structure the field’s self-image even as it is questioned. Trans studies does not replace queer theory but reorients it toward embodiment and administration. Anti-gender backlash does not sit outside the field as mere context; it becomes one of the objects through which the field now understands itself politically. Homonormativity and anti-imperial critique continue to unsettle liberal narratives of inclusion. Datafication and governance extend older concerns with normativity into infrastructural domains. Survival, care, and futurity recast the field’s affective and ethical horizon. What emerges is not a tidy paradigm shift but a dense argumentative ecology.

The remainder of this introduction clarifies the article’s present-oriented problem, its relation to earlier canonical work, and the method by which the discourse clusters are selected and read.

## 1.1 The problem of the present

To ask why queer theory should be mapped in 2026 is really to ask what is distinctive about the present conjuncture. The answer is not that the foundational archive has become obsolete. It is that older conceptual resources are now being tested under altered conditions. A field once closely identified with anti-identitarian critique and the deconstruction of normativity is now operating amid intensifying anti-gender campaigns, polarized conflicts over rights and recognition, platform-mediated sexual life, and widespread concern with institutional breakdown, precarity, and survivability. The present is therefore not just “new.” It is a moment in which inherited queer concepts are being asked to explain objects that are at once more administrative, more geopolitical, and more infrastructural than before.

This is why recent field-framing texts matter so much. When scholars ask “What makes theory queer? Why care?” they are not merely rehearsing definitional niceties. They are marking a pressure point: if queer theory once took its critical distinctiveness for granted, that distinctiveness now has to be argued for under contemporary conditions. Likewise, to speak of queer studies “in and from the ruins” is to reject any easy narrative of linear progress. The present appears instead as a terrain of damaged promises, intensified management, and contested futures.

In that sense, the “problem of the present” is double. It is an external political problem—backlash, governance, reaction, precarity—but also an internal disciplinary problem. The field must decide whether its inherited methods are still adequate, whether its key distinctions remain useful, and whether its objects have shifted enough to require new conceptual emphasis.

## 1.2 From canon to current discourse

This review does not attempt a total history of queer theory from its late twentieth-century formation onward. Instead, it works by placing the canonical inheritance in dynamic relation to live discourse clusters. The canon remains indispensable because current arguments still rely on it, revise it, or resist it. Anti-normativity, performativity, critique of identity, and suspicion of liberal inclusion remain part of the field’s grammar. But those inheritances are now refracted through newer debates over trans embodiment, anti-gender mobilization, homonationalism, data infrastructures, and survival.

The article therefore moves neither chronologically nor purely conceptually. It proceeds by clusters of argument in which older and newer texts coexist. This is important because the field’s most active debates are rarely generated by recent work alone; they are produced through the reactivation of older concepts under changed conditions. One might say that queer theory in 2026 is not post-canonical so much as **canonically unsettled**.

## 1.3 Method and scope

Methodologically, this is a **thematic review essay**. It identifies major discourse clusters on the basis of three criteria: first, recurrence across recent field-framing and topic-specific scholarship; second, conceptual centrality to current debates about the meaning and future of queer theory; and third, capacity to connect canonical inheritances with 2024–2026 developments. The aim is not exhaustive coverage of every subfield but analytical usefulness. The question is not “what has been published?” in the aggregate; it is “which clusters best organize the live argumentative structure of the field now?”

The review is also selective in scale. It focuses on Anglophone and theory-adjacent scholarship while repeatedly noting that one of the field’s major tensions concerns precisely the limits of North Atlantic framing. That tension is not solved here, but it is treated as constitutive, especially in the sections on anti-imperial critique and global queer politics. Finally, this article treats “queer theory” and “queer studies” as partially overlapping rather than strictly separable domains. That overlap is necessary if one wants to understand why governance, data, and survival now appear not as peripheral applications, but as central conceptual sites.

With that framing in place, the next section turns to the oldest live core of the field: anti-normativity, and the renewed question of what still makes theory queer.

# Section 2. Anti-normativity and the question of what still makes theory “queer”

If one were forced to name the single most enduring inheritance of queer theory, the strongest candidate would still be **anti-normativity**. By this I do not mean only opposition to heterosexual normativity in the narrow sense, nor merely refusal of fixed sexual identities. Anti-normativity names a broader critical disposition: suspicion toward the social, institutional, and epistemic processes by which some forms of life become natural, legitimate, and desirable while others are rendered deviant, immature, excessive, impossible, or disposable. Even where queer theory has changed object, scale, and method, this disposition still structures the field’s self-understanding. Recent work in queer criminology states the point plainly, beginning “with the premise that queer theory is primarily committed to anti-normativity.” The fact that this premise now has to be restated is itself revealing. Anti-normativity remains central, but it is central in a newly reflective way.

The historical force of anti-normativity lay in its ability to dislodge common assumptions about identity, social order, and the good life. It refused to treat gender and sexual categories as transparent descriptions of pre-given realities. It examined how such categories were produced through discourse, law, medicine, culture, and administration, and it drew attention to the coercive work done by apparently benign terms such as normal, mature, healthy, relational, or stable. In this sense, anti-normativity was never simply rebellious style. It was an epistemological and political wager: that the norm does not merely regulate life from outside, but shapes what counts as a life in the first place. That wager remains foundational to how the field thinks, even as the contemporary archive increasingly asks whether anti-normativity is enough.

What is striking in 2025–2026 is not the disappearance of anti-normativity but its repositioning. The question “What makes theory queer? Why care?” is symptomatic because it indicates that the field can no longer assume its defining commitments are self-evident. The anti-normative inheritance is still there, but it now appears less as a triumphant essence than as a contested method whose scope, adequacy, and distinctiveness must be argued for anew. One reason is simple success: anti-essentialism, performativity, critique of identity, and suspicion of normality have traveled widely beyond the older queer-theory canon. Another reason is political pressure: amid anti-gender backlash, administrative capture, surveillance, and struggles over embodiment and care, the field now faces problems that are not solved by denaturalization alone. Anti-normativity therefore persists, but under conditions of extension, dilution, and challenge.

This section argues that anti-normativity remains the oldest live core of queer theory, but that its contemporary status is best understood through a threefold movement. First, it survives as the field’s deepest methodological inheritance. Second, it has been criticized for abstraction, repetition, and political insufficiency. Third, it is being reworked in a present marked by backlash, governance, and survival rather than by the earlier confidence that critique alone might destabilize the social order. The question is thus not whether anti-normativity is over. The question is what anti-normativity can still do, and what it cannot do by itself.

| Social order | Norms naturalize domination by appearing neutral | Backlash and governance now require analysis of institutions and infrastructures, not only norms |
| Politics | Critique disrupts settled categories and opens alternative lives | Critique alone may not address care, survival, or organized reaction |

## 2.1 The anti-normative inheritance

To understand why anti-normativity still matters, one has to resist reducing it to a slogan. In its strongest form, anti-normativity is not simply dislike of rules or celebration of difference for its own sake. It is a mode of analysis that asks how norms become common sense, how they attach themselves to institutions and affects, and how they shape the field of intelligible personhood. The critical target is not only overt prohibition but the quieter authority of the ordinary: the family form that seems natural, the developmental sequence that appears self-evident, the public comportment that reads as mature, the sexual style that becomes respectable, the relationship model that gets counted as emotionally real.

This is why anti-normativity was so generative for queer theory’s formative archive. It allowed scholars to move beyond straightforward claims for inclusion and ask a sharper set of questions. Inclusion into what? On what terms? At what cost? What if the very institutions into which excluded subjects sought admission were themselves structured by disciplinary norms that could not simply be occupied innocently? That critical posture linked queer theory to feminism, psychoanalysis, deconstruction, Foucauldian genealogy, and later to queer of color critique, even when those traditions disagreed on objects and methods. Anti-normativity thus served as a kind of conceptual grammar through which otherwise divergent projects recognized one another as part of a shared intellectual formation.

Recent scholarship shows that this inheritance is still active. The 2025 queer criminology article that opens from anti-normativity is important not because it says something wholly new, but because it demonstrates that the commitment continues to organize contemporary work outside the older literary-theoretical core. At the same time, the symposium framing “What makes theory queer? Why care?” suggests that this inheritance now requires explicit defense and clarification. Anti-normativity is no longer simply presumed; it is itself an object of reflection.

One consequence of this inheritance is that queer theory has long been skeptical of political settlement. Whenever a regime of recognition begins to look stable, queer critique tends to ask what exclusions or simplifications make that settlement possible. This helps explain the field’s unease with liberal narratives of progress, its wariness toward domesticated forms of inclusion, and its recurrent insistence that normality is not the neutral opposite of oppression but one of its principal vehicles. Even where queer theory’s objects have changed—from textuality to institutions, from subculture to governance—the anti-normative disposition continues to animate its suspicion toward easy resolutions.

Yet the inheritance is not singular. There have always been multiple anti-normativities. Some are primarily deconstructive, concerned with exposing contradiction and contingency. Others are more affective, attentive to shame, attachment, and ordinary discomfort. Still others are materialist, interested in how norms organize labor, race, kinship, and state power. This multiplicity matters because some current criticisms of anti-normativity target only one of these strands, usually the most abstractly negative or formally anti-identitarian version. A more careful account recognizes that anti-normativity has never been one pure thing.

## 2.2 Critiques of anti-normativity’s limits

The present pressure on anti-normativity comes from several directions at once. The most familiar critique is that anti-normativity can become **too abstract**. If the norm is treated mainly as an epistemic problem or as a symbolic structure to be unsettled, then the analysis may say too little about institutions, infrastructures, or the lived demands of care and administration. One can destabilize identity categories all day and still not explain how health systems classify people, how schools manage gender, how states police migration, or how queer and trans subjects secure housing, safety, treatment, or collective continuity. This is not an argument against critique. It is an argument that critique can become politically thin when it remains too formally attached to negation.

A second critique is that anti-normativity can become **self-repeating**. Once denaturalization becomes the expected gesture, it risks hardening into an academic style. Categories are shown to be contingent, norms are exposed as historically produced, identities are revealed as unstable—and yet the operation no longer surprises, because it has become the field’s default posture. The question raised by “What makes theory queer? Why care?” can thus be read partly as a response to routinization. If critique becomes predictable, what still gives it force?

A third critique is more political. Anti-normativity can understate the fact that many subordinated groups need recognition, categories, and institutional legibility in order to survive. This issue becomes especially sharp in relation to trans studies, disability, health policy, anti-discrimination law, and demographic data. Here the norm is not always experienced only as repression; sometimes the problem is exclusion from systems that allocate care, rights, and resources. In such cases, a theory that treats all normalization as equally suspect may fail to distinguish between violent regulation and necessary forms of social support. The contemporary field does not resolve this tension, but it increasingly foregrounds it.

This is one reason anti-normativity now often appears less as a destination than as a **critical check** on other projects. It can still ask indispensable questions of recognition politics, but it no longer automatically settles them. When queer theory confronts anti-gender backlash, for instance, the issue is not only how norms operate but how organized movements, funding networks, law, religion, and media infrastructures mobilize “gender” as an enemy object. Likewise, when queer theory turns to datafication and governance, the issue is not merely the instability of identity but the material consequences of being classified, inferred, or made visible in administrative systems. Anti-normativity remains present, but it must now share explanatory space with political economy, institutional analysis, infrastructure studies, and embodied social theory.

The critique of anti-normativity is therefore not simply hostile. Much recent work does not reject it outright; rather, it tries to **re-situate** it. Anti-normativity still matters, but not as a sufficient politics, and not as the only way of being queerly critical. In that sense, the contemporary challenge is internal and developmental rather than purely external. The field is asking how to preserve anti-normativity’s sharpness without allowing it to become either moralized posture or empty reflex.

## 2.3 Anti-normativity after crisis

The most important shift in 2026 is not theoretical exhaustion but change in atmosphere. The queer-theoretical present increasingly presents itself not as a moment of exhilarating destabilization but as one of damaged institutions, organized reaction, and uncertain futures. That tonal shift matters because anti-normativity operates differently under such conditions. In an earlier moment, one might imagine critique as opening space by exposing the contingency of the norm. In the current moment, critique often has to operate in tandem with defense, care, and survival. It is no accident that recent framing work speaks of queer studies “in and from the ruins.” The phrase suggests a field working amid loss, attrition, and compromised infrastructures rather than under the sign of pure subversion.

Under these conditions, anti-normativity becomes at once more necessary and less sufficient. More necessary, because backlash often presents itself as restoration of the natural, the ordinary, or the proper. Anti-normativity remains one of the best tools for showing that such appeals are historically produced and politically weaponized. Less sufficient, because exposing contingency does not itself build durable worlds, counter institutions, or practices of care. The present therefore pushes queer theory toward a double task: retain suspicion toward normalization while also thinking about how life is maintained under hostile conditions.

This double task helps explain the growing prominence of survival, care, and infrastructure in recent queer scholarship. These are not simply new topics added onto an older anti-normative core; they are signs that the field is trying to think what critique looks like when the problem is no longer only normative closure, but also exhaustion, precarity, and institutional damage. In that context, anti-normativity becomes more modest and perhaps more useful. It is less likely to present itself as the whole political horizon. It functions instead as a method that keeps open the question of what kinds of support, recognition, and collective life can be built without immediately hardening into new regimes of exclusion.

One might put the point this way: anti-normativity survives by ceasing to imagine itself as sovereign. It remains the field’s oldest live core, but it now does its work best when placed in conversation with embodiment, administration, geopolitics, and survival. The recent literature suggests that queer theory is not abandoning its anti-normative inheritance so much as learning to work with it under altered historical conditions. If Section 1 argued that queer theory in 2026 is a field of overlapping pressure zones, then this section shows that anti-normativity still underlies that field—but as an inheritance under stress, revision, and renewed justification.

The next section turns to one of the clearest sites where that stress becomes conceptually productive: the relationship between queer theory and trans studies, and the problem of embodiment, recognition, and administrative life.

# Section 3. Queer theory, trans studies, and the problem of embodiment

If anti-normativity remains the oldest live core of queer theory, then the encounter with **trans studies** is one of the clearest points at which that inheritance has been pressured, revised, and made newly productive. The relationship is not external. Trans studies did not arrive from outside to challenge a fully formed queer theory; the two fields share genealogies, archives, and conceptual vocabularies. Susan Stryker’s well-known phrase “**evil twins**” remains useful precisely because it captures both intimacy and antagonism. Recent work continues to return to that formulation, and a 2025 article on “the intersection of queer theory and transgender sexuality” uses it to frame the long, complex entanglement between the two traditions.

Yet the current significance of this relation lies less in genealogical curiosity than in what it reveals about the changing demands on queer thought. Classical queer theory often privileged anti-fixity, performativity, anti-essentialism, and the instability of identity categories. These moves were intellectually and politically transformative. But they could also produce a certain distance from embodiment, administrative violence, medical systems, and the concrete lived stakes of gender recognition. Trans studies has repeatedly pressed on precisely those points. In doing so, it has not simply corrected queer theory from outside; it has forced queer theory to reckon with what happens when the critique of identity meets the materiality of bodies, the bureaucracy of recognition, and the uneven distribution of livable lives. Recent scholarship explicitly argues for frameworks that retain queer theory’s emphasis on fluidity while centering “the materiality of trans theory’s embodiment of gender.”

This makes the queer/trans relation one of the field’s most consequential hinge points in 2026. The issue is not merely whether trans studies should be “included” within queer theory, nor whether queer theory has been superseded by more grounded approaches. The more important question is conceptual: **what happens to queer theory when embodiment can no longer be treated as secondary, when recognition is not merely ideological but administrative, and when the stakes of gender are lived through medicine, law, data, and everyday survival?** Recent work on epistemic recognition, trans data epistemologies, applied trans studies, and embodiment as an analytical category suggests that these concerns are no longer peripheral. They are among the main ways the field is being reoriented.

The argument of this section is that queer theory and trans studies are now best understood neither as merged nor as wholly separate. Rather, they form a zone of productive instability structured by three recurrent questions. First, what do they inherit from one another historically and conceptually? Second, how does trans studies shift queer theory toward embodiment, administration, and material conditions? Third, is the current movement best described as synthesis, alliance, or enduring tension? The answers matter because this is one of the places where queer theory’s broader move toward infrastructure, governance, and survival becomes especially visible.

| Question | Queer-theory tendency | Trans-studies pressure or revision |
|---|---|---|
| What is gender? | Historically approached through performativity, instability, critique of naturalization | Re-centered as embodied, lived, administered, and materially consequential |
| What is the political problem? | Normativity and the production of intelligibility | Cisnormativity, pathologization, administrative violence, material conditions of life |
| What is at stake in recognition? | Suspicion of stable identity and liberal recognition | Necessity of recognition for healthcare, law, safety, and epistemic survival |
| What is the methodological shift? | Deconstructive and anti-identitarian critique | Applied, infrastructural, embodied, and often explicitly reparative analysis |

## 3.1 Shared genealogies and divergent emphases

The relationship between queer theory and trans studies is frequently narrated through overlap. That is not wrong, but it can be too tidy. The overlap is real: both fields have challenged naturalized assumptions about sex and gender, both have interrogated normativity, and both emerged partly through dissatisfaction with earlier identity-based frameworks. But their emphases have never been identical. Stryker’s “evil twins” phrase continues to circulate because it names this situation exactly: kinship without sameness, shared origin without conceptual harmony. Recent work in *Sexualities* restates the point directly, describing “theoretical conceptualisations of gender and sexuality” as sharing a “complex and intertwined history” while insisting that unresolved questions remain about where trans and gender-diverse sexuality is situated.

Historically, queer theory’s most influential interventions often worked by unsettling categories. Identity was treated as contingent, performative, unstable, and politically ambivalent. That work was indispensable, but it also generated a familiar criticism: if one remains too attached to fluidity, one risks under-describing the lived density of embodiment. Trans studies has persistently pointed toward that limit. It has insisted that gender is not only a discursive field of instability but also something lived through bodies, institutions, temporalities, and material constraints. This is one reason recent scholarship does not merely call for better trans “inclusion” in queer theory. It argues for a new conceptualization capable of holding together “the fluidity of queer theory’s approach to sexuality” and “the materiality of trans theory’s embodiment of gender.”

This is not just a difference in object. It is a difference in **analytic pressure**. Queer theory, in its most classical form, often gains power by showing that what appears natural is socially produced. Trans studies, especially in its more recent applied forms, often gains power by showing that what is socially produced can still be brutally real in its effects. Pathologization, gatekeeping, administrative delay, legal recognition, diagnostic categories, and bodily autonomy are not merely examples of normativity in action. They are infrastructures of life management. To put the matter bluntly: queer theory often asks how categories are destabilized; trans studies often asks what happens when lives are forced to pass through those categories anyway. Recent work in applied trans studies reflects this shift clearly, identifying cisnormativity, pathologization, and gender minority stress as prominent explanatory frameworks while arguing that even these remain incomplete unless they are linked to broader systemic power.

The shared genealogy therefore should not be romanticized. It matters because it explains why the conversation is so intense: the two fields are close enough to wound one another conceptually. But the divergence matters just as much. It is precisely because queer theory and trans studies share a family resemblance that their differences over embodiment, materiality, and recognition become so consequential.

## 3.2 Materiality, recognition, and administration

The point at which this divergence becomes most visible is the question of **recognition**. Classical queer theory has often treated recognition ambivalently, sometimes even suspiciously. Liberal recognition can stabilize identities, reward legibility, and assimilate subjects into institutions that remain normatively structured. That suspicion is still valuable. Yet trans studies has repeatedly shown that recognition is not only an ideological trap. It can also be the condition of access to healthcare, legal status, safety, and epistemic standing. To fail to recognize this is to risk treating survival as if it were merely a theoretical compromise.

Recent work on trans experiences of epistemic recognition makes this especially clear. A 2025 study of the Finnish Trans Act reform debate examines how law reform can be a moment of hope while simultaneously exposing trans people to harmful public discourse. Importantly, the article links recognition to epistemic injustice rather than reducing it to formal policy. Recognition here is not just being named by the state; it is being granted intelligibility without being forced through humiliating public contestation.

This emphasis on epistemic and administrative life is part of a broader reorientation. Trans studies has increasingly foregrounded how gender is mediated by documents, databases, expertise, institutions, and systems of categorization. The recent work on “trans data epistemologies” is revealing in this respect. It argues that trans people are not only subjects of data capture but producers of “specialized ways of knowing” grounded in experiences of racialized, disabled, aging, queer transness. That is significant because it extends the question of embodiment into data, administration, and knowledge production. The issue is no longer only whether gender categories are unstable. It is also how trans subjects navigate, resist, or tactically use infrastructures that demand legibility.

The same shift appears in recent work on embodiment more broadly. A 2026 article in *Journal of Gender Studies* argues that embodiment has become a prevalent concern over the past decade and treats body, affects, and emotions as analytically central rather than derivative. A related 2025 essay on queer embodied methodology argues that reflexivity in LGBTQ+ research often stops short of fully grappling with how researchers’ bodies and affective presences condition knowledge production. Both pieces are relevant here because they show embodiment becoming more than an object of personal narrative. It becomes a methodological and epistemological issue.

What trans studies adds, then, is not a simple return to bodily truth. It is a more complex account of how bodies are lived, known, categorized, administered, and made politically consequential. This reorients queer theory away from any temptation to treat embodiment as merely the conservative residue of identity. It also changes the meaning of the critique of normativity. The problem is not only that norms define some genders as natural and others as deviant. It is that those norms are embedded in healthcare pathways, documentary systems, public controversies, and expert regimes that govern who can access a livable future.

## 3.3 The current synthesis and its tensions

How, then, should one describe the present relation between queer theory and trans studies? The answer is not consensus. Recent scholarship strongly suggests a movement toward rearticulation, but it does not erase tension. Schumacher’s 2025 article is emblematic because it does not argue for abandoning queer theory in favor of trans theory, nor for absorbing trans studies back into an older queer frame. Instead, it calls for an “epistemological framework” adequate to transgender sexuality, one capable of retaining queer fluidity while centering trans materiality. That formulation is conciliatory in tone, but it is also diagnostic: the need for such a framework implies that earlier arrangements were insufficient.

One way to describe the current moment is as an **unstable synthesis**. There is clear convergence around certain themes: embodiment matters; administration matters; recognition cannot be dismissed wholesale; trans life is not adequately explained by abstract anti-fixity alone. But there is not full agreement about what follows. Some strands of trans studies remain skeptical of queer theory’s tendency to diffuse the specificity of trans struggle into generalized anti-normativity. Conversely, some queer-theoretical work worries that greater emphasis on recognition, diagnosis, or material conditions can risk re-stabilizing categories that queer critique has long treated as politically dangerous. These are not misunderstandings; they are structural tensions arising from different problem-settings.

Recent work on cis-supremacy is helpful here because it shows how trans studies is moving beyond the question of inclusion into more systemic accounts of domination. A 2024 sociology article argues that prominent concepts in applied trans studies—cisnormativity, pathologization, and minority stress—are valuable but incomplete unless they are supplemented by stronger accounts of power. This is a useful intervention because it demonstrates that trans studies is not merely adding embodiment to queer theory. It is developing its own robust theories of domination and material conditions. That development may deepen alliance with queer theory in some areas while sharpening divergence in others.

The current synthesis, then, should not be mistaken for disciplinary peace. It is better understood as a field condition in which queer theory can no longer ignore embodiment, administration, or trans materiality, while trans studies continues to negotiate its proximity to and distance from queer critique. The result is productive precisely because it is unresolved. This unresolved quality has important consequences for the rest of the article. It helps explain why anti-gender backlash becomes so central, why governance and data infrastructures emerge as key objects, and why survival and care increasingly displace older fantasies of critique as sufficient political action.

| Model of the queer/trans relation | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Assimilation model**: trans studies folded into queer theory | Preserves historical continuity and shared critique of naturalization | Risks flattening embodiment, administration, and trans-specific conditions |
| **Separation model**: trans studies breaks from queer theory | Protects conceptual specificity and material focus | Risks overstating distance and losing shared genealogies |
| **Unstable synthesis model** | Captures overlap, revision, and productive tension | Less tidy; requires ongoing negotiation rather than theoretical closure |

For the purposes of this article, the third model is the most convincing. It preserves the fact that queer theory and trans studies are historically entangled, while also acknowledging that the field’s center of gravity has shifted. Queer theory in 2026 is not simply being “updated” by trans studies. It is being compelled to rethink what critique looks like when embodiment, administrative violence, and material conditions can no longer be treated as secondary to the instability of identity.

The next section turns from this hinge between theory and embodiment to one of the most urgent political formations shaping the present field: anti-gender backlash, and the re-politicization of “gender” as an object of reaction, fear, and transnational mobilization.

# Section 4. Backlash, anti-gender politics, and the re-politicization of “gender”

One of the clearest shifts in queer theory’s present is that it now has to theorize **organized reaction** with far more precision than before. Earlier queer-theoretical work was certainly attentive to normativity, moral regulation, family ideology, and the policing of sexuality. But the mid-2020s have made it difficult to treat these as diffuse background conditions alone. “Gender” has itself become a political flashpoint: not simply a scholarly term, nor merely a field of social contestation, but a highly mobile enemy-concept through which right-wing, nationalist, religious, and authoritarian projects mobilize fear, grievance, and imagined civilizational threat. Recent discussions of Judith Butler’s *Who’s Afraid of Gender?* repeatedly emphasize this point, noting that anti-gender movements cast gender as a threat to “patriarchal power, national identity, and social order,” and that Butler treats the anti-gender campaign as a major component of contemporary right-wing politics.

This matters because it changes what queer theory has to explain. The field can no longer confine itself to analyzing how gender and sexuality are normalized or pathologized in a generalized sense. It also has to explain how “gender ideology” becomes an extraordinarily effective rallying signifier—capable of linking family panic, nationalist resentment, anti-migrant hostility, anti-trans mobilization, religious conservatism, and hostility to feminism under a single banner. A growing body of recent work treats anti-gender politics not as a local culture-war reaction but as a **transnational formation** with shared tactics, circulating vocabularies, and infrastructural supports. A 2025 review article maps a decade of research on anti-gender politics and stresses that these movements are sustained by transnational connections, funding flows, and rapidly evolving media ecologies. A 2024 edited volume description similarly frames anti-gender mobilizations as shaped by coloniality, racial capitalism, and resurgent nationalisms across multiple world regions.

The argument of this section is that anti-gender backlash has become one of the central objects through which queer theory now understands the present. This is not because backlash is wholly new, nor because queer theory suddenly became political. It is because the current anti-gender formation forces the field to move beyond a narrow account of normativity toward a more explicit analysis of movements, institutions, funding, media infrastructures, and political economy. Recent work on capitalist crisis, development institutions, Russian disinformation, Southeast Asian backlash, and long-duration anti-gender discourse all point toward the same conclusion: anti-gender politics is not a marginal moral panic but a sophisticated and adaptive formation whose force lies precisely in its ability to condense multiple anxieties into a legible enemy object called “gender.”

| Earlier queer-theoretical emphasis | Anti-gender politics forces attention to… |
|---|---|
| Normativity as diffuse social regulation | Organized movements, campaigns, and institutional strategy |
| Sexuality/gender as sites of cultural contest | “Gender ideology” as a portable political weapon |
| Moral panic as episodic reaction | Transnational networks, funding, media ecosystems, and policy circulation |
| Critique of heteronormativity | Analysis of nationalism, authoritarianism, religion, and political economy |

## 4.1 Anti-genderism as a transnational formation

The term **anti-gender politics** names more than opposition to trans rights or feminist reform in any one national setting. It refers to an increasingly interconnected field of mobilization in which “gender ideology,” “family values,” “children’s safety,” religious morality, demographic anxiety, anti-elitism, and civilizational struggle are woven together into a coherent reactionary narrative. Recent research repeatedly warns against reading these mobilizations as isolated domestic controversies. Gore’s 2025 article explicitly analyzes the “transnational anti-gender movement” and argues that queer political economy is needed to understand its tactics. Korolczuk’s 2025 review similarly emphasizes technological infrastructures, cross-border funding, and the rapid transfer of strategies among anti-gender actors.

This transnational framing matters for two reasons. First, it clarifies why anti-gender rhetoric appears simultaneously local and familiar across very different settings. The movement is effective precisely because it is portable: it can be translated into distinct national idioms while retaining a stable emotional structure. “Gender” can be cast as foreign imposition, bureaucratic overreach, elite decadence, danger to children, threat to religion, or attack on the nation, depending on local need. Second, the transnational framing explains why the movement cannot be understood as mere spontaneous conservatism. It is mediated through advocacy networks, conferences, digital platforms, parliamentary discourse, NGOs, churches, policy templates, and media amplification. The very success of anti-gender discourse lies in the way it turns abstract panic into institutional action.

Recent scholarship has also broadened the explanatory frame beyond Europe and North America. A 2025 study of backlash around the UN’s *Being LGBT in Asia* project argues that Southeast Asian anti-gender mobilization cannot be understood simply as local cultural resistance to Western liberalism; it has to be read through geopolitical translation, institutional mediation, and the regional politics of governance. That matters because it complicates the older tendency to treat anti-genderism as either purely “Western” reaction or merely local conservatism elsewhere. The current literature instead suggests a more entangled formation in which transnational scripts are localized through specific histories of state power, development, religion, and postcolonial politics.

A useful consequence of this literature is that it pulls queer theory toward **movement analysis**. Earlier work on normativity sometimes operated at a high level of abstraction, focusing on discourse, representation, and the production of intelligibility. Anti-gender studies does not allow such abstraction to remain untroubled. It asks who is organizing, how narratives circulate, what infrastructures sustain them, and how apparently moral claims are converted into durable political projects. This is one of the clearest ways the present pushes queer theory beyond critique of norms in the narrow sense and toward more explicit accounts of power in motion.

## 4.2 Queer theory’s response to reaction

The rise of anti-gender politics has forced queer theory to change register. It is no longer enough to show that gender and sexuality are contingent or socially produced. Reactionary actors do not simply deny contingency; they weaponize it. “Gender ideology” functions precisely as a phantasmic object onto which broader anxieties are projected. Butler’s intervention has been especially influential here because it argues that anti-gender politics does not respond to a single coherent theory of gender but to a fantasy of collapse: gender becomes the name for everything supposedly destroying civilization, family, nation, and moral order. Recent reviews of Butler’s book repeatedly stress this point, describing gender as a “phantasm” or enemy-image that condenses diffuse anxieties into mobilizable fear.

This is where queer theory’s older expertise in discourse and affect still matters. Anti-gender politics is powerful not only because it is well organized, but because it works affectively. It converts uncertainty into fear, pluralism into threat, and social change into a narrative of civilizational injury. Butler’s interpreters note that the anti-gender movement gains force by staging gender as a total danger: to children, to religion, to national continuity, to heterosexual reproduction, to common sense itself. Queer theory remains well placed to analyze this symbolic condensation. But recent work also makes clear that symbolic analysis alone is not enough. What matters is how fear is institutionalized—through lawmaking, school policy, media campaigns, development discourse, and administrative rollback.

A notable feature of the recent literature is its refusal to reduce anti-gender politics to mere irrationality. The movement is not simply a mistake about gender. It is an effective coalition technology. It links constituencies that may not otherwise cohere: conservative Christians, nationalist parties, anti-migrant movements, patriarchal family advocates, anti-elitist populists, and even some neoliberal actors invested in depoliticizing social reproduction. Recent work in *Third World Quarterly* argues that anti-gender politics can converge with the “logics of smart economics,” revealing affinities between reactionary family politics and technocratic development paradigms. Gore’s queer political economy account similarly suggests that anti-genderism must be read through capitalist crisis and broader restructuring rather than solely through moral ideology.

Queer theory’s response, then, increasingly takes the form of **thickened explanation**. The task is no longer only to unmask moral rhetoric. It is to show how anti-gender discourse binds together affect, institution, economy, and transnational strategy. This thickening of analysis is one of the clearest marks of the field’s present. It also changes the place of queer theory within wider debates. Rather than only critiquing normalization after the fact, queer theory is now more directly engaged in diagnosing reactionary formations as they organize themselves in real time.

## 4.3 Explanatory tensions: religion, nation, economy, authoritarianism

Even within this more developed anti-gender literature, there is no single agreed explanation of the phenomenon. Instead, the field is organized by several competing, overlapping emphases. One approach foregrounds **religion**, especially Christian conservative mobilization and the moral authority of family discourse. Another emphasizes **nation** and civilizational politics, treating anti-genderism as a way to police borders, demographic futures, and imagined cultural integrity. A third foregrounds **authoritarianism**, reading anti-gender campaigns as part of a broader right-wing strategy to consolidate hierarchy and attack pluralist institutions. A fourth stresses **political economy**, linking backlash to neoliberal crisis, austerity, social dislocation, and the management of social reproduction. Recent literature strongly suggests that none of these explanations alone is sufficient.

The most persuasive accounts are those that hold these dimensions together without collapsing them. The 2026 article on anti-gender discourse and masculinity over a thirty-year period is useful because it insists on the longue durée: anti-gender politics is not a sudden eruption but a recurrent discursive field that adapts to changing political conditions. Korolczuk’s review, meanwhile, highlights how technologies and funding streams intensify and accelerate this field. Gore adds a queer political economy angle, while Narayanaswamy complicates the picture by showing that anti-gender politics may not simply oppose neoliberal governance but can sometimes converge with it. Taken together, these studies suggest that anti-genderism should be understood less as a singular ideology than as a **flexible assemblage** that gains strength by moving across religion, market rationality, nationalism, and media systems.

This explanatory plurality matters for queer theory because it prevents a comforting simplification. If anti-gender politics were merely the return of old religious conservatism, it could be analyzed as repetition. If it were merely nationalist panic, it could be read through a familiar framework of exclusion. But the current literature suggests something more adaptive and therefore more difficult: a politics capable of inhabiting liberal institutions, digital platforms, development discourse, democratic language, and expert rhetoric while also mobilizing reactionary fantasies. That adaptive quality is precisely why queer theory now has to widen its analytic frame.

| Explanatory lens | What it illuminates | What it misses if used alone |
|---|---|---|
| Religion | Moral authority, family discourse, anti-sexual politics | Media infrastructures, funding networks, secular nationalism |
| Nation / civilizational politics | Borders, demographic anxiety, anti-migrant and anti-elite narratives | Economic restructuring, technocratic governance |
| Authoritarianism | Hierarchy, repression, rollback of pluralism | Why anti-gender discourse resonates affectively in everyday life |
| Political economy | Crisis, social reproduction, neoliberal convergence | Symbolic and theological force of “gender ideology” |
| Transnational movement analysis | Circulation of tactics, scripts, institutions, and actors | Specific local histories unless carefully grounded |

For the purposes of this article, anti-gender politics is best understood through a combined lens: transnational formation, affective condensation, institutional strategy, and political-economic articulation. That combination helps explain why backlash has become one of the main sites through which queer theory now rethinks its own task. The field is no longer dealing only with normativity as diffuse social order. It is confronting coordinated projects that mobilize “gender” as a crisis-object in order to reorganize public life.

The next section turns to another major cluster in which the politics of recognition, incorporation, and geopolitical power come sharply into view: visibility, homonormativity, and anti-imperial critique.

# Section 5. Inclusion, homonormativity, and anti-imperial critique

If anti-gender backlash forces queer theory to analyze organized reaction, the discourse around **visibility, homonormativity, and anti-imperial critique** forces it to analyze a different problem: what happens when queer subjects are not excluded, but **included selectively, strategically, and under unequal geopolitical conditions**. This discourse remains central because it unsettles one of the most durable liberal assumptions about sexual politics: that recognition and visibility are straightforward indicators of progress. The field’s response has been to show that inclusion is never politically innocent. It may improve lives, reduce vulnerability, and widen public legitimacy, but it can also narrow acceptable forms of queerness, reward legibility and respectability, and be used to certify the virtue of institutions, corporations, or states whose broader operations remain violent or exclusionary. Recent work on corporate-sponsored queer visibility, on rainbow diplomacy, and on transnational queer advocacy all suggest that the question is no longer whether visibility matters, but **how it is organized, by whom, and toward what political ends**.

This is the conceptual terrain in which **homonormativity**, **homonationalism**, **pinkwashing**, and broader **anti-imperial critique** continue to matter. These terms do not mean the same thing, and one of the field’s recurring frustrations is that they are too often collapsed into one another. Homonormativity concerns the domestication of queer politics through respectability, privatization, consumer culture, and institutional manageability. Homonationalism names the incorporation of some queer subjects or rights discourses into nationalist projects and civilizational self-description. Pinkwashing names a more public-facing instrumentalization of LGBTQ-friendly imagery to launder or distract from state, corporate, or military violence. Anti-imperial critique asks whether queer rights discourse itself can function as an instrument of geopolitical hierarchy or settler legitimacy. Recent overview work explicitly describes homonationalism as the linkage between LGBTQ subjects or rights discourse and nationalism, and describes pinkwashing as a related practice in which a state advertises sexual modernity to obscure colonial or violent realities.

The power of this cluster lies in its refusal of easy binaries. It does not ask whether queer inclusion is simply good or simply bad. It asks how gains are structured, what kinds of queer subjects become publicly valuable, which others remain disposable, and how sexual modernity can become a language of national or institutional self-legitimation. At the same time, the discourse has been contested from within. Some scholars worry that concepts such as homonationalism and pinkwashing have become too portable, too easily generalized, or too dismissive of local queer agency and real rights gains. Recent literature on UN advocacy and rainbow diplomacy makes precisely this point: queer rights discourse can be entangled with soft power and still remain a site of meaningful struggle, appropriation, and anti-essentialist activism.

This section argues that inclusion and anti-imperial critique remain inseparable problems in queer theory in 2026. Visibility and recognition cannot be analyzed only as domestic questions of representation. They must also be read through neoliberal respectability, geopolitical ranking, and the unequal circulation of rights language across states, institutions, and media. The discourse is best understood through three linked problems: the costs of visibility; the transformation of queer inclusion into nationalist or imperial value; and the unresolved question of whether transnational queer politics can be rights-based without reproducing the civilizational hierarchies it often seeks to resist.

| Term | Core problem | Typical scale |
|---|---|---|
| **Homonormativity** | Queer inclusion through respectability, domesticity, privatization, consumer citizenship | Domestic, institutional, market |
| **Homonationalism** | Sexual modernity folded into nationalist self-legitimation and exclusion of racialized others | National and geopolitical |
| **Pinkwashing** | Use of LGBTQ-friendly imagery or rights claims to launder violence or domination | Public diplomacy, branding, state/corporate image |
| **Anti-imperial queer critique** | Rights discourse as potentially universalizing, colonial, or civilizationally hierarchical | Transnational, imperial, settler-colonial |

## 5.1 Visibility and its costs

Queer theory’s critique of visibility is not a rejection of being seen. It is a refusal of the idea that visibility is ever neutral. The political force of this refusal is easy to miss because public discourse often presents visibility as an uncomplicated good: marginalized groups become legible, stigma recedes, representation expands, and institutions learn to include. Much of that is true, and the field has never seriously denied the material benefits that can attach to recognition. But queer theory’s intervention has been to ask what forms of life visibility rewards, what kinds of comportment it makes desirable, and what infrastructure of legibility lies beneath apparently generous inclusion.

This is where the discourse on homonormativity remains crucial. The old insight was that queer politics may be welcomed when it appears compatible with coupledom, domesticity, consumer citizenship, upward mobility, and institutional calm. Recent work updates that insight rather than abandoning it. The 2026 article on “corporate-sponsored queer visibility” in London argues that anti-normativity itself can be absorbed into market rationalities, linking queerness to happiness, authenticity, and productivist self-fashioning rather than oppositional politics. That is significant because it shows how the problem has evolved. Inclusion is no longer only about marriage, military service, or domestic respectability in the older sense. It is also about corporate aesthetics, diversity branding, lifestyle coherence, and professionally manageable difference.

A related recent article on “homocapitalism” argues that homosexual subjectivity can be folded into capital accumulation in ways that reach beyond Euro-American settings. The importance of this argument is not that capital has discovered queerness only recently, but that the linkage between queer visibility and market logic is increasingly explicit and transnational. Visibility can now operate as a corporate asset, a city-branding strategy, a labor-management technique, and a sign of cosmopolitan virtue. The point is not that such visibility is unreal. It is that it is politically structured. To be visible under these conditions often means being readable through terms compatible with investment, branding, or institutional productivity.

This is why queer theory continues to distinguish between **recognition** and **liberation**. Recognition may reduce vulnerability while simultaneously narrowing the range of public queerness. It may attach value to those queer subjects who are coupled, competent, productive, patriotic, or stylishly self-possessed, while rendering others excessive, difficult, racialized, poor, disabled, or insufficiently assimilable. Recent field-framing work that urges attention beyond the isolated individual can be read as part of this same impulse: a resistance to treating visibility as a private achievement rather than a socially stratified condition.

## 5.2 Homonationalism and pinkwashing

If homonormativity asks how queer subjects become acceptable within liberal social order, **homonationalism** asks how that acceptability can be scaled up into national self-description. Jasbir Puar’s intervention remains central here because it shifted the question from exclusion to **selective incorporation**. The issue is not only that queer people have been denied membership in the nation; it is that some queer subjects can become evidence of the nation’s modernity, civility, or tolerance. A later restatement by Puar describes “acceptance and tolerance for gay and lesbian subjects” as having become a “barometer” of sovereignty. The formulation remains powerful because it captures the geopolitical turn: sexual modernity becomes a measure of whether a nation counts as advanced, civilized, or worthy of authority.

This has two consequences. First, queer inclusion can become part of a national story about progress. Second, that story often depends on the production of a sexual or racial other figured as backward, intolerant, patriarchal, or dangerous. Recent overview scholarship continues to stress that homonationalism operates through the collusion of LGBTQ rights discourse with nationalism and often through Orientalist or Islamophobic contrasts. This is why the concept remains relevant in Europe, North America, and elsewhere: it helps explain how liberal sexual politics can coexist with anti-migrant politics, securitization, and civilizational rhetoric. New work on British “homo-nativism” explicitly examines the strategic alignment of sexual-liberal discourse with nativist exclusion, suggesting that the analytic travels beyond its earliest canonical sites.

**Pinkwashing** names a related but more public and often more activist-facing dynamic. Here the emphasis falls not on the nation’s general sexual self-conception, but on the instrumental display of LGBTQ-friendliness to obscure or soften perceptions of violence, occupation, repression, or corporate wrongdoing. Recent scholarship continues to define pinkwashing in exactly these terms. The point is not that every rights gain is fake or every public mention of queer inclusion is cynical. Aeyal Gross’s work is important precisely because it insists on this distinction: “real and sometimes imagined” gains can still be “appropriated for propaganda.” That formulation remains one of the most useful in the literature because it keeps the analysis focused on **instrumentalization**, not on denying the existence of actual improvements in queer life.

At the same time, the literature has become more careful about overreach. Jason Ritchie’s widely cited critique warned that homonationalism had become “virtually hegemonic” as an interpretive framework in some parts of queer scholarship and activism, risking conceptual overextension and flattening of context. Recent overview work echoes that concern, noting criticisms about the universalization of the concept at the expense of local complexity. This is a serious internal debate. A theory that explains too much begins to explain too little. The most persuasive recent work therefore retains homonationalism and pinkwashing as critical tools while demanding greater historical and geopolitical specificity.

## 5.3 Global rights, decolonial critique, and the “queer international”

The anti-imperial side of this discourse asks a deeper question: can queer rights circulate globally without reproducing imperial or civilizational hierarchy? Joseph Massad’s critique of the “Gay International” remains the foundational provocation here because it argued that Western rights discourse could universalize sexual categories and impose them elsewhere under the sign of liberation. Whether one agrees with the full argument or not, its importance is undeniable. It forced queer theory to confront the possibility that rights language might travel not only as emancipation but also as power.

Yet the literature has not stood still. Recent work increasingly pushes beyond a simple binary between universal rights and anti-imperial skepticism. A 2025 article on LGBTI+ advocacy at the United Nations explicitly offers “a more nuanced analysis” than the older “Western imposition” framework alone. It argues that activists are not merely passive carriers of external discourse; they also contest cultural essentialism and help construct what the author calls a “queer subject of rights” under conditions that remain politically unequal. This is a crucial update. It does not reject anti-imperial critique; it refuses to let anti-imperial critique become so totalizing that it erases non-Western queer agency.

A similar tension appears in recent work on **rainbow diplomacy**. The 2025 *International Affairs* article by Conway and Edenborg argues that state support for LGBTQ rights in foreign policy is often inconsistent, symbolic, and entangled with wider strategic interests. At the same time, the authors do not reduce such diplomacy to mere bad faith. Their concern is precisely with its unevenness: rhetorical commitments may exist without durable institutional support, but that does not mean diplomacy is politically irrelevant. This helps sharpen the larger argument of this section. Rights discourse can be instrumentalized, but instrumentalization is not the whole story. The field must hold together critique of soft power with attention to actual openings, alliances, and material effects.

This is why recent decolonial work often proceeds through complication rather than negation. Calls to produce theory “from” non-Western locations, to think queer critique through decolonial and nonlinear frameworks, or to analyze global queer governance more carefully all reflect dissatisfaction with any easy story of either diffusion or refusal. The question has become one of **translation, scale, and asymmetry**: how rights claims move, who controls the terms of their movement, and how local actors tactically inhabit, resist, or repurpose them. The phrase “queer international,” recently revisited in this literature, is useful insofar as it marks precisely this tension between transnational solidarity and geopolitical hierarchy.

| Position | Core claim | Risk if overstated |
|---|---|---|
| Liberal inclusionism | Visibility and rights are primarily signs of progress | Ignores selectivity, marketization, and geopolitical instrumentalization |
| Homonormativity critique | Inclusion often rewards manageability, privatization, and respectability | Can underplay the material value of recognition |
| Homonationalism / pinkwashing critique | Sexual modernity can legitimate nationalism, securitization, and violence | Can become overgeneralized or flatten local agency |
| Nuanced anti-imperial rights critique | Rights claims are entangled with power but can still be appropriated strategically by local actors | Can become vague if asymmetries of power are not specified |

The most persuasive 2026 position is not a simple midpoint between celebration and suspicion. It is a layered analysis in which queer inclusion is treated as materially consequential, politically selective, and geopolitically mobile all at once. Visibility matters; rights matter; but neither can be read outside the structures that make them valuable to some institutions, some nations, and some publics. That is why this discourse remains one of the field’s most durable pressure zones. It prevents queer theory from mistaking incorporation for emancipation, while also preventing anti-imperial critique from collapsing into a flat dismissal of every global rights project.

The next section turns to the domain in which many of these questions about legibility, inclusion, exposure, and management become infrastructural: governance, surveillance, and data systems.

# Section 6. Governance, surveillance, and data infrastructures

One of the clearest signs that queer theory’s center of gravity has shifted is the growing importance of **governance, surveillance, and data infrastructures** as conceptual rather than merely topical concerns. The field is no longer asking only how sexuality and gender are represented, normalized, or stigmatized in discourse. It is also asking how they are **captured, inferred, sorted, moderated, monetized, and regulated** through platforms, databases, recommender systems, identity architectures, and institutional records. Rebecca Saunders’ 2025 formulation of **“sexual datafication”** is especially useful because it names this shift directly, arguing that “the next significant phase in the history of sexuality” takes place “through data power,” and that sexuality is now “constructed and regulated by data power” in ways shaped by capitalist, state, and colonial systems.

This is not a departure from queer theory’s older concern with normativity so much as a change in where normativity is located. In earlier formulations, norms were often approached through discourse, kinship, morality, and institutional culture. The newer work shows that norms are also embedded in **technical infrastructures**: fields in a database, profile categories on a platform, recommender systems, data pipelines, knowledge graphs, age-assurance systems, and algorithmic classifiers. The key question is no longer only what identities mean. It is also how identities and intimacies become computationally legible, and what kinds of power that legibility enables. Saunders’ article makes the interdisciplinary stakes explicit, placing sexual datafication at the intersection of queer, feminist, and postcolonial surveillance studies, political economy, AI and algorithm studies, platform studies, and digital sociology.

The importance of this discourse in 2026 lies in the fact that it brings together several problems that had previously been treated separately: visibility and exposure, rights and risk, data justice and administrative control, queer self-representation and institutional categorization. Recent work on **trans data epistemologies** is particularly revealing here. Rather than treating trans communities only as objects of extraction or misclassification, Stevens and Doğan argue that trans activists are producing “transgender ways of knowing with data,” using data activism to protect communities and support community care amid proliferating threats to trans rights. This is a major development because it complicates the simple opposition between data as domination and data as justice. It suggests that the politics of data is now one of the central places where queer theory must think about power, care, and survival together.

At the same time, the legal and policy environment has caught up with some of these concerns. In the European Union, the Digital Services Act prohibits targeted advertising when profiling uses sensitive data such as sexual orientation, and official Commission materials explicitly frame this as part of the new online environment. The AI Act likewise identifies “sexual or political orientation” among the kinds of specific categories implicated in biometric categorisation systems, and Council materials around the final regulation state that biometric categorisation for the purpose of law enforcement in relation to characteristics such as sexual orientation “should be prohibited.” These developments do not solve the problem. But they show that the risks queer theory has been diagnosing are now recognized, at least partly, in regulatory frameworks.

The argument of this section is that governance and data infrastructures have become one of the field’s central pressure zones because they transform queer theory’s classic concern with legibility into a problem of **technical and administrative power**. The field is now asking at least three linked questions: how does queerness become an object of infrastructural governance; when does data serve justice and when does it deepen exposure or control; and what happens when sexuality and gender are modeled in databases, platforms, and AI systems that were not designed with queer life in view? These questions do not replace earlier ones about visibility and anti-normativity. They extend them into a domain where categories are not only narrated but operationalized.

| Earlier queer-theoretical concern | Infrastructural reformulation |
|---|---|
| Visibility | Traceability, searchability, and platform exposure |
| Identity categories | Database schema, profile fields, classifier labels |
| Normativity | Technical defaults, moderation policies, ranking systems |
| Recognition | Legal-data categories, verification systems, records |
| Exclusion | Misclassification, erasure, non-representation, hostile inference |

## 6.1 From discourse to infrastructure

The move from discourse to infrastructure does not imply that discourse has ceased to matter. Rather, it means that discourse now works through systems that structure action at scale. Saunders’ account of sexual datafication is instructive because it insists that the regulation of sexuality—traditionally analyzed through morality, medicine, law, and culture—now also takes place through “data power.” Her framing is expansive: sexual datafication explores how “data cultures and practices significantly impact on sexual relationships and attitudes,” and how sexuality is shaped by capitalist, state, and colonial systems through data. That is a stronger claim than saying platforms merely host sexual content or that apps mediate dating. It means that sexuality itself is increasingly produced inside a technical environment that disciplines, economizes, and classifies.

This infrastructural turn helps explain why queer theory has become more interested in projects such as Wikidata, recommender systems, and data models. The 2024 article **“What does it mean to be queer in Wikidata?”** offers a concrete example. It argues that ongoing digitization and datafication create significant risks for marginalized communities and shows how a transnational online community grappled with the representation of gender beyond narrow binary assumptions. The article is important not because Wikidata is uniquely powerful, but because it demonstrates how data modeling decisions—what fields exist, how categories are named, how bots and policies work—shape whether queer and trans identities become computationally real, misrepresented, or erased. The authors explicitly describe “missteps and lack of care” alongside later efforts at “data repair,” which is an unusually sharp way of showing that infrastructure is not neutral scaffolding but an active site of ethical and political struggle.

Seen this way, queer theory’s infrastructural turn is continuous with its older suspicion of naturalization. The difference is that the norm is no longer encountered only as public common sense or institutional morality. It appears as a default field in a form, as a model in a knowledge base, as a ranking logic in a feed, as a category in biometric classification, or as a moderation rule embedded in platform architecture. This is why the field increasingly overlaps with critical data studies. The underlying question remains familiar—who becomes legible on what terms?—but the mechanisms are now technical, procedural, and often opaque.

## 6.2 Data, protection, and exposure

A central debate within this discourse concerns whether more data about queer and trans populations is emancipatory, dangerous, or both. On one side, the argument for data is strong: without demographic visibility, health disparities remain undercounted, discrimination remains harder to document, and public policy can continue to treat queer and trans populations as statistically negligible. On the other side, data capture creates archives that can be repurposed, leaked, weaponized, or used to intensify monitoring. The field has increasingly moved beyond this simple polarity, but the tension remains real.

Recent trans-data scholarship is useful here because it refuses to collapse the issue into a yes-or-no question. Stevens and Doğan show that trans activists are using data not only because they have been captured by datafication, but because data can support “community care,” policy work, and media production in a hostile environment. Their framing of “transgender ways of knowing with data” is especially important because it displaces the assumption that data is only something done to marginalized populations. At the same time, the article’s starting point—“proliferating threats to trans rights”—makes clear that such data practices arise under pressure. Data is both a survival tool and a site of vulnerability.

The policy landscape reflects this same ambivalence. The European Commission’s DSA materials state that platforms can no longer show ads based on sensitive data such as sexual orientation and that targeted advertising is prohibited when profiling uses special categories of personal data, including sexual orientation. This is not just a consumer-protection detail. It is an official acknowledgment that sexual identity data can become a vector of manipulation and unequal treatment when folded into platform business models. At the same time, the DSA also emphasizes transparency, feed choice, and repository requirements for advertising, which suggests that regulation is trying to mediate between visibility and control rather than simply withdrawing from data altogether.

A similar tension appears in policy on minors and age assurance. The Commission’s 2025 materials on the protection of minors under the DSA recommend age-assurance methods for adult content that are “accurate, reliable, robust, non-intrusive, and non-discriminatory.” The need for all of those qualifiers is telling. Protective infrastructures can also become intrusive infrastructures. Queer theory’s contribution here is to insist that “safety” and “protection” are never analytically pure terms. They must be examined in light of how verification, inference, and access controls distribute burden across different populations.

This is the key point: the politics of data is not only about collection. It is about **purpose, circulation, inference, and downstream use**. To say that data can serve justice does not answer how it will be categorized, who will store it, how it will travel, or under what future conditions it may be repurposed. That is why queer theory remains wary even when data is mobilized for ostensibly progressive ends. The problem is not solely bad intention; it is the structural tendency of data systems to outlive the purposes for which they were built.

## 6.3 Platformed sexuality and algorithmic sorting

The third major shift in this discourse is the move from static data categories to **platformed and algorithmic sexuality**. Sexuality is not only stored; it is ranked, inferred, marketed, recommended, and filtered. Saunders’ framing of sexual datafication emphasizes exactly this transition, describing how data cultures shape sexual relationships and attitudes and how sexuality is disciplined and economized in the twenty-first century. Her more recent 2025 article on Pornhub insights, while outside the core argument of this section, reinforces the same broader point: platforms do not merely host sexual life; they actively produce new sexual cultures through metrics, analytics, and market logics.

The relevance for queer theory is immediate. Once sexuality is entangled with recommender systems, ad-tech, moderation pipelines, and public repositories, queer life is no longer only a matter of identity expression or community formation. It becomes an object of **algorithmic sorting**. Some users become more discoverable, some less visible; some content is flagged, some amplified; some identities are treated as sensitive, some monetizable, some risky. The official DSA description of feed options is revealing in this regard: users on large platforms can now opt for non-personalized feeds, thereby choosing whether to receive algorithmically suggested content. The regulation assumes, in other words, that recommendation systems are already structuring what users see, and that this structure matters enough to require explicit choice.

The same logic extends into AI governance. The AI Act’s treatment of biometric categorisation matters not because it solves all classification problems, but because it codifies an institutional recognition that certain inferences—such as assigning people to categories including sexual orientation on the basis of biometric data—are especially dangerous. The text defines biometric categorisation systems as systems that assign people to “specific categories” on the basis of biometric data and explicitly notes that such categories may include “sexual or political orientation.” Council materials around the regulation go further by stating that the use of biometric categorisation for law-enforcement purposes in relation to characteristics such as sexual orientation should be prohibited. This is a striking development: a core queer-theoretical concern about the extraction of intimate identity through technical systems is now part of primary regulatory architecture.

At the same time, one should avoid overstating regulatory closure. Formal bans on some practices do not eliminate the wider problem of inference, profiling, or platform governance. A great deal of algorithmic sorting happens not through explicit declaration of identity but through proxies: browsing, networks, purchasing, watch history, geolocation, visual cues, and behavioral clustering. The queer-theoretical lesson here is that **inference may be more consequential than confession**. One need not say “I am queer” for systems to act as though they know something intimate about one’s sexuality or gender. This is one reason the discourse on data infrastructures has become so important: it explains how legibility operates even when no one explicitly asks the traditional identity question.

| Domain | Queer-theoretical concern | Infrastructural manifestation |
|---|---|---|
| Platforms | Who is seen, ranked, and monetized | Recommenders, ad targeting, moderation, feed architecture |
| Databases | How identities are stabilized or erased | Schema design, category fields, default binaries, data repair |
| AI systems | Who is inferred and on what basis | Biometric categorisation, predictive profiling, automated sorting |
| Regulation | Whether harm can be limited without deepening surveillance | Sensitive-data rules, ad bans, age assurance, transparency obligations |

Taken together, these developments make clear why governance and data infrastructures now occupy such a central place in queer theory’s contemporary map. The issue is not simply that queer people use platforms or appear in datasets. It is that sexuality and gender have become increasingly governable through technical systems whose categories, defaults, and business models help shape the conditions of queer life. In this sense, Section 6 extends the earlier themes of the article rather than departing from them. Anti-normativity reappears here as suspicion toward technical normalisation; the queer/trans relation reappears as a struggle over embodiment and data; anti-gender backlash reappears in conflicts over classification and regulation; anti-imperial critique reappears in questions about how data power travels across states, platforms, and unequal populations.

The next section turns from this infrastructural present to the field’s increasingly explicit concern with endurance: survival, care, futurity, and the question of how queer theory thinks under damaged conditions rather than only against normativity.

# Section 7. Survival, care, futurity, and the future of the field

If the earlier sections have traced queer theory’s movement through anti-normativity, embodiment, backlash, incorporation, and infrastructure, this final section turns to the field’s changing **affective and ethical horizon**. One of the clearest developments in recent work is that queer theory is increasingly concerned not only with transgression, critique, or denaturalization, but with **how queer life is sustained under damaged conditions**. This is not a minor tonal adjustment. It marks a substantive shift in what counts as a pressing problem. The question is no longer only how subjects exceed or unsettle norms; it is also how they endure exhaustion, institutional hostility, infrastructural neglect, environmental precarity, and the slow wearing down of collective possibility. Recent field-framing work in *GLQ* speaks of queer studies “in and from the ruins,” which is significant precisely because “ruins” names more than a mood. It suggests a condition in which the field must think amid attrition, compromised institutions, and frayed promises rather than under the sign of pure transgressive vitality.

This change has several sources. One is political: backlash, anti-gender mobilization, and uneven rights regimes have made the language of mere visibility or expressive freedom feel insufficient. Another is infrastructural: as sexuality and gender become increasingly mediated through data systems, bureaucracies, and public-health institutions, the field is drawn toward questions of maintenance, care, and material support. A third is ecological and temporal: recent scholarship explicitly links queer life to climate, damaged futures, and the problem of survival under broader conditions of planetary instability. The result is that “survival,” “care,” “livability,” and “future possibility” are no longer just adjacent ethical themes. They are becoming organizing concepts. A 2026 article in *Futures* asks directly what it would mean to consider the “survival of the queerest” and treats queer survival as a problem of future possibilities rather than mere continuation.

This shift does not mean queer theory has abandoned critique. Rather, critique is being redistributed. The field still asks what norms do, but it increasingly asks under what conditions critique itself remains livable, how minoritarian worlds are reproduced, and what kinds of care are needed when institutions are not merely exclusionary but fraying. Recent work on queer/trans climate futures, on health inequity and erasure, and on embodied research practice suggests that queer thought is being pulled toward a more **reparative and infrastructural sensibility** without ceasing to be suspicious of normalization.

The argument of this section is that survival, care, and futurity have become central not because queer theory has softened into therapeutic concern, but because the present demands a theory of **maintenance under pressure**. The field is increasingly asking what kinds of social worlds, infrastructures, attachments, and embodied practices keep queer life possible when critique alone cannot. This section develops that claim through three movements: the turn from transgression to survival, the expanding relevance of ecology and damaged futures, and a final reflection on what this means for the future of queer theory itself.

| Earlier emphasis | Emerging emphasis |
|---|---|
| Transgression, anti-normativity, denaturalization | Survival, care, endurance, maintenance |
| The subject against the norm | Communities and infrastructures under attrition |
| Queerness as disruption | Queerness as livability under hostile conditions |
| Futurity as utopian or anti-reproductive debate | Futurity as damaged, contested, ecological, and infrastructural |

## 7.1 From transgression to survival

The move from transgression to survival is not a clean break, and it would be misleading to narrate it as one. Queer theory has always contained concerns with vulnerability, loss, and world-making. But recent work makes these concerns newly explicit and central. The *GLQ* framing of queer studies “in and from the ruins” is useful because it suggests that the field is working under conditions where the old confidence in critique as a destabilizing force must coexist with a harsher recognition: destabilization is not always liberating when lives are already precarious, when institutions are failing, and when hostility is organized rather than ambient.

This is one reason recent work on survival matters. The 2026 *Futures* article explicitly asks what it means to “queer” survival, and in doing so it resists the idea that survival is merely a minimal residue after politics. Instead, survival appears as a conceptual and ethical problem in its own right: how futures are imagined, what kinds of endurance are possible, and what forms of care are required when conditions are structurally adverse. The significance of this move is that it relocates queer theory’s critical energy. Rather than opposing normativity only through refusal or disruption, the field increasingly asks how subjects and communities persist, adapt, and keep worlds open.

This does not mean that survival is politically innocent. On the contrary, survival is always unevenly distributed. Some lives are more easily sustained than others; some forms of care are institutionally supported while others are privatized, informal, or rendered impossible. That is why the turn to survival should not be mistaken for a retreat into individual resilience discourse. The better recent work resists that. It treats survival as social, infrastructural, and historical. To survive is not simply to cope. It is to inhabit systems of health, housing, kinship, law, environment, and public culture that may or may not make queer life durable. This broadening of scale is one of the most important developments in the field’s present.

A related consequence is that the old opposition between assimilation and transgression starts to lose some of its organizing power. If the political problem is how to keep queer and trans life possible under attrition, then the relevant question is not only whether a practice is norm-breaking, but whether it sustains worlds, redistributes care, or makes endurance more available. This is not an abandonment of anti-normativity. It is a revaluation of what critical action looks like when the immediate task is not solely to undo social forms, but to keep people alive within and beyond them.

## 7.2 Ecology, infrastructure, and damaged futures

One of the most striking 2025–2026 developments is the widening of queer futurity into explicitly ecological and infrastructural terrains. Earlier debates about futurity often unfolded through the opposition between utopian hope and anti-reproductive negativity. Those debates remain part of the archive, but recent work increasingly reframes the issue around **damaged futures**: climate instability, institutional exhaustion, public-health inequality, and the uneven distribution of livable environments. A 2025 special cluster on queer/trans climate futures in *Australian Feminist Studies* makes this shift explicit, bringing queer and trans analysis into direct relation with climate politics and the problem of inhabitable worlds.

This matters because it changes the meaning of futurity. The future is no longer approached only as a symbolic horizon of hope, reproduction, or refusal. It is also approached as a material question: who will have air, shelter, healthcare, mobility, and social support; whose bodies and communities are treated as worth maintaining; which infrastructures are allowed to decay. Queer theory’s encounter with ecology is therefore not merely thematic. It extends the field’s long-standing concern with livable lives into environmental and infrastructural registers. The same can be said of recent work on health inequity and erasure. A 2026 article in *Health & Social Work* treats “queer embodiment and erasure” as central to understanding health inequity, arguing that queer and trans lives are shaped by forms of invisibility and institutional neglect that are not reducible to individual stigma.

This overlap between ecology, embodiment, and infrastructure is especially important for the future of queer theory because it prevents the field from imagining survival as a purely intimate or cultural process. Survival depends on systems. It depends on clinics, housing, food, transport, documents, archives, social services, and public space. It depends on whether gendered and sexual life can be lived without constant administrative violence or physical precarity. In that sense, the ecological turn and the infrastructural turn are not separate developments; they converge around the question of what makes worlds habitable.

| Site of futurity | Older emphasis | 2025–2026 expansion |
|---|---|---|
| Kinship and reproduction | Whether queer futures challenge or reproduce normative family time | Futures shaped by care networks, damaged institutions, and social reproduction under strain |
| Utopia / negativity | Hope versus anti-futurity | Survival amid ecological crisis, public hostility, and infrastructural decay |
| Embodiment | Identity and selfhood | Health inequity, erasure, and material conditions of sustaining bodies |
| Public space | Recognition and belonging | Environmental vulnerability, policy neglect, and differential access to support systems |

## 7.3 Conclusion: Queer theory after consolidation

What, then, does this turn toward survival, care, and damaged futurity imply for the future of queer theory? The first implication is that the field appears increasingly unwilling to define itself solely through opposition to normativity. That inheritance remains indispensable, but it now operates alongside questions of maintenance, repair, and collective endurance. The field is not becoming less critical; it is becoming more explicit about the fact that critique unfolds in conditions where people require support, infrastructures, and durable forms of sociality.

The second implication is that queer theory’s interdisciplinarity is deepening rather than dissolving. The themes explored in this final section—survival, care, ecology, embodiment, health inequity—cannot be contained within older literary-theoretical boundaries. They require engagement with social work, environmental studies, public health, science and technology studies, legal theory, and social theory more broadly. The recent article on queer embodied methodology is indicative here. It argues that research itself must grapple with embodied presence, affect, and the social conditions under which knowledge is produced. This is a smaller-scale methodological example of the wider shift: queer theory is increasingly reflexive about its own conditions of possibility.

The third implication is historiographic. If one looks back across the discourse clusters mapped in this article—anti-normativity, trans studies, anti-gender backlash, homonormativity and anti-imperial critique, governance and data infrastructures, and finally survival and care—a pattern emerges. Queer theory in 2026 is less defined by one dominant concept than by the way its older concepts are being **repositioned under pressure**. Anti-normativity persists, but now alongside administration and embodiment. Critique of inclusion persists, but now alongside geopolitical and corporate analysis. Suspicion of legibility persists, but now in data systems and AI governance. Futurity persists, but now as a problem of ecology and endurance. This does not amount to a paradigmatic replacement. It amounts to a changed field condition.

| Discourse cluster | Classical concern | 2026 reorientation |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-normativity | Undoing naturalized norms and identities | Retained as method, but insufficient on its own |
| Queer/trans relation | Instability of gender and sexuality | Embodiment, recognition, administration, materiality |
| Anti-gender politics | Normative backlash | Organized reaction, transnational movement, political economy |
| Homonormativity / anti-imperial critique | Costs of inclusion | Geopolitical, corporate, and diplomatic instrumentalization |
| Governance / data | Legibility and regulation | Technical infrastructure, inference, profiling, administrative power |
| Survival / care / futurity | Alternative worlds and temporalities | Endurance, ecology, damaged institutions, maintenance |

The most convincing conclusion is not that queer theory has abandoned its earlier commitments, nor that it has simply widened into an amorphous interdisciplinary field. It is that the field has entered a phase of **post-consolidation pressure**. Its canonical moves remain available, but they no longer command the present on their own. What defines queer theory in 2026 is the need to think normativity, embodiment, backlash, governance, and survival together. That conjunction is what gives the field its current force. It is also what makes field-mapping worthwhile: without such a map, one risks mistaking older canonical keywords for the whole of the present.

If Section 1 asked why queer theory should be mapped now, and Sections 2–6 traced the major discourse clusters through which the field currently operates, this final section suggests why the map matters. It matters because queer theory’s future will likely depend not on inventing a single new master concept, but on sustaining this difficult conversation among critique, care, infrastructure, and livability under conditions that remain volatile, unequal, and unresolved.
