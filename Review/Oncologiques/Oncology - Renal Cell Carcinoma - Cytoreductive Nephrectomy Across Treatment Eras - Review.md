## 1\. Historical evolution of cytoreductive nephrectomy in metastatic renal cell carcinoma

Cytoreductive nephrectomy (CN) first achieved prominence in the cytokine era, when systemic therapy for metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) consisted largely of interferon‑alpha (IFN‑α) and high‑dose interleukin‑2, both associated with modest response rates and median overall survival (OS) under one year in unselected populations (54,58). In this context, two pivotal randomized trials—SWOG 8949 and EORTC 30947—demonstrated that nephrectomy followed by IFN‑α significantly improved OS compared with IFN‑α alone in patients with primary tumour in situ and synchronous metastases (56,58,59). The pooled analysis documented a median OS of 13.6 months in the CN + IFN‑α arm versus 7.8 months in the IFN‑α alone arm, corresponding to an absolute survival gain of approximately 5.8 months and a hazard ratio around 0.69 favouring CN (56,58,59). EORTC 30947 reported median OS 17 versus 7 months (CN + IFN vs IFN alone), with a hazard ratio of 0.54 (95% CI 0.31–0.94), reinforcing the survival advantage of primary tumour debulking in fit patients receiving cytokine therapy (56).

Eligibility criteria in these cytokine‑era trials were relatively broad. Patients required histologically confirmed mRCC with primary tumour in situ, acceptable performance status (ECOG 0–1), and resectable disease, but there were no formal restrictions based on multivariable prognostic models, which had not yet been established (36,42,55). Consequently, many individuals who would now be classified as IMDC poor‑risk were offered CN, provided they were operable and medically fit (54,58). Long‑term survivors beyond five years, while uncommon, occurred more frequently in the CN arms and were typically characterized by low metastatic burden (often lung‑predominant), absence of extensive visceral disease, and preserved performance status (56,59,72,85). These observations supported a paradigm in which CN was routinely recommended for most patients with synchronous mRCC and reasonable surgical risk, with systemic cytokine therapy applied as an adjunct (54,58,59).

The advent of VEGF‑targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), particularly sunitinib, fundamentally altered the treatment landscape and prompted re‑evaluation of CN (45,56). Retrospective series in the TKI era suggested that CN combined with TKIs was associated with improved survival, especially in favourable and intermediate‑risk patients, but these analyses were subject to substantial selection bias, as surgery was preferentially offered to younger, fitter patients with fewer metastases (69,72,74). To address this, the phase III CARMENA trial randomized 450 patients with primary clear‑cell mRCC of intermediate or poor MSKCC risk to either immediate CN followed by sunitinib or sunitinib alone (24,45,102). CARMENA demonstrated that sunitinib alone was non‑inferior to CN plus sunitinib, with median OS of 18.4 months in the sunitinib‑alone arm versus 13.9 months in the CN arm (61,102). Subgroup analyses indicated no OS benefit for upfront CN in intermediate‑risk patients and potential detriment in poor‑risk patients, in whom surgery may delay or preclude effective systemic therapy (61,67,103).

The SURTIME randomized trial further refined the question by examining timing rather than presence of CN in patients with synchronous mRCC receiving sunitinib (24,62,97,101). Participants were randomized to immediate CN followed by sunitinib or to three cycles of sunitinib with the option of deferred CN in responders or stable disease (62,97,101). While deferred CN did not improve the 28‑week progression‑free rate (primary endpoint), OS favoured the deferred approach, with median OS 32.4 months versus 15.0 months for immediate CN (HR 0.57; 95% CI 0.34–0.95) (97,101). Notably, almost all patients in the deferred‑CN arm received sunitinib, whereas a significant proportion in the immediate‑CN arm never received systemic therapy due to postoperative decline or early progression (97,101). These data suggested that initial systemic therapy can serve as a biological filter, identifying patients with disease control who may benefit from CN while sparing rapid progressors from futile surgery (24,45,62). Together, CARMENA and SURTIME shifted practice away from routine upfront CN towards selective, often deferred, surgery in TKI‑treated mRCC (24,45,61).

- Key characteristics of the cytokine‑era CN paradigm:
	- Broad eligibility based on performance status and operability, without formal prognostic scoring (36,42,54).
	- Significant absolute OS benefit from CN on a background of modest systemic efficacy, with a visible increase in 5‑year survivors (56,58,59).
- Key changes introduced in the TKI era:
	- Demonstration that upfront CN is not required for all patients and may harm intermediate/poor‑risk patients needing urgent systemic therapy (24,61,67,102).
	- Introduction of deferred CN after demonstrating benefit from TKIs, emphasizing biological selection and minimizing futile surgeries (24,62,97,101).

## 2\. Immunotherapy era: trial design, patient selection, and the changing role of CN

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and IO–TKI combinations have further transformed first‑line mRCC treatment, achieving higher objective response rates, deeper responses, and longer OS than TKI monotherapy (24,45,93). Dual‑ICI regimens such as nivolumab–ipilimumab and combinations of ICIs with VEGF‑TKIs (e.g. pembrolizumab–axitinib) deliver 3–4‑year OS probabilities approaching or exceeding 50% in intermediate‑risk clear‑cell mRCC, even without mandated CN, reshaping the baseline survival landscape (61,74,93). In this context, the central question has evolved from whether CN is beneficial at all to identifying in which patients and at what time CN can add meaningful benefit to potent systemic therapy (24,44,93,104–106).

Several prospective IO‑era CN trials formalize a more stringent approach to patient selection. NORDIC‑SUN (NCT03977571) is a randomized phase II study evaluating deferred CN in patients with synchronous mRCC whose primary tumour remains in situ (18,53). All patients receive first‑line systemic therapy—typically IO/IO or IO–TKI—and are reassessed after 3–6 months (18,53). Only those with ≤3 IMDC risk factors, adequate performance status (ECOG 0–1), and sufficient surgical candidacy are randomized to CN plus continued systemic therapy versus continued systemic therapy alone (18,53). Patients with >3 IMDC factors or poor surgical fitness remain on medical therapy and are not randomized to CN (18). This design directly integrates modern prognostic scoring and dynamic response assessment to narrow the CN candidate pool to biologically favourable or at least non‑rapidly progressive disease (18,53,93).

SEVURO‑CN (NCT05753839) explores the role of CN in intermediate and poor‑risk clear‑cell mRCC treated with nivolumab–ipilimumab (6,9,94). Eligible patients must have biopsy‑proven metastatic clear‑cell RCC, primary tumour in situ, ECOG 0–1, and no contraindications to ICI therapy such as uncontrolled autoimmune disease or significant cardiovascular comorbidities (6,9). Patients receive nivolumab–ipilimumab and are allocated to different CN strategies (upfront, deferred, or no CN), with OS as the primary endpoint and multiple translational endpoints including ctDNA dynamics, tumour‑infiltrating lymphocytes, and microbiome composition (6,9,93). By deliberately focusing on IMDC intermediate/poor‑risk categories and incorporating IO‑specific safety considerations, SEVURO‑CN seeks to define the incremental value of CN in a well‑characterized IO‑treated population (6,9,93).

The IVE trial (NCT07233668) randomizes clear‑cell mRCC patients with synchronous disease to immediate versus deferred CN in the setting of ipilimumab–nivolumab therapy (21,29). Inclusion criteria emphasize IMDC intermediate risk, ECOG 0–1, and technical operability, thereby excluding very poor‑risk patients and those unlikely to tolerate both surgery and IO (21,29,47). Both arms receive IO; the key variable is CN timing, echoing SURTIME but within an IO‑doublet framework (21,29). In parallel, PROBE (SWOG S1931) randomizes only responders or stable‑disease patients after an initial course of IO to continued systemic therapy alone versus systemic therapy plus CN, with OS as the primary endpoint (35,52,65). Rapid progressors and poor surgical candidates are filtered out before randomization, operationalizing the notion that CN should be reserved for IO‑responsive disease (35,52,65).

Perioperative phase II protocols further refine candidate selection. A phase II trial of neoadjuvant nivolumab–cabozantinib followed by CN and optional metastasectomy (NCT04322955) enrolls patients with locally advanced or metastatic RCC and mandates neoadjuvant IO–TKI prior to nephrectomy, focusing on resectable disease and aiming to enhance pathologic response rates (16,69,80). Similarly, a phase II study of perioperative pembrolizumab ± axitinib (NCT04370509) randomizes patients to neoadjuvant pembrolizumab versus pembrolizumab–axitinib, followed by CN and adjuvant therapy, with pathologic complete response and radiologic response as key endpoints (22,26,80). These protocols often report high rates of extensive tumour necrosis or even pT0 in the nephrectomy specimen after IO‑based therapy, particularly among patients who achieve deep radiologic responses before surgery (80,86).

Overall, IO‑era trials articulate a much narrower and more biologically conditioned indication for CN compared with earlier eras. Modern selection integrates:

- Explicit risk stratification and exclusion of very poor‑risk disease:
	- Use of IMDC and/or MSKCC scores, with randomization restricted to patients with a limited number of adverse factors (18,21,52,93).
	- Routine exclusion of patients with rapidly progressive, high‑burden disease or severe comorbidity unlikely to tolerate CN (18,21,52).
- Dynamic response‑ and burden‑based criteria:
	- Requirement for disease control or response to IO before considering CN, as in NORDIC‑SUN and PROBE (18,35,52,65).
	- Emphasis on oligometastatic patterns and high debulkability (>90% of total tumour volume) in perioperative IO trials (80,81,86).

## 3\. Survival outcomes across eras: medians, risk stratification, and the KM tail

Survival outcomes associated with CN must be interpreted in the context of evolving systemic therapy. In the cytokine era, CN combined with IFN‑α produced a clear and clinically meaningful OS benefit over IFN‑α alone, as shown in the pooled SWOG 8949 and EORTC 30947 analysis (56,58,59). Median OS improved from approximately 7–8 months to 13–17 months with CN plus IFN‑α, and Kaplan–Meier curves showed persistent separation, with a higher proportion of patients surviving beyond three and five years in the CN arms (56,58,59,72). These findings were robust enough to establish upfront CN as standard for fit patients in the pre‑TKI era (54,58,59).

With the introduction of TKIs, systemic therapy alone began delivering median OS in the 18–30‑month range in selected populations, and CN’s relative contribution to survival became less certain (45,56,71). CARMENA, the first randomized phase III trial of CN in the TKI era, demonstrated that sunitinib alone was non‑inferior to CN followed by sunitinib in patients with intermediate or poor MSKCC risk (24,45,61,102). At a median follow‑up of 50.9 months, median OS was 18.4 months in the sunitinib‑alone arm and 13.9 months in the CN arm, with similar progression‑free survival and objective response rates between groups (61,102). Exploratory subgroup analyses showed no OS benefit for CN in intermediate‑risk patients and a tendency towards worse outcomes in poor‑risk patients, leading to the conclusion that routine upfront CN is not advisable in patients requiring immediate systemic therapy, particularly those with adverse risk profiles (61,67,103,45).

SURTIME addressed timing and biological selection by randomizing synchronous mRCC patients to immediate CN followed by sunitinib or three cycles of sunitinib followed by optional deferred CN (24,62,97,101). Although the trial was underpowered for its progression‑free rate primary endpoint, OS was significantly longer in the deferred‑CN arm (median 32.4 months, 95% CI 14.5–65.3) compared with the immediate‑CN arm (median 15.0 months, 95% CI 9.3–29.5; HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.34–0.95) (97,101). Notably, a higher proportion of patients in the deferred arm actually received systemic therapy, and roughly one‑third were appropriately spared CN due to early progression, thereby avoiding the morbidity of futile surgery (97,101,110). These results supported a strategy of initial systemic therapy with CN reserved for non‑progressors, improving both median OS and the distribution of long‑term survivors in selected TKI‑treated populations (24,62,97).

Beyond single trials, an individual‑patient‑data meta‑analysis in the targeted therapy era found that CN combined with TKIs improved long‑term survival, with a five‑year life‑expectancy ratio of approximately 1.48 and an absolute 5‑year life‑expectancy gain around 9.4 months (71). However, the benefit was concentrated in favourable and intermediate‑risk patients with limited metastatic burden; poor‑risk patients derived little or no durable survival improvement from CN (71). Kaplan–Meier curves from these pooled analyses showed a visibly “fatter” right‑hand tail among CN recipients in appropriate risk strata, reinforcing the notion that CN primarily benefits those with more favourable disease biology (69,71,74).

In the IO era, systemic therapy alone has markedly improved outcomes. Dual ICI regimens and IO–TKI combinations yield 3‑ to 4‑year OS rates of approximately 50% or more in intermediate‑risk mRCC, and durable complete responses in a minority (61,74,93). IO‑era CN trials are designed with this elevated baseline in mind. PROBE (SWOG S1931), for instance, assumes median OS of 25 months in the IO‑only arm and is powered to detect a 47% improvement (HR 0.68) with CN in IO‑responsive patients (65). NORDIC‑SUN, SEVURO‑CN, and IVE all use OS or progression‑free survival as primary endpoints and restrict CN to subsets defined by IMDC risk, disease control, and surgical feasibility (18,6,21,35,52). Because results are pending, current evidence relies on retrospective IO‑CN series and deferred‑nephrectomy cohorts. These studies report median OS in the 24–46‑month range and suggest that patients undergoing CN after robust IO response have longer OS and more prolonged treatment‑free intervals than similar responders managed without CN, although confounding by selection remains substantial (80,86,90,104,108).

Taken together, survival outcomes across eras reflect three fundamental patterns:

- Era‑specific shifts in CN’s absolute contribution to OS:
	- Large absolute benefit on a low baseline in the cytokine era (56,58,59).
	- Neutral or negative impact of upfront CN in intermediate/poor‑risk TKI‑treated patients (24,61,67,102).
- Growing importance of timing and biologic selection:
	- Improved OS with deferred CN in SURTIME underscores the value of selecting non‑progressors for surgery (24,62,97,101).
	- IO‑era trials condition CN on IO response, anticipating that benefit will be restricted to responder/stable disease subsets (18,35,52,65,80,109).

## 4\. Clinicopathologic features of long‑term survivors: CN versus non‑CN “tail phenotypes”

Long‑term survivors (>5 years) in mRCC represent a distinct minority whose clinical and pathological features have been increasingly characterized in the CN and systemic‑therapy literature (69,71,81,85,88). These “tail phenotypes” are defined by favourable or intermediate prognostic profiles, low metastatic burden, clear‑cell histology, and deep responses to systemic therapy; CN appears to amplify the probability of joining this tail primarily in such patients rather than rescuing poor‑risk biology (69,71,80,86,90).

Risk stratification using IMDC or Motzer models reveals that most long‑term survivors fall into favourable or intermediate categories, with 0–2 adverse risk factors (71,81,85,88). In contemporary CN cohorts, upwards of 80% of patients have one to two IMDC risk factors, and long‑term survival is almost exclusively seen in this group, whereas poor‑risk patients rarely survive beyond five years regardless of surgical intervention (71,81,88,91). Prognostic factor analyses in systemic‑therapy–only cohorts mirror these findings, with long‑term survivors exhibiting good performance status, normal haemoglobin, and fewer adverse laboratory parameters (85,90,91).

Metastatic burden and distribution are key discriminants. Studies in the TKI era show that patients with oligometastatic disease—often ≤2 organ sites and lung‑predominant or nodal‑only involvement—have five‑year OS rates in the 25–40% range when treated with CN plus systemic therapy, whereas those with ≥3 organ sites have markedly lower long‑term survival (69,71,85,88). Surgical series emphasize that the fraction of total tumour volume that can be removed is critical; patients in whom >90% of tumour volume (including the primary and dominant metastases) can be resected demonstrate superior disease‑specific survival (81,84). IO‑era deferred CN cohorts similarly report that oligometastatic disease and high debulkability are over‑represented among patients who discontinue systemic therapy after CN and remain in durable remission (80,86,90).

Histologic subtype and sarcomatoid features further refine the long‑term phenotype. Clear‑cell RCC predominates among long‑term survivors in both CN and non‑CN cohorts, consistent with the higher sensitivity of this subtype to TKIs and ICIs (85,88,93). Sarcomatoid differentiation is generally associated with aggressive behaviour and poor prognosis, yet IO‑era data indicate that a minority of sarcomatoid‑rich tumours can achieve dramatic responses to ICIs (74,77). In such cases, deferred CN often reveals extensive necrosis or even complete pathological response in the primary tumour, and some of these patients contribute to the tail of long‑term survivors (77,86,93). However, even in these favourable responders, sarcomatoid‑positive long‑term survivors remain rare, underscoring that CN cannot fully overcome intrinsically aggressive tumour biology (74,88).

Depth of systemic response is increasingly recognized as a major determinant of tail membership. In targeted‑therapy IPD meta‑analyses, patients who achieved partial or complete responses to TKIs and underwent CN had the most favourable long‑term outcomes, while those with progressive disease despite surgery derived little benefit (71). IO‑era deferred CN studies report that most patients selected for nephrectomy after ICI or IO–TKI therapy exhibit at least partial radiologic response, with pathology often demonstrating ≥95% necrosis or pT0 in the nephrectomy specimen (80,86,90). These patients frequently discontinue systemic therapy after CN and remain progression‑free at follow‑up, suggesting a functional‑cure phenotype in a subset (80,86). By contrast, IO‑only long‑term survivors commonly have smaller baseline tumour burden or have had prior nephrectomy outside of a CN paradigm, and their pathologic status is less well characterized (77,90,108).

Comparative analyses highlight both overlap and divergence between CN and non‑CN tail phenotypes. Both groups are enriched for favourable/intermediate risk, low metastatic burden, clear‑cell histology, and deep systemic responses (71,85,88,90). However, CN‑treated long‑term survivors often have had substantial in situ primary tumour at baseline and achieve surgical clearance of residual disease, sometimes with near‑total pathologic response, enabling longer treatment‑free intervals (80,86). Non‑CN long‑term survivors, on the other hand, tend to rely on durable systemic control alone and may retain small residual lesions with ongoing surveillance (77,90,108).

- Shared features of long‑term survivors with and without CN:
	- Favourable/intermediate IMDC or Motzer risk, with 0–2 adverse factors (71,81,85,88,90).
	- Oligometastatic, debulkable disease and clear‑cell histology, with sarcomatoid differentiation uncommon among >5‑year survivors (69,71,85,88,90).
- Distinctive features of CN‑associated tail phenotypes:
	- Ability to resect >90% of tumour volume and achieve high rates of pathologic near‑complete response after IO‑based therapy (80,81,86).
	- Higher likelihood of prolonged treatment‑free survival after CN, particularly in IO responders selected for deferred surgery (80,86,90,104,108).

## 5\. Future directions: integrating era‑specific evidence into precision CN strategies

The cumulative evidence from cytokine, TKI, and IO eras indicates that CN is neither universally beneficial nor obsolete; instead, it occupies a targeted role in carefully selected patients with favourable or intermediate risk, limited metastatic burden, and demonstrable sensitivity to systemic therapy (24,45,71,93,104–107). Translating this into practice requires the development of precision CN strategies that integrate clinical, pathological, and molecular information with era‑appropriate systemic backbones.

One priority is to formalize “cure‑fraction” and long‑term survival modeling using mixture‑cure and landmark analyses applied to patient‑level data from randomized and high‑quality observational studies. Such models can estimate how CN modifies both early hazard (perioperative risk, delayed systemic therapy) and late benefits (durable remission) after adjusting for IMDC risk, metastatic pattern, and response depth (71,75,90,109). Landmark analyses at 6–12 months of systemic therapy could stratify patients by response category (complete response, partial response, stable disease, progression) and compare outcomes between those who proceed to CN and those who continue medical management only (62,80,90,109). These approaches may clarify the incremental contribution of CN to the tail of the survival curve in IO‑treated populations beyond what standard Kaplan–Meier comparisons can demonstrate (71,75).

A second priority is integrating translational biomarkers into IO‑CN trials. Serial ctDNA measurements, immune profiling of tumour and blood, and radiomic signatures from imaging could help identify patients most likely to benefit from CN (9,18,35,80,86,93,108). For instance, early and sustained ctDNA clearance in IO responders may signal minimal residual disease and a high probability of benefit from consolidative CN, whereas persistent ctDNA despite partial radiologic response might argue against surgery alone and in favour of continued or intensified systemic therapy (80,86,93). Prospective IO‑CN trials such as SEVURO‑CN, NORDIC‑SUN, and PROBE are well positioned to embed such biomarker programs and link them to long‑term outcomes (18,6,35,52,65,93).

Third, patient selection algorithms should incorporate objective measures of metastatic burden and debulkability, moving beyond broad descriptors such as “oligometastatic” to quantitative metrics (69,81,88). Tools that integrate number and size of metastases, organ distribution, and proportion of tumour volume resectable with acceptable morbidity could be combined with IMDC risk and response depth to generate a CN candidacy score (69,71,81,88,90). This score could then guide multidisciplinary decision‑making, with curative‑intent CN and metastasectomy reserved for patients meeting stringent thresholds and consolidation of IO response prioritized over symptomatic or prophylactic nephrectomy in high‑burden disease (24,35,45,98,103).

Fourth, special subgroups such as sarcomatoid‑rich and non‑clear‑cell RCC warrant dedicated study within IO‑CN frameworks. Sarcomatoid tumours, while aggressive, can show profound responses to ICIs, and early reports suggest that CN in the setting of deep IO response may lead to extensive necrosis and durable control in select cases (74,77,86,93,108). Stratified or biomarker‑enriched cohorts within PROBE‑like and NORDIC‑SUN‑like trials could clarify whether CN adds value beyond IO alone in these subtypes (18,35,52,93,108).

Finally, future research should pay greater attention to patient‑centred outcomes and health‑economic considerations. CN carries perioperative risks and may worsen long‑term renal function, potentially affecting quality of life and eligibility for subsequent therapies (24,56,74,80). Evaluating treatment‑free survival, symptom burden, renal function trajectories, and economic impact across CN and non‑CN strategies will be crucial to determining where CN offers net benefit from both patient and health‑system perspectives (56,74,80,90,98,109). As data from PROBE, NORDIC‑SUN, SEVURO‑CN, and IVE mature, harmonized analyses and external validation in real‑world registries will be necessary to convert trial findings into robust, implementable algorithms (18,35,45,52,65,93,104,108).

- Key research and implementation priorities:
	- Develop and validate composite CN selection tools incorporating IMDC risk, metastatic burden, response depth, debulkability, and biomarkers, calibrated across cytokine, TKI, and IO‑era datasets (69,71,81,88,93,104–107,109).
	- Embed cure‑fraction modeling, translational endpoints, and patient‑centred outcomes into IO‑CN trials, linking CN decisions to long‑term survival, treatment‑free time, quality of life, and health‑system impact (18,35,52,65,80,90,93,98,108,109).
