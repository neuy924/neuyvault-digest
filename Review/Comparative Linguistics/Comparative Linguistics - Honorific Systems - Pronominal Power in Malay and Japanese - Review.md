## 1. Introduction: The Cartography of Social Distance

The comparative study of Malay (*Bahasa Melayu*) and Japanese (*Nihongo*) reveals a sophisticated socio-linguistic infrastructure designed to map and enforce power dynamics. Both languages function as "high-context" systems where meaning is heavily dependent on the relative status of the interlocutors. In these frameworks, language is not merely a tool for communication but a mechanism for the continuous calibration of social distance, obligation, and hierarchy.

### 1.1 The Concept of "Face" and Linguistic Governance

In both cultures, the preservation of "Face" (*Maru/Menzu*) is the primary driver of linguistic choice. Malay *Adab* (refinement/etiquette) and Japanese *Keigo* (honorifics) serve as the formal protocols for this governance. While Western linguistic structures often prioritize clarity and individual agency, Malay and Japanese prioritize the "Social Self," where the "I" only exists in relation to a specific "Other."

### 1.2 Feudal Residue and Modern Syntax

The modern power structures within these languages are artifacts of their feudal histories—the Melaka Sultanate and the Edo Shogunate, respectively. The transition from absolute monarchy to modern bureaucracy has not erased these hierarchies; instead, it has "nominalized" them, transforming overt power into subtle syntactic choices that dictate who may speak, how they may speak, and the degree of deference required.

## 2. Pronominal Avoidance and the "Hole" in the Subject

A defining characteristic of both systems is the inherent "danger" of the second-person pronoun ("You"). Direct address is frequently viewed as a linguistic assault, leading to a system of pronominal avoidance that effectively erases the subject-object distinction in favor of role-based identification.

### 2.1 Pro-Drop and Zero-Pronoun Existence

Both languages are "radical" pro-drop systems. The omission of the subject is not merely an abbreviation; it is a strategic erasure. By speaking in a "Zero-Pronoun" state, the speaker bypasses the risk of misidentifying rank.

| Linguistic Feature | Japanese Application | Malay Application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direct "You"** | *Anata* (Often avoided/condescending) | *Kamu/Engkau* (Restricted to inferiors/peers) |
| **Substitution** | Use of Title/Position (*Sensei, Shachō*) | Use of Title/Kinship (*Tuan, Cikgu, Abang*) |
| **Result** | **De-personalization** of the interaction. | **Relational** anchoring of the interaction. |

### 2.2 Semantic Bleaching and Honorific Descent

A shared historical phenomenon is the "bleaching" of honorifics, where once-high pronouns descend into common or even derogatory usage. The Japanese *Omae* (originally "the one before me") and the Malay *Awak* (originally palace-usage) have shifted from high-deference terms to casual or downward-facing addresses. This creates a perpetual linguistic arms race where new layers of "High" language must be constructed to maintain the sacred distance between ranks.

## 3. Honorific Architectures: Keigo vs. Adab

While both systems aim to establish hierarchy, the mechanisms differ in their focus. Japanese *Keigo* is an institutionalized, grammaticalized system, whereas Malay *Adab* is a moral and situational system of refinement.

### 3.1 Japanese Keigo: The Institutional Valve

Japanese power is expressed through three primary registers: *Sonkeigo* (Exalted), *Kenjōgo* (Humble), and *Teineigo* (Polite). The "Humble" form is particularly significant in power dynamics, as it requires the speaker to linguistically "lower" themselves or their in-group to elevate the "out-group" (Master/Customer).

### 3.2 Malay Adab: The Refined/Coarse Axis

Malay power dynamics operate on the axis of *Halus* (Refined) and *Kasar* (Coarse). Unlike the rigid verb conjugations of Japanese, Malay power is articulated through pronominal selection and the use of *Bahasa Istana* (Palace Language) or *Bahasa Halus*. The use of *Hamba* (Slave/Servant) as a first-person pronoun is not merely a sign of low status but a mark of high *Adab*—a paradoxical "refinement through self-abasement."

---

## 4. The Grammar of Agency: Causatives and Passives

The ability to assign or deflect agency is a critical component of linguistic power. In both systems, the Dominant party possesses the "Active Voice," while the subordinate is linguistically "acted upon."

### 4.1 Japanese Causative-Passive (Shiyeki Ukemi)

The Japanese causative-passive (*~saserareru*) is the ultimate tool for documenting relinquishment of will. It expresses that one was "made to do" something by a superior. In a power structure, forcing a subordinate to use this form removes their ability to claim intent or error; their failure is framed as a "caused" event rather than a chosen one.

### 4.2 Malay Passive and Nominalization

Malay power structures utilize the *Pe-...-an* nominalization and the passive *Di-* prefix to remove the "actor." By saying *"Lantai sudah dicuci"* (The floor has been cleaned) instead of *"Saya sudah cuci lantai"* (I have cleaned the floor), the servant erases their own agency. The action is presented as a completed state, satisfying the Master’s requirement without centering the servant’s effort.

---

## 5. The Sociolinguistics of Shame and Compliance

Both cultures utilize "Face" management as a form of social control. The concepts of *Haji* (Japanese) and *Malu* (Malay) are the emotional anchors for their respective linguistic power structures.

### 5.1 Institutional Disgrace vs. Moral Exposure

In Japan, *Haji* is often institutional; it is the shame of failing one's role or office. The language of *Haji* focuses on incompetence and protocol failure. In Malaysia, *Malu* is more moral and personal; it is the shame of being unrefined or "exposed" as *Kasar*. Linguistic humiliation in Malay involves stripping away the submissive's *Adab* to reveal their "coarse" animal nature.

### 5.2 The "Linguistic Straitjacket"

When these systems are maximized, they create a "Linguistic Straitjacket." The submissive must audit every syllable to ensure it fits the Master’s required register. This constant monitoring creates a massive cognitive load, effectively paralyzing the submissive’s capacity for independent thought or dissent. The language itself becomes the cage, ensuring that even in silence, the hierarchy is maintained.

---

## 6. Synthesis: The Convergent Evolution of Authority

The parallel concerns of Japanese and Malay linguistic structures suggest a convergent evolution of authority in high-context Asian societies. Both systems achieve "Subjective Nullification"—the dissolution of the individual in favor of the role.

| Dynamic | Japanese Model | Malay Model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source of Power** | Institutional / Office (*Yaku*) | Moral / Character (*Budi*) |
| **Primary Mechanism** | Grammatical Conjugation (*Keigo*) | Pronominal / Lexical Choice (*Adab*) |
| **Submission State** | The **Efficient Tool** (Institutional) | The **Refined Servant** (Moral) |
| **End Goal** | **Ego-Dissolution** through Duty. | **Ego-Dissolution** through Refinement. |

The study concludes that power in these languages is not merely about who commands whom, but about the creation of a shared reality where the hierarchy is as immutable as the grammar itself.
