## 1\. Introduction: The Problem of “Object-Power” in Modern Life

Modern social life is densely populated by entities that behave, in ordinary language and lived experience, as if they possessed agency: *the market* “demands” cost reductions; *the budget* “forces” service redesign; *the protocol* “requires” a particular pathway; *the metrics* “show” what must be done. These are not merely metaphors. They are practical orientations—ways of speaking and acting that track real constraints and consequences. Yet they also pose a theoretical problem: how do human-made arrangements come to appear as objective, external powers that govern humans? This problem—call it **object-power** —is the conceptual hinge of the present review and the rationale for a narrative spine running from Marx to Weber, then to Lukács and Habermas, and finally to Polanyi.

### 1.1 The phenomenon: object-power as a social fact, not a rhetorical flourish

Object-power names a class of situations in which **things, numbers, or formal systems acquire the practical status of agents**. A hospital’s waiting list target becomes a force that reorganizes staffing; a credit score becomes a gatekeeper of life chances; a price index becomes a justification for wage suppression; an algorithmic ranking becomes an authority that structures visibility and income. In each case, the “object” is not inert. It participates in causal chains and institutional decision-making. Yet its power is always mediated by social relations: ownership, authority, legitimacy, administrative procedures, political decisions, and shared beliefs about what counts as evidence or compliance.

Two clarifications prevent immediate confusion. First, object-power is not the claim that objects literally have intentions. It is a claim about **how agency is distributed and experienced** in complex systems: agency can be delegated to procedures, encoded in infrastructures, and stabilized by rules such that outcomes appear to flow from “the system” rather than from contestable human choices. Second, object-power is not reducible to “ideology” in the sense of propaganda or false belief. People who say “the numbers require it” may be tracking genuine resource constraints. The theoretical task is to explain why those constraints take the form of **apparently objective necessities**, why they are often resistant to moral appeal, and why they can be experienced as *natural* even when historically produced.

### 1.2 Working definitions: distinguishing the main conceptual tools

Because the terms used to diagnose object-power travel across traditions, a set of operational definitions is useful. The following table isolates how each key concept will function in this review (as analytic instruments rather than as exhaustive reconstructions of each author’s corpus).

| Concept | Primary question it answers | “Object-power” appears as… | What it tends to foreground |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Fetishism** (Marx) | Why do social relations appear as properties of things? | commodities/money/metrics as if value or necessity inheres in them | the value-form; exchange mediation; “necessary appearance” |
| **Rationalization / disenchantment** (Weber) | Why do rules, calculation, and bureaucracy expand—and what do they do to meaning? | procedures/administration as impersonal authority | formal rationality; bureaucracy; predictability; loss/privatization of meaning |
| **Reification** (Lukács) | How does the commodity form shape consciousness and social ontology? | the world as a thing-system (“second nature”) | fragmentation; calculability; totality as antidote |
| **System vs lifeworld** (Habermas) | How do money/power steer action, and when do they invade meaning-rich domains? | metrics/markets/administration colonizing communicative spaces | communicative rationality; steering media; colonization |
| **Embeddedness / disembedding** (Polanyi) | Under what historical conditions do markets become sovereign over society? | “market necessity” as political destiny | market society; fictitious commodities; double movement |

These concepts overlap, but their overlaps are productive rather than redundant. Marx clarifies how *economic forms* generate objectified appearances; Weber explains the expansion of *administrative calculability* and its cultural consequences; Lukács theorizes the internalization and generalization of thing-form into experience; Habermas offers a normative-diagnostic framework for the invasion of communicative life by system imperatives; Polanyi supplies a historical political economy of when “market logic” becomes disembedded and therefore more likely to present itself as necessity.

### 1.3 Method: why a conceptual spine, and why this sequence

A review with pedagogical aims must decide whether to proceed chronologically, conceptually, or by contemporary case studies. The present approach is **conceptual with a chronological spine**, because the object-power problem is cumulative: later theorists frequently inherit, transform, and contest earlier diagnoses. Marx provides the clearest “template” in commodity fetishism: the appearance of objective necessity is not an error to be corrected by better ideas alone but a pattern generated by social organization. Weber then explains how modern institutions intensify object-power through formal rationality and bureaucratic rule. Lukács fuses these insights into a theory of reified consciousness and the fragmentation of experience. Habermas reframes the problem as a conflict between system steering and lifeworld meaning, allowing a more explicit normative vocabulary. Polanyi anchors the discussion historically: market sovereignty is not timeless; it is politically made and periodically countered.

This sequence is not a claim that these thinkers agree. It is a pedagogical strategy: the reader first learns how “things become powerful” in political economy (Marx), then how rule-systems deepen the impersonality of that power (Weber), then how the thing-world becomes a lived ontology (Lukács), then how it colonizes communicative domains (Habermas), and finally how these patterns intensify or weaken with disembedding/embeddedness dynamics (Polanyi).

### 1.4 Roadmap: what will be explained (and what will be bracketed)

The sections that follow will treat object-power as a family of mechanisms rather than a single cause. Marx will ground the analysis in the value-form and the logic of fetish-character; Weber will supply the institutional sociology of calculative domination; Lukács and Habermas will translate these into theories of consciousness, normativity, and social integration; Polanyi will explain why market imperatives sometimes become culturally hegemonic as “necessity,” and why societies repeatedly attempt to re-embed them. Throughout, contemporary examples (metrics, audit culture, algorithmic governance, financialization) will be used not as mere illustrations but as test sites for conceptual distinctions—especially the difference between **constraint** (real limits) and **naturalized necessity** (limits treated as intrinsic properties of numbers or systems).

## 2\. Marx: Commodity Fetishism as the Prototype of Social Relations Appearing as Things

Marx’s account of commodity fetishism in *Capital* is not an ornamental aside about consumer “obsessions.” It is a theory of how a specific social organization of production and exchange generates **systematic appearances** —forms of everyday objectivity in which social relations take on the guise of properties of things. The pedagogical difficulty is that Marx is diagnosing something that feels *obvious* and *natural* in capitalist life: of course objects have prices; of course wages look like the “value” of labor; of course budgets appear as hard constraints; of course “the market” seems to punish or reward. Fetishism is precisely the name for this *obviousness* when it is, in Marx’s analysis, a historically produced form of appearance.

### 2.1 The value-form and “necessary appearance”: why exchange makes social relations look object-like

The commodity is Marx’s key unit because it condenses a social riddle into a mundane object. A commodity has a **use-value** (it is good for something) and an **exchange-value** (it can be exchanged in definite quantitative ratios). The enigma begins when exchange-value presents itself as if it were grounded in the commodity’s material body, even though material properties do not by themselves explain why one item exchanges for another at a given rate. Marx’s wager is that what exchange-value “expresses” is not physics but a **social relation**: the way human labor is organized, compared, and validated in a system where production is privately undertaken and coordinated *after the fact* through exchange.

This is why the fetish-character is not a psychological accident. In a world where producers do not directly coordinate their labors as a common plan, “sociality” is achieved indirectly: only through successful exchange does a product count as socially necessary labor. The consequence is epistemic as well as economic. Social relations become legible primarily in the **object-language** of prices, wages, and monetary flows. Under such conditions, it is not surprising that the commodity seems to “have” value the way it has weight, or that money seems to “contain” value the way a vessel contains water. Marx calls this an inversion: human relations become visible only in a form that hides their human character.

A useful way to teach this point is to distinguish **constraint** from **reification of constraint**. Constraints are real: resources are limited, time is finite, coordination is hard. Fetishism begins when the form those constraints take—money-prices, wages, interest rates—presents itself as an objective necessity that belongs to things, rather than as a historically specific mode of social mediation.

### 2.2 Close reading of Capital Ch. 1 §4: the fetish-character of the commodity

Marx’s §4 argument proceeds by exclusion and then reconstruction. First, he denies that the “mystery” of commodities can be explained by their physical properties. Second, he identifies the source of the mystery in the **social form** of labor under commodity production. The “secret” is not in the wood of the table but in the relational network that makes tables commensurable with linen, labor with money, and heterogeneous activities comparable through an abstract metric.

Pedagogically, §4 can be read as describing a social world in which the most consequential relations are **indirect**. Producers encounter one another not primarily as cooperative agents but as owners of things. Their interdependence is real, yet it is mediated by exchange such that the social character of labor appears as a property of products. What looks like a neutral numerical fact (“this is worth that”) is, in Marx’s diagnosis, the visible surface of a deeper social relation—one that includes power, property, and compulsion, even when no single person is “ordering” the result.

### 2.3 Marx’s analogical toolkit: religious analogies vs non-religious control cases

Marx famously uses religion as an analogy because it models a recognizable inversion: humans create gods, then treat them as independent beings that govern humans. The point is not that capitalism is “a religion” in the same way; the point is that both domains exhibit a structure where human products confront humans as autonomous powers. Religion supplies a vocabulary for **object-agency** —the sense that our creations become alien authorities.

But Marx also uses non-religious “control cases” (Robinson Crusoe, the peasant household, the association of free producers) to show what changes when social labor is coordinated transparently rather than through exchange. These cases are not utopias; they are analytic contrasts. They help isolate the fetish effect by presenting situations where the relation between labor and product is directly intelligible—where accounting does not require the commodity form to translate activities into a universal equivalent.

The two toolkits therefore teach different lessons:

| Marx’s device | What it clarifies | What it makes vivid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Religious analogy** | Inversion of agency; human products as alien powers | The lived feel of “things commanding” (mystical veil, object authority) |
| **Non-religious control cases** | How mediation changes intelligibility | Why exchange-based coordination produces opacity and naturalized necessity |

The upshot is methodological: Marx does not rely on metaphor alone. He uses metaphor to capture phenomenology (how it feels), and control cases to isolate mechanism (how it is generated).

### 2.4 Beyond the commodity: money fetishism and the escalation of objectivity

If the commodity already encourages value to appear thing-like, money intensifies the effect by offering a universal equivalent. Money makes heterogeneous goods commensurable in a single numerical register; it becomes the privileged surface where social relations show themselves. This is why money so readily appears to “do” things—make things happen, produce outcomes, compel behavior. The fetish deepens as the mediation becomes more abstract: commodity → money → capital. With each step, social relations become more powerful and more opaque, and their objectified form becomes more difficult to treat as contingent.

This escalation matters for the broader “object-power” problem introduced in Section 1. Modern institutions often experience budgets, prices, and performance metrics as *hard facts* that dictate action. Marx’s contribution is to show why such facts acquire an aura of inevitability: in commodity-mediated life, social coordination is written in the grammar of things and numbers. The fetish-character is not merely an illusion to be dispelled; it is a socially produced form of objectivity that structures what appears actionable, negotiable, or even thinkable.

## 3\. Weber: Rationalization, Disenchantment, and the Administrative “Iron Cage”

If Marx explains why social relations in a commodity economy appear as **thing-like necessities** —prices, money, and “the market” as quasi-objective forces—Weber explains why modern societies increasingly govern themselves through **procedures** that treat the world as administrable. Where Marx’s object-power often crystallizes around commodities and monetary metrics, Weber’s object-power takes the form of **rule-systems**: bureaucracy, calculability, auditability, and the spread of rational–legal authority. The result is not simply more “organization,” but a specific kind of organization—one that privileges formalized means–end calculation and tends to relocate questions of meaning and value into domains that lack institutional force.

### 3.1 Rational–legal authority and bureaucracy as coordination technology

Weber’s sociology of domination is anchored in the claim that modernity increasingly legitimates authority on **rational–legal** grounds: obedience is owed not to a person’s lineage (traditional authority) or to extraordinary charisma (charismatic authority), but to **rules** and offices. Bureaucracy is the organizational form that best embodies this logic. It is “rational” in the sense that it is designed for technical efficiency: specialized roles, hierarchical supervision, written records, procedural continuity, and predictability.

For pedagogical purposes, it is crucial to treat bureaucracy as a **technology of coordination** rather than merely a pathology. Large-scale systems—health services, universities, taxation, welfare, rail networks—cannot function at scale without routinization. The Weberian point is not “bureaucracy is irrational,” but rather: once rule-governed administration becomes dominant, it introduces a distinctive kind of constraint. Decisions can be justified as rule-compliance even when their value-content is contested. In such environments, power often operates through a phrase that sounds innocently neutral: “that’s the procedure.”

This is one reason Weber is indispensable for the object-power problem. Bureaucracy produces constraints that feel *objective* because they are impersonal and repeatable. One can appeal to compassion or context, but the system’s strength lies precisely in its capacity to remain indifferent to context. The individual encounters not a person but a **rule-world**.

### 3.2 Weber’s four rationalities and the drift toward formal rationality

To understand why bureaucratic constraint expands, Weber (and standard interpretations of Weber) distinguish multiple senses of rationality. The most useful pedagogical map is the fourfold typology: **practical, theoretical, substantive, and formal**. The typology matters because it reveals a modern drift: institutions increasingly prioritize **formal rationality** —calculation and rule-consistency—over **substantive rationality**, which is oriented to values and ends.

| Rationality type | Guiding question | Typical institutional expression | Characteristic risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Practical | “What works here and now?” | ad hoc judgment, local problem-solving | short-termism; inconsistency |
| Theoretical | “How does this fit into an explanatory system?” | science, models, classification | abstraction detached from life |
| Substantive | “What is good/just/meaningful?” | ethics, religion, political ideals | value conflict; pluralism |
| Formal | “What follows from rules and calculation?” | bureaucracy, accounting, metrics, law | proceduralism; loss of meaning |

The drift toward formal rationality is not merely cultural preference; it is an institutional selection effect. Formal rationality scales: it allows coordination among strangers, across time, with accountability. Substantive rationality does not scale as easily because it depends on contested values and interpretive judgment. Hence modern institutions repeatedly translate substantive questions (“What care is owed?” “What is fair?”) into formal proxies (eligibility criteria, KPIs, scoring systems). The proxies then develop their own authority. The number becomes not a representation of value but a substitute for value.

### 3.3 Disenchantment: technique displacing the sacred without replacing meaning

Weber’s notion of **disenchantment** (Entzauberung) is often misunderstood as a claim that modern people are simply less religious. More precisely, disenchantment refers to a transformation in the cultural conditions of plausibility: the world becomes increasingly conceived as **knowable, predictable, and controllable** through technique. This shift does not eliminate meaning; it changes where meaning can be authoritatively located. In a disenchanted world, meaning becomes privatized or pluralized—located in personal conviction, lifestyle, or subculture—while public coordination is increasingly carried out through formal systems.

Disenchantment is therefore not a synonym for nihilism. It names a structural tension: modern societies can become brilliantly competent at controlling means (efficiency, throughput, risk management) while becoming institutionally clumsy at articulating ends (what counts as a good life, a good death, a just distribution, a dignified relationship). The difficulty is that ends-talk is “irrational” in the narrow sense of not being formally calculable. When this happens, the language of administration expands into domains where it does not belong, producing familiar distortions: care becomes a throughput problem, education becomes a ranking problem, justice becomes a compliance problem.

### 3.4 Triangulation with Marx: market compulsion vs bureaucratic constraint

Here the Marx–Weber triangulation becomes pedagogically sharp. Marx shows how “objective necessity” is produced by the **value-form**: market mediation makes social relations appear thing-like. Weber shows how objective necessity is produced by **rational–legal systems**: bureaucratic mediation makes decisions appear rule-like. In modern institutions, these mediations frequently interlock. Market pressures demand cost containment; bureaucracies implement containment through procedures and metrics. The resulting constraint can feel doubly objective: “the market requires it” (Marxian register) and “the policy mandates it” (Weberian register).

The analytic payoff is to distinguish two modes of compulsion that can be easily conflated:

- **Market compulsion (Marx):** the pressure generated by competitive exchange, profitability, and the commodification of labor and goods; necessity presents itself as economic law.
- **Administrative compulsion (Weber):** the pressure generated by rule-governed systems that privilege formal rationality; necessity presents itself as procedural law.

Both are real. Both produce object-power. But they are not identical, and their remedies differ: reducing market compulsion through social protection may increase administrative compulsion; loosening bureaucracy may intensify exposure to market volatility. Weber’s contribution is to make this trade-off legible: the “iron cage” is not merely capitalism; it is the broader modern architecture of formal rationality that can persist even when markets are tamed.

In the next section, this convergence becomes the explicit focus: metrics and numbers as the hinge where Marx’s fetish-character of social objectivity meets Weber’s rationalized administration—where “the numbers” become both market signals and bureaucratic commands.

## 4\. Marx + Weber Together: Metrics as the Meeting Point of Market and Bureaucracy

Numbers are not merely representations. In modern institutions they are **instruments of coordination**, and often **instruments of domination** —not because arithmetic is sinister, but because quantified forms allow decisions to travel across distance, time, and organizational layers with minimal need for interpretive agreement. This is precisely why metrics become the privileged site where Marx’s fetish-character of objectivity and Weber’s rationalized administration converge. The same spreadsheet cell can simultaneously function as a *market translation* (a monetary constraint expressed as cost, price, margin) and an *administrative command* (a target, threshold, or compliance condition). The practical result is what Section 1 called **object-power**: the experience that “the numbers require it.”

### 4.1 Numbers as steering devices: when calculation becomes governance

Weber’s contribution makes it clear that quantified indicators are not epiphenomena; they are governance technologies. Formal rationality prefers objects that can be compared, audited, and made legible to distant authorities. Metrics do this by converting heterogeneous realities—clinical complexity, educational growth, care quality, safety, dignity—into commensurable units. Once converted, these units can be inserted into bureaucratic circuits: they can be tracked, rewarded, punished, escalated, budgeted.

A central teaching point is that the *political* and *moral* work of metrics occurs **before** the number appears: selecting the variable, defining the denominator, deciding the acceptable range, choosing what counts as success, and establishing what consequences attach to deviation. Formal rationality then treats these prior judgments as a settled background and foregrounds the number as objective evidence. This is how metrics acquire authority: the system’s legitimacy is displaced from deliberation about ends to compliance with means.

### 4.2 Marxian fetishism in quantified form: prices, budgets, and KPIs as “thing-agency”

Marx’s framework explains why numerical constraints often appear as properties of things rather than expressions of social relations. In commodity society, value is mediated through exchange; money becomes the universal expression of social labor; and budgets appear as “hard limits” that compel action. The fetish-character is intensified by quantification because numbers are excellent at **concealing their own social genesis**. They look like neutral facts, particularly when expressed as prices, tariffs, “cost per case,” or productivity targets.

To see the fetish dynamic, one can perform a simple diagnostic translation. Take the statement: “We must restructure because the numbers don’t work.” A Marxian reading asks: which social relations are being condensed and hidden by “the numbers”? Ownership and funding regimes, wage relations, procurement contracts, pricing systems, bargaining power, and political choices about allocation. The fetish occurs when the condensed product (the number) is treated as the cause rather than as the **form of appearance** of a social relation.

Importantly, Marx’s fetishism is not refuted by the fact that budgets are real. Budgets are real constraints—but their authority is socially produced. The fetishistic move is to treat their authority as *natural* and their categories as *self-evident*, rather than as outcomes of historically specific forms of organization and power.

### 4.3 Weberian rationalization in quantified form: auditability, standardization, compliance

Weber’s framework clarifies why the modern institution is drawn to metrics even when they distort reality. Metrics generate **auditability**, and auditability generates governability. Once performance is rendered in a number, it becomes possible to compare units, incentivize behavior, and justify decisions as rule-consistent. In this sense, metrics are ideal bureaucratic objects: portable, impersonal, and seemingly fair.

This is the deep reason why “procedural compliance” so often substitutes for substantive judgment. When a system is built to defend itself against accusations of arbitrariness, it prefers formal criteria that can be documented. Numbers serve as a moral technology: they allow the institution to say, in effect, “it is not us; it is the rule.” The individual then encounters the administrative constraint not as someone’s decision but as an impersonal necessity.

### 4.4 Case illustrations: three contemporary scenes of metric object-power

To teach the Marx–Weber convergence, it helps to examine where “the numbers” simultaneously express market constraints and bureaucratic commands.

| Scene | The metric object | Marxian reading (fetish-character) | Weberian reading (formal rationality) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Healthcare throughput | waiting list targets, cost per case, LOS | budgetary categories appear as natural constraints; “efficiency” seems intrinsic to the number | audit culture; standardization; compliance regimes; defensible decision-making |
| Platform labor | ratings, acceptance rate, algorithmic rank | income compulsion appears as “the algorithm”; market dependence is mediated by a numeric score | rule-system without a face; automated bureaucracy; legibility for enforcement |
| Credit and housing | credit score, interest rate, affordability index | life chances appear as properties of a number; social inequalities become “risk” | formal eligibility criteria; impersonal gatekeeping; procedural justification |

In each scene, the metric performs a dual role. It is *economic* insofar as it translates social relations into monetary equivalents and constraints. It is *bureaucratic* insofar as it converts those constraints into enforceable procedures. The result is a potent hybrid: fetishized objectivity administered through rational–legal form.

### 4.5 A practical distinction: constraint vs naturalized necessity

The central pedagogical payoff of bringing Marx and Weber together is to explain why metric authority is often experienced as non-negotiable. The institution does not merely *use* numbers; it becomes **organized around** them. Yet it is crucial to separate:

- **Constraint**: limited resources, coordination problems, trade-offs.
- **Naturalized necessity**: the belief that the specific metric categories and thresholds are inevitable, value-neutral, and self-justifying.

Marx helps identify naturalization through the fetish-character: numbers appear to *contain* necessity. Weber helps identify naturalization through formal rationality: numbers appear to *embody* fairness and procedure. Combined, they explain why objections framed in substantive terms (“this harms care,” “this violates dignity,” “this is unjust”) often fail to move institutions whose legitimacy is anchored in calculability and compliance.

This convergence sets the stage for Lukács and Habermas. If Marx and Weber explain how object-power is generated at the level of economic forms and administrative systems, Lukács asks how this object-world becomes a lived ontology—how persons themselves come to experience reality as thing-like. Habermas asks how metric and market logics invade meaning-rich domains that depend on communication, not just control. Metrics, in other words, are not merely tools; they are the hinge where modernity’s two most powerful coordination regimes—money and bureaucracy—meet, consolidate, and become believable.

## 5\. Lukács: Reification as the Fusion of Commodity-Form and Rationalized Objectivity

Where Marx explains why capitalist social relations take on **thing-like forms** (commodity, money, price) and Weber explains how modern organization expands **formal rationality** (bureaucracy, calculability, rule-systems), Lukács proposes that these are not merely parallel developments. Rather, the commodity-form becomes a **general model of objectivity** —a template that spreads outward from market exchange into law, administration, science, and ultimately into the structure of experience itself. Lukács names this generalized condition **reification**: a social world in which relations, processes, and persons are apprehended as objects, and in which the “object-world” appears as a second nature—fixed, calculable, externally law-governed.

### 5.1 From fetishism to reification: how the commodity structure generalizes across social life

Lukács inherits from Marx the insight that the commodity is not just an item traded in markets but a **social form** that reorganizes relations among people. In Marx’s fetishism analysis, value appears as a property of things because producers encounter one another through exchange rather than through direct coordination. Lukács radicalizes this by arguing that the same form—quantification, commensuration, abstraction—spreads beyond commodities into the organization of institutions and the shape of cognition.

Pedagogically, it helps to treat the commodity-form as a *logic of translation*. Diverse qualities are translated into a common metric: labor into time; goods into money; actions into measurable outputs. Once translation is installed as the dominant mode of coordination, it does not remain confined to the market. Legal reasoning, administrative procedure, and professional practice increasingly mirror the commodity-form by privileging what is **countable, comparable, and rule-governed**. The point is not that law “becomes a commodity,” but that the commodity-form supplies the modern model of what counts as objective: objectivity becomes equated with **formalizable calculation**.

This is where Lukács effectively “joins” Marx and Weber. Marx supplies the underlying social engine—commodity mediation; Weber supplies the institutional texture—bureaucratic rationalization; Lukács explains how the two converge into a total social form that becomes **self-evident reality**.

### 5.2 Consciousness and fragmentation: why reified reality feels natural and inevitable

Reification, for Lukács, is not only an external structure; it is an internalized form of consciousness. A reified world is experienced as fragmented into discrete objects and isolated domains, each governed by its own technical rationality. The subject then encounters social life as if it were a set of quasi-natural facts: categories and metrics appear not as historically produced tools but as the very furniture of reality.

This claim has two layers that are worth separating for teaching purposes.

1. **Epistemic layer (how we know):** Reification encourages a style of thought that treats social processes as object-like systems. The more the institution depends on measurable indicators, the more “knowledge” becomes identified with what can be audited. Qualitative judgment is not refuted; it is displaced as “subjective.”
2. **Affective layer (how it feels):** The reified world feels unavoidable because it confronts individuals as an already-built environment. People can dislike the metric regime, yet still orient their behavior to it because it controls resources, status, and survival.

This helps explain a distinctive modern anxiety: even when we recognize that “the numbers” are proxies, we continue to act as if the proxies were the real. Lukács’s vocabulary for this is not mere deception but a structurally induced **second nature** —a world that behaves like nature because it is stabilized by systems that punish deviation.

### 5.3 Totality as method and antidote: what it means and why it matters

Lukács’s most controversial and most fruitful concept is **totality**. Reification fragments; totality re-connects. The claim is not that one can know everything, but that one must grasp how seemingly separate domains are structurally interrelated. Without such grasp, critique becomes local and easily reabsorbed: one optimizes within the metric rather than questioning the system that makes the metric sovereign.

For pedagogy, “totality” can be introduced as an anti-mystification method:

- **Local causality** says: “This outcome is caused by this immediate factor.”
- **Totality-thinking** asks: “What structural arrangement generates the field in which these factors appear, and why do they appear as necessary?”

This method is especially illuminating for the “numbers as object-power” phenomenon. Reification encourages the belief that a KPI is simply the “truth.” Totality-thinking asks: what institutional incentives, funding regimes, administrative procedures, and political settlements make this KPI authoritative, and what does it conceal?

### 5.4 Reification in institutions: persons as cases, qualities as quantities, judgment as protocol

The most teachable aspect of Lukács’s reification is its ability to describe how object-power migrates into subjectivity and professional practice. Consider three recurrent translations:

| Translation | What is translated | Reified outcome | Typical institutional effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Person → case** | a life situation into a category | individuality becomes a file, a slot, a unit | depersonalization; triage as ontology |
| **Quality → quantity** | care/learning/trust into metrics | what counts is what is countable | proxy becomes target; gaming emerges |
| **Judgment → protocol** | situated expertise into rules | discretion is treated as risk | compliance replaces responsibility |

These are not merely “bad management.” They are the institutional expression of a reified objectivity that equates rational control with quantification. Under reification, protocols are not just guides; they become reality-constituting. Individuals learn to narrate themselves in system-friendly categories (“I am eligible,” “I meet criteria,” “my score is X”), and organizations learn to treat moral dilemmas as optimization problems.

A crucial nuance: Lukács is not advocating anti-rational romanticism. The critique is not of reason as such, but of **one-sided reason** —a form of rationality that recognizes only what can be formalized and then treats formalization as equivalent to truth. In this sense, reification is the deepening of the Marx–Weber convergence described in Section 4. Metrics and bureaucratic procedures do not merely constrain from outside; they install a world in which constraint is experienced as the natural order of things.

Lukács thereby prepares the transition to Habermas. If reification explains how a thing-world becomes cognitively and institutionally dominant, Habermas asks a further question: what happens when this dominance invades domains of life that require communicative understanding—domains where legitimacy cannot be reduced to calculation without doing violence to meaning? The next section takes up this normative and diagnostic shift by distinguishing system mechanisms from lifeworld reproduction and by analyzing the colonization of the latter by the former.

## 6\. Habermas: System vs Lifeworld and the Colonization Thesis

Lukács explains how a commodity-mediated and rationalized society can become experienced as a **thing-world** —a “second nature” in which metrics, categories, and protocols appear as reality itself. Habermas takes the analysis one step further by introducing a structural distinction that is both sociological and normative: modern societies coordinate action through *two different logics*, and pathologies arise when one logic expands into domains that require the other. The conceptual payoff is immediate for the “numbers as object-power” problem: Habermas gives language for why metric governance can feel not merely constraining, but **misplaced** —effective for steering systems, yet destructive when it replaces communication in domains where legitimacy depends on mutual understanding.

### 6.1 Communicative action and communicative rationality: coordination by reasons

Habermas’s foundational claim is that a large part of social order is reproduced not through coercion or incentives but through **communication** —everyday processes of reaching understanding about facts, norms, and identities. When we coordinate by talking, arguing, giving reasons, and repairing misunderstandings, we are operating in what Habermas calls the domain of **communicative action**. “Rationality” here does not mean calculative efficiency; it means the capacity to justify claims and be answerable to others. A clinician discussing options with a patient, colleagues deliberating in an MDT, or citizens debating policy are paradigmatic cases: the coordination depends on reasons that others could, in principle, accept.

This introduces a critical distinction. In Weber, rationalization often suggests the triumph of calculability; in Marx, rationality is entangled with the value-form’s objectivity. Habermas offers a second kind of rationality— **communicative rationality** —whose primary medium is not money, law, or metrics, but language oriented toward understanding. This makes it possible to criticize metric governance without falling into anti-rational romanticism. The complaint is not “reason has gone too far,” but “a particular rationality (instrumental/strategic) has displaced another (communicative).”

### 6.2 System mechanisms: money and power as steering media

Habermas famously distinguishes **lifeworld** and **system**. The lifeworld refers to the background resources that make communication possible: shared meanings, norms, trust, identity, and cultural reproduction. The system refers to large-scale subsystems (notably the economy and state administration) that coordinate action through **steering media**: money and power. These media allow coordination without needing participants to reach shared understanding each time. If a price changes, behavior shifts; if an administrative rule changes, procedures shift. No consensus is required—only compliance or adaptation.

Here is where the “numbers” phenomenon becomes legible in Habermasian terms. Metrics, targets, and performance indicators are often the interface through which money and power steer complex organizations. They enable a department to be governed at a distance: funding can be tied to throughput; status can be tied to rankings; compliance can be tied to audits. Such steering is often necessary for large-scale coordination. The problem begins when steering media become not only **tools** but **substitutes** for communicative processes that sustain legitimacy and meaning.

### 6.3 Colonization of the lifeworld: when metrics invade meaning-rich domains

Habermas’s most influential diagnostic concept is **colonization of the lifeworld**: system logics expand into lifeworld domains and reorganize them according to money/power imperatives. Colonization is not merely increased administration; it is a transformation in the *grammar of reasons*. In colonized domains, actions are justified less by mutual understanding (“this is right/good/appropriate”) and more by system outputs (“this meets the target,” “this is reimbursable,” “this reduces risk exposure”).

This yields a precise way to interpret “object-power.” When a clinician says “the system requires it,” Habermas would ask: is the relevant decision properly a **system-steering** matter (resource allocation, capacity planning) or a **lifeworld** matter (care as a relationship of understanding and trust)? Colonization occurs when system rationality displaces lifeworld rationality—when the patient encounter is treated as a throughput problem, when education becomes a ranking exercise, when professional judgment is rewritten as compliance.

A teaching-friendly table can clarify typical signs:

| Domain (lifeworld-heavy) | What communicative coordination requires | Colonizing substitution | Pathology produced |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clinical care | trust, informed consent, interpretive judgment | targets, coding, throughput metrics | legitimacy deficits; moral distress |
| Education | development, dialogue, formative evaluation | rankings, standardized performance indices | teaching-to-the-test; loss of meaning |
| Community/politics | deliberation, normative justification | technocratic management, cost-benefit only | cynicism; democratic hollowing |

Crucially, Habermas does not deny that metrics can improve fairness or efficiency. Colonization is diagnosed when metric success becomes a surrogate for reasons that ought to be publicly contestable.

### 6.4 Positioning Habermas vs Lukács: differentiation and normative reconstruction

Habermas and Lukács converge in their sensitivity to objectification, but diverge in structure and remedy. Lukács treats reification as a generalized condition rooted in the commodity form and argues that fragmentation can be countered only by grasping **totality** —a comprehensive understanding of the social whole that reveals how appearances are produced. Habermas is more differentiation-friendly: modernity contains multiple rationalities and subsystems, and pathologies arise from **boundary violations** —system mechanisms intruding into lifeworld reproduction.

This difference matters for analysis of metrics. A Lukácsian reading tends to treat metric domination as an instance of reification’s total logic: the world becomes an object-system. A Habermasian reading asks where and why system steering is appropriate, and where it becomes illegitimate colonization. In this sense, Habermas provides an explicit normative vocabulary for diagnosing when “numbers as object-power” is not only alienating, but *wrongly placed*.

The transition to Polanyi is now prepared. Habermas has shown how money and power steer systems and how their expansion can colonize lifeworld domains. Polanyi provides the historical political economy of *when* the economic system’s steering medium—market pricing—becomes socially sovereign, i.e., when markets are disembedded and begin to reorganize society rather than being contained within it. Polanyi thus offers a temporal and institutional account of intensification: why certain eras and reforms dramatically increase the reach of system imperatives and thereby magnify the conditions under which fetishism, rationalization, reification, and colonization become socially pervasive.

## 7\. Polanyi: Embeddedness, Disembedding, and the Historical Conditions of Intensification

Habermas’s system–lifeworld distinction clarifies how money and power can steer action without communicative agreement, and why pathologies arise when system logics colonize meaning-rich domains. Polanyi adds a crucial historical-political dimension: *under what conditions does the economic system’s steering medium—market pricing—claim sovereignty over society as a whole?* In Polanyi’s framework, the authority of “the market” is not an anthropological constant but a political project, one whose success and reversal help explain when fetishism and disenchantment intensify, and why “numbers as object-power” becomes culturally persuasive.

### 7.1 Embeddedness as baseline: economy within social relations

Polanyi’s starting point is deceptively simple: in most historical settings, economies are **embedded** in social relations. Economic activity is governed by social institutions—kinship, religion, political authority, customary norms, guilds, and local obligations. Prices may exist, trade may exist, but they do not constitute an autonomous “economic sphere” that dictates the terms of social life. Embeddedness is therefore not a romantic claim about harmony; it is a descriptive claim about institutional arrangement: economic coordination is subordinated to social and political coordination.

This is the first major pedagogical lesson Polanyi offers to the object-power discussion: the experience that “the market demands…” is historically specific. It depends on the extent to which society has organized itself such that market signals override other normative frameworks. Embeddedness, by contrast, makes the social genesis of economic constraints more visible. When allocation is openly political or customary, “necessity” is harder to naturalize as an impersonal force.

### 7.2 Disembedding and fictitious commodities: why market society produces crises

Polanyi’s signature diagnosis of modernity is **disembedding**: the attempt to create a self-regulating market system that organizes society around price mechanisms. Disembedding is not merely “more trade.” It is the restructuring of institutions so that markets become the primary coordinators, and social life must adapt to their signals. This attempt requires converting foundational elements of social life into tradable inputs—what Polanyi calls **fictitious commodities**: **labor**, **land**, and **money**.

They are “fictitious” because they are not produced for sale in the way ordinary commodities are. Labor is human life-time and capacity; land is nature and place; money is a social institution of accounting and obligation. Treating these as if they were ordinary commodities generates instability and harm: wages fluctuate with market cycles; land is degraded or displaced; money and credit amplify booms and busts. In Polanyi’s terms, a fully self-regulating market would “annihilate” society and nature, which is why it cannot persist without countervailing protections.

For the fetishism/rationalization problem, the key is this: disembedding makes “market necessity” appear as a kind of natural law. When labor, land, and money are subjected to price movements, the resulting dislocations are experienced as impersonal constraints (“competitiveness,” “confidence,” “rates,” “the bond market”). The authority of numbers is strengthened because marketized domains increasingly encounter one another through quantified signals. Disembedding thus supplies historical conditions that intensify Marx’s fetish-character and Weber’s formal rationality simultaneously: more life is organized through prices and administrative responses to prices.

### 7.3 The double movement: marketization and social protection as recurrent dynamics

Polanyi’s second major contribution is the **double movement**: the expansion of markets provokes protective counter-movements. This is not a moral narrative of “society wakes up,” but a structural claim: when marketization threatens social reproduction, political forces—often diverse and contradictory—seek to re-embed economic activity through regulation, welfare institutions, labor protections, and public provisioning.

Pedagogically, the double movement explains why object-power does not simply intensify in a linear fashion. When disembedding accelerates, fetishistic forms of necessity (“the market made us do it”) become culturally dominant. When re-embedding intensifies, some of that fetishistic plausibility can weaken because decisions become more explicitly political: choices are routed through legislation, public budgeting, and institutional negotiation rather than being presented as automatic results of price signals.

However, Polanyi also helps clarify a paradox already implied by Weber and Habermas: re-embedding often requires **more administration**. Regulation, safety nets, and public provisioning commonly increase bureaucratic thickness. Thus a society can reduce market compulsion while intensifying the procedural experience of constraint—fewer “market laws,” more “policy requirements.” This is why Polanyi complements rather than replaces Weber: social protection can soften market sovereignty while tightening the iron cage.

### 7.4 Re-reading Marx–Weber through Polanyi: when each “lock” tightens

Polanyi allows one to historicize the “two locks” model (market compulsion vs bureaucratic rationalization). Disembedding tends to tighten the market lock; re-embedding can loosen it while tightening administrative constraint. This interaction can be expressed as an analytic grid:

| Historical/institutional movement | Market lock (Marx: compulsion/fetishism) | Bureaucratic lock (Weber: formal rationality) | Typical “numbers talk” |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Disembedding** (marketization) | strengthens: price signals and competition dominate | often strengthens: auditing and targets intensify to manage volatility | “the market demands…,” “rates require…,” “cost pressures force…” |
| **Re-embedding** (social protection) | can weaken: choices become overtly political/social | can strengthen: rules, eligibility, compliance expand | “policy requires…,” “criteria mandate…,” “targets and guidelines…” |
| **Hybrid equilibrium** | both present: market imperatives administered bureaucratically | both present: steering via money + rules | “the numbers say…,” “the system requires…” |

This table does not claim deterministic outcomes. It clarifies tendencies and trade-offs. Polanyi’s conceptual benefit is to show that the social authority of markets is historically produced, and that the institutional counter-reaction is not simply “freedom,” but often a shift from one mode of compulsion to another.

For the present review’s pedagogical aims, Polanyi therefore functions as a temporal and institutional hinge: he explains why, in certain periods and sectors, “market necessity” becomes an all-purpose justification, and why protective politics emerges to contest it—even at the cost of bureaucratic expansion. In the final synthesis, this will allow the mechanisms from Marx (fetish-character), Weber (rationalization), Lukács (reification), and Habermas (colonization) to be treated not as timeless diagnoses, but as historically variable patterns that intensify or weaken depending on whether markets are embedded within social institutions or permitted to reorganize society in their own image.

## Summary##

Modern life often feels like it’s run by things rather than by people. We say “the market demands it,” “the budget forces it,” “the algorithm decided,” or “the rules require it.” In this project we treated that feeling as a real puzzle: **how do human-made systems come to seem like objective forces that control us?** We used four major thinkers—Marx, Weber, Lukács, Habermas, and then Polanyi—to explain different parts of the same phenomenon.

**Karl Marx** starts with everyday objects in capitalism: commodities (things bought and sold). Marx says that in a market society, people don’t coordinate work directly (“you make this, I make that, and we plan together”). Instead, they coordinate *through exchange*. That makes social relationships show up in the form of prices and money. Over time, value starts to feel like it is inside the object itself—like the object naturally “has” its price. Marx calls this **commodity fetishism**: social relationships between people appear as relationships between things. The key point is that fetishism is not just “people being confused.” The system itself makes the confusion likely, because money and prices are the main way the society communicates what counts and what doesn’t.

**Max Weber** explains a different force: **rationalization**. Modern societies rely heavily on rules, procedures, forms, and bureaucracy to handle complexity. That is often useful: rules create fairness, predictability, and accountability. But Weber warns that this can become an “ **iron cage** ”—a world where the system is technically efficient but cold, rigid, and hard to change. Weber also helps us see that “rational” can mean different things. Sometimes it means what works in practice; sometimes it means building a scientific explanation; sometimes it means acting according to values; and sometimes it means following rules and calculations. Modern institutions tend to prioritize the last one—rule-based calculation—because it scales.

When we combine Marx and Weber, a key meeting point appears: **metrics and numbers**. Numbers aren’t only descriptions; they become tools of control. A target, a KPI, a budget line, or a performance score can act like a command. Here Marx helps explain why numbers feel like natural facts (“the budget is just reality”), while Weber explains why institutions love numbers (they make auditing and management easier). Together they show why people experience “the numbers” as powerful, even if the numbers were created by human decisions.

**György Lukács** takes this further with the idea of **reification**. Reification means treating living social processes—people, relationships, values—as if they were things. Lukács argues that capitalism doesn’t just shape markets; it shapes how people think and how institutions operate. The world becomes a “thing-world,” where what feels most real is what is measurable and countable. People can even start to see themselves as objects: productivity, rankings, scores, and outputs become part of identity. Lukács says breaking this requires seeing the “total picture” of how the system fits together, not just solving problems inside one small part.

**Jürgen Habermas** adds a key distinction: **system vs lifeworld**. The “system” includes big structures like markets and state administration that coordinate life using money and power. The “lifeworld” is everyday meaning—relationships, trust, shared understanding, and moral reasoning. Habermas argues that problems happen when system logic spreads into lifeworld spaces. This is called **colonization of the lifeworld**: when care, education, or community life gets treated mainly as targets, costs, and outputs instead of human meaning and dialogue.

Finally, **Karl Polanyi** explains the historical conditions that make these forces stronger or weaker. He argues that economies are usually **embedded** in social rules and protections. A “market society” tries to **disembed** the economy—letting markets rule society. Polanyi says this creates instability, especially because it treats labor, land, and money like normal commodities even though they’re not. He also describes a “ **double movement** ”: when market pressure gets too intense, societies push back with protections like regulation and welfare systems. That can reduce the feeling that “the market is destiny,” but it may also increase bureaucracy (more rules).

Together, these thinkers show that modern “object-power” has multiple sources: markets, bureaucracy, reified thinking, system invasion of everyday life, and historical waves of market expansion and protection. The big lesson is that what feels like natural necessity is often the result of human-built systems—and understanding the mechanism is the first step to changing how much control those systems should have.
