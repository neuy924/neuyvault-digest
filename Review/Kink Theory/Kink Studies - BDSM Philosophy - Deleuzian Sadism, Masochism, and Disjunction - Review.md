## 1) Against “sadomasochism”: Deleuze’s methodological wager

Deleuze’s rejection of a sadism–masochism unity is not a sociological denial that practices can mix; it is a methodological refusal of what he treats as a **clinical abstraction**. The target is the explanatory shortcut: assemble “abstract elements” (pain, domination, submission, cruelty), then claim they transform into each other, thereby licensing a single syndrome. Deleuze insists that this move mistakes **equivalences and translations** for **transitions and transformations**: if two phenomena share features, that does not entail they are two phases of one mechanism.

This is why his language keeps returning to the “concrete situation” and the “specific world” of each perversion: not as an empirical census, but as a demand for **structural description**. The conceptual unit is not a trait (“likes pain”) but an apparatus: a rule-system, a narrative scaffolding, a temporal ordering, a role-grammar, and a relation to law. The upshot is a kind of anti-syndromic taxonomy: sadism and masochism are not opposites along a slider; they are different machines whose outputs may superficially resemble each other.

Two distinctions do most of the work. First, Deleuze treats **masochism** as a practice of *juridical construction* —contracts, clauses, education, persuasion—by which the “victim” actively participates in forming the scene and even forming the partner. This is why he can say that personal ads belong to a masochistic language and are excluded from “true sadism,” and why the masochist “elaborates contracts.” Second, Deleuze treats **sadism** as the inverse with respect to juridical form: the sadist “abhors and tears up every contract” and instead “needs institutions.” The contract/institution split is not decoration; it supplies a criterion of incompatibility. If one apparatus requires consensual juridical scripting to function, while the other is structurally anti-contractual and thrives on instituted sovereignty, then “sadomasochism” becomes a category error unless one can show how the two logics genuinely compose rather than merely co-occur.

Deleuze’s wager here is also historiographical: he reads Sade and Sacher-Masoch as **primary laboratories** for these apparatuses, and he takes eponymous naming seriously enough to complain about its unfairness while still using it diagnostically. Reception commentary often notices this tension: Deleuze wants to depathologize and de-syndromize, but he also stabilizes two ideal-types with striking crispness. This tension becomes the hinge for later critiques: is Deleuze rescuing difference from a flattening syndrome, or is he substituting one rigid dichotomy for another?

| Dimension | Masochism (apparatus) | Sadism (apparatus) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Juridical form | contract → law → rite | anti-contract; institution |
| Role production | education/persuasion; partner is “formed” | sovereignty/command; partner is positioned |
| Explanatory unit | concrete situation / world | concrete situation / world |

## 2) Two languages and a triad: imperative, descriptive, demonstrative

Deleuze’s “two languages” claim is best read as a thesis about **how writing constitutes power**, not simply how it represents it. He argues that pornological literature reaches its limit only through an internal doubling of language. In the Sadean case, the doubled structure is organized as a triad: **imperatives** (words of order), **descriptions** (scenes, bodies, arrangements), and a higher **demonstrative** function that subordinates the first two. The “demonstrative” is not merely argumentation; it is the logic by which a regime claims necessity—reasoning that presents itself as law-like, even when it is theatrically excessive.

This triad matters because it supplies Deleuze with a formal reason to deny sadism–masochism complementarity. In Sade, imperatives and descriptions are “bathed” in demonstration; the scene is instituted as if by axioms. In Masoch, by contrast, Deleuze stresses a different higher function: mythic/persuasive staging—letters, contracts, the slow construction of a scenario whose legitimacy is produced rhetorically rather than deduced. What looks like “domination” in both is, in his account, generated by different speech-acts: founding institutions through demonstrative reason versus generating rites through contractual law and narrative persuasion.

A useful way to operationalize Deleuze’s triad (and to test it against primary text, as we did earlier with Sade’s statute-like sequences) is to treat each function as a **mode of constraint**:

- The **imperative** constrains by command (“do X”), and produces hierarchy by direct address.
- The **descriptive** constrains by enclosure (“here is the world you are in”), making alternatives vanish by world-building.
- The **demonstrative** constrains by necessity (“given premises P, Q follows”), making hierarchy appear rational and inevitable.

Deleuze’s strong claim is that in sadism these three are not equal partners: the demonstrative absorbs the other two and converts them into components of an instituted order. That is why he can treat sadism as a kind of metaphysical enterprise in some reception discussions: the cruelty is not just an appetite but a medium for a cold, quasi-philosophical operation. Critics sometimes bristle here, because “sadism as metaphysics” risks becoming so abstract that the specifically sexual stakes look like an afterthought that must be reattached. But as a formal claim about regimes of language, the triad is doing real work: it explains why Deleuze thinks the sadist does not need a contract (contracts negotiate); the sadist needs an order that can present itself as deduced.

Deleuze’s language-theory also quietly aligns with his broader philosophical sensibility: difference is not a matter of surface content but of generative form. If two texts contain similar acts, that does not entail they are produced by the same apparatus. The triad provides a discriminative tool—one that can be pushed too hard (flattening Sade’s internal heterogeneity), but one that makes his rejection of “sadomasochism” more than a contrarian slogan.

## 3) From contract to rite: law’s inversion and the “three rites”

In *Du contrat au rite*, Deleuze states the hinge with near-algebraic clarity: **from contract to myth, by way of law; law emerges from the contract, but throws us into rites**. The contract is modern, voluntary, time-bounded; yet it becomes the portal to an impersonal symbolic order precisely because it generates a law that exceeds the contract’s limits. Deleuze emphasizes the “mystification” internal to the contract–law relation: law claims durability, universality, and opposability that the contract, strictly speaking, cannot provide. Masochism exploits this slippage rather than suffering it.

The decisive transformation is the **transfer of paternal law into maternal hands**, which Deleuze frames not as a mere reassignment of who wields punishment but as a change in what law *is for*. In this transfer, law begins to **order what it was supposed to forbid** —a functional inversion. Closely tied is Deleuze’s temporal operator: punishment is not a causal path to pleasure but a **prerequisite**, a rite-form (“first X, then Y”) that reorganizes the scene’s legitimacy. In this way, the contract is not the opposite of ritual; it is a modern machine for producing ritual.

Deleuze then names three rites that “coexist and interfere,” forming “the great masochistic myth”: **chasse** (hunting), **agriculture** (an agrarian community presided over by the woman), and **seconde naissance** (a second birth imposed by the woman). The third is “essential,” the telos of the first two: second birth is the symbolic endpoint for which initiation and world-building are preparatory.

These rites are not “themes.” They are operators that neutralize the father-function by reorganizing law, guilt, and identity. Deleuze’s father-annulment vocabulary is explicit: the father is “excluded, annulled”; the “resemblance of the father” is treated as fault; the image and resemblance of the father are humiliated; the superego as agent of repression is emptied alongside genital sexuality; and the ever-present danger is the “return of the father” that cracks the defenses. When mapped to the rites, the logic becomes legible:

- **Chasse** is initiatory because it is a threshold scene that installs maternal sovereignty and begins the exclusion of paternal return.
- **Agriculture** institutionalizes (without being “institution” in the Sadean sense) a maternal law-world: a community form in which guilt and superego are rerouted away from paternal authority.
- **Seconde naissance** consummates neutralization by making the father structurally unnecessary: rebirth in a register where “the father has no role.”

**Table 2. Deleuze’s three rites as functions**

| Rite | Formal function | Father-neutralization axis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chasse | initiation / capture into maternal jurisdiction | exclusion; warding off paternal return |
| Agriculture | world-building under maternal law | rerouting guilt; emptying superego authority |
| Seconde naissance | telos: symbolic regeneration | father has no role; identity re-founded |

## 4) Reception and critique: where Deleuze may overstate disjunction

Deleuze’s apparatus-thinking has been influential precisely because it resists flattening. But the reception has a recurring worry: he rescues difference from a syndrome by building a dichotomy so clean that it risks becoming its own kind of flattening. A major historical critique from Alison M. Downham Moore argues that Deleuze’s dichotomy can elide the difference between **desire** and **pathological category**, partly because it is insufficiently attentive to the medical history of sexuality that produced the very terms “sadism” and “masochism.” On this reading, Deleuze’s “non-communication” between sadism and masochism risks importing a sexological typology into philosophy, where it then looks like a metaphysical necessity rather than a historically contingent classification.

This critique bites at two pressure points. First, **historicity**: if “sadism” and “masochism” are not natural kinds but moving targets in psychiatric discourse, then claiming their strict incommensurability may treat historical artifacts as ontological blocks. Second, **corpus constraint**: Deleuze’s reliance on emblematic authors can overdetermine the result—Sade becomes the demonstration/institution pole; Masoch the contract/rite pole—while the broader textual and lived field may contain hybrids, translations, and cross-contaminations that a rigid disjunction cannot easily accommodate.

A different, more internal critique concerns Deleuze’s portrayal of sadism as a form of thinking. Lauwaert and Harris highlight the counterintuitive core of Deleuze’s Sade: the sadist is, at heart, a metaphysician who thinks by means of suffering and indifference, which raises the concern of how to account for the “properly sexual nature” of this metaphysical perversion. The issue is not that this reading is wrong, but that it may shift the comparison between sadism and masochism onto a plane where disjunction is guaranteed: if sadism is recoded as metaphysics-in-action, then of course it will not unify with masochism’s juridical-ritual dramaturgy. Critics ask whether Deleuze has, in effect, changed the subject in order to win the argument.

Yet sympathetic readers also note that Deleuze’s two apparatuses can look like **parallel series**: contract/law/rite versus institution/demonstration; humor versus irony; suspense versus acceleration. The parallelism suggests possible translation rules rather than absolute non-communication, and it invites a softer thesis: not that “sadomasochism” is meaningless, but that it is a **family of composite practices** whose internal architectures vary. This is the point at which Deleuze’s ideal types may be most useful and most risky: useful as discriminative lenses; risky when treated as exhaustive partitions.

What survives critique most robustly is Deleuze’s insistence on **form**: that the same surface behaviors can be generated by different logics of law, time, and language. Even a historian’s skepticism about his dichotomy can accept the methodological injunction: don’t infer unity from resemblance; identify the apparatus that makes resemblance possible.
