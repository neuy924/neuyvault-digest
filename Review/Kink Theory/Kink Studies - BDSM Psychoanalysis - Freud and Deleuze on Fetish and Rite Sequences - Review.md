## 1) Cock and Ball Torture (CBT): fetish-object, disavowal, and the juridical staging of “law inverted”

In Freud’s *Fetishism*, the fetish is not “a sexy thing” so much as a **stabilizer** for a contradiction: two incompatible attitudes can coexist (“I register X; nevertheless I refuse Y”), and the fetish-object anchors that coexistence so it doesn’t collapse into panic, denial, or psychosis. Read that way, CBT in BDSM practice is less about a “taste for pain” and more about how **bodily specificity** can function as an anchoring token for a double structure. The “object” here may be literal (a specific body-part focus, a particular implement, a ritualized cue) or symbolic (a rule, phrase, or scenario that condenses meaning). The Freudian lens asks: *what contradiction is being stabilized by this fixation?* In kink contexts, a common (but not universal) pattern is that CBT can stage a tension between vulnerability and control, potency and surrender, invulnerability fantasies and the undeniable fact of bodily dependence. Freud would predict that the more “non-substitutable” the focus becomes, the more it may operate as a fetish-like anchor—especially when the scene’s meaning depends on **that** focus rather than any other.

Deleuze shifts the emphasis away from object-fixation and toward **apparatus**: contract → law → rite → myth. CBT—when practiced as BDSM rather than impulsive aggression—often has a strikingly Deleuzian structure: explicit negotiation, boundaries, safeness constraints, role assignments, and a procedural temporality (“first we do X, then Y becomes possible”). What matters is not the sensation in isolation but the **juridical sequencing** that makes the sensation intelligible. Deleuze’s key insight is that masochism is not “pain-seeking” but **law-producing**: the contract generates law; law is then transformed so that what would ordinarily be prohibited becomes, within the scene, *commanded* (a controlled inversion). In CBT scenes, this can look like “the forbidden” (injury-risk, taboo vulnerability, threatened potency) being made speakable and bearable precisely because the scene’s law authorizes and constrains it at once. The contradiction “law obeyed / law overturned” can be stabilized procedurally: obeying the safety law is what permits the law-inversion fantasy to exist.

Where Freud and Deleuze genuinely diverge is the explanatory priority. A Freudian reading can drift toward “defense” (CBT as compromise formation around anxiety about potency, vulnerability, humiliation, etc.). A Deleuzian reading foregrounds **production** (CBT as a scene-machine that manufactures a specific temporality—anticipation, prerequisite, threshold, transformation—by ritualizing constraint). In practice, both can operate simultaneously: for some, CBT may be the fetish-like “token” that makes a contradictory self-experience livable; for others, CBT is just one module within a broader apparatus whose true engine is contract/law/rite. Ethically, both lenses point to the same risk marker: when meaning becomes sovereign—when the token must be used regardless of context, or when the procedure becomes non-renegotiable—the stabilizer can become a trap rather than a tool.

## 2) Bondage: fetish as tokenized certainty vs rite as choreography of constraint

Bondage is almost tailor-made for teasing apart Freud’s **object anchor** and Deleuze’s **procedural anchor**, because it is literally about constraint, permission, and the transformation of “cannot” into “may (within conditions).” In Freud’s fetishism frame, bondage can function fetishistically when a **particular sign of constraint** (a specific material, knot, position, or visual cue) becomes the privileged stabilizer that makes a contradiction tolerable. The contradiction is often some variant of “I am autonomous / I must be held,” or “I am safe / I want to feel endangered,” or “I decide / I surrender.” The fetish-object does not resolve these; it allows them to coexist by giving the psyche something to “hold onto” (sometimes quite literally) that bypasses the demand for integration. Freud’s “two attitudes” model becomes legible when a person both knows they are safe and simultaneously experiences an “as if” danger or helplessness that remains compelling. The fetish-like token sustains the “as if” without requiring delusion.

Deleuze’s account, however, suggests that bondage is less about the object and more about a **ritualized grammar**. Bondage typically involves negotiated roles, explicit permissions, and a temporal structure of thresholds (“once restrained, the scene becomes possible; once released, the scene ends/changes”). That is Deleuze’s contract→law→rite pipeline in miniature. The “law” here is not merely constraint; it is **authorization through constraint**. Bondage can produce the paradox that Deleuze finds central: the law that forbids harm or non-consent is precisely what enables the scene’s inversion of everyday autonomy norms. The participant can experience both attitudes—“I obey the law” and “I overturn everyday law”—because the apparatus distributes them: obedience is enacted as following the negotiated constraints; overturning is enacted as inhabiting the role-world those constraints authorize.

The phenomenology differs accordingly. A Freudian token-anchor tends to feel **binary**: the scene “clicks” when the cue is present; it may feel “off” when the cue is missing. A Deleuzian procedural anchor tends to feel **narrative**: the sequence matters, the transitions matter, the rite matters. This is where bondage often drifts into “mythic intensity”: the scene becomes more than restraint; it becomes a transpersonal script about trust, surrender, protection, or transformation. In Deleuze’s terms, the rite can generate myth—an impersonal sense that “this is how things must happen” in the scene-world. Ethically, that’s double-edged: intensity can be nourishing when renegotiation remains cheap; rigidity emerges when the choreography becomes compulsory regardless of bodily feedback, mood, or consent state.

If you want a clean way to distinguish “Freud-ish” from “Deleuze-ish” bondage in real practice: ask whether the primary stabilizer is **a token** (a specific cue that anchors arousal/meaning) or **a sequence** (a ritual script whose law-like structure is the engine). Both can coexist, but they fail differently: token systems fail through substitution crises (only this works), while procedural systems fail through jurisdiction creep (the rules colonize more of life than intended).

## 3) Erotic humiliation and degradation: disavowal’s “I know—nevertheless” vs the apparatus of naming, law, and role-worlds

Humiliation/degradation play is where Freud and Deleuze diverge most sharply in *what* they think is being explained. Freud’s fetishism and splitting framework is sensitive to how a subject can maintain **two attitudes** toward the self simultaneously: “I am worthy” and “I am worthless,” “I am respected” and “I am contemptible,” “this is play” and “this is true.” In healthy, consensual kink contexts, the “this is play” attitude is not a trivial disclaimer; it is the condition that allows the contradictory attitude to be safely explored. Freud’s model predicts that a stabilizer—sometimes an object, sometimes a phrase, sometimes a ritual boundary—may be needed to keep the contradictory self-attitudes from collapsing into either shame-flooding or emotional numbness. The Freudian question becomes: *what is being disavowed, and what is being preserved?* In humiliation play, it’s often not “reality facts” but **self-evaluations** and their consequences. The play can create a controlled zone in which shame is invoked but simultaneously bracketed, allowing the subject to experience and metabolize affect that might otherwise be intolerable.

Deleuze’s approach reframes humiliation less as intrapsychic defense and more as **linguistic-juridical production**. His distinction between masochism and sadism hinges partly on “languages”: masochism as contract, persuasion, and ritualized law; sadism as demonstration and institution. Humiliation play often involves *naming* —titles, roles, scripted speech acts—that function like micro-laws: words don’t merely describe; they **do** something. In Deleuzean terms, this is where the “law” of the scene is installed and inverted. The contract (explicit or implicit) makes certain speech acts permissible and others forbidden; the rite (sequenced enactment) renders the speech acts transformative rather than merely hurtful. The contradiction “law obeyed / law overturned” becomes explicit: everyday moral law forbids degradation; scene-law authorizes it under constraints, precisely by treating it as a ritual function rather than a spontaneous insult.

The ethical boundary between “mythic intensity” and “mythic rigidity” is particularly salient here. Intensity looks like a shared transpersonal script that deepens intimacy while remaining revisable; rigidity looks like a script that becomes sovereign and starts overwriting reality (“you are this” becomes identity rather than role). Freud would describe a danger of splitting hardening into fixed self-contempt (“the play attitude collapses; only the degraded attitude remains effective”). Deleuze would describe apparatus capture: the scene-law becomes jurisdiction over the person, expanding beyond negotiated bounds. Both models converge on the same practical marker: **renegotiation cost**. If a participant cannot safely revise the script without being treated as betraying the “truth” of the role-world, intensity has tipped toward rigidity.

Conceptually, the two theories thus triangulate humiliation play: Freud explains how the psyche can hold contradictory self-attitudes without integration; Deleuze explains how law-like speech acts and rites can produce a world in which those contradictions become coherent as procedure. The play becomes ethically defensible not because it is “harmless,” but because its stabilizers keep the contradiction staged rather than totalizing.

## 4) Drone play: splitting as compartmentalized agency vs rite as algorithmic selfhood

“Drone play” (as discussed in contemporary kink communities) typically refers to roleplay in which a person adopts a “drone” persona—often characterized by reduced individuality, scripted behavior, monotone affect, or a sense of being programmed. Without getting into erotic instruction, it’s conceptually rich for Freud/Deleuze because it foregrounds *agency, law, and identity as apparatus*.

Freud’s “two attitudes” model offers a powerful reading: drone play often depends on holding two stances at once—“I am choosing this” and “I am not choosing; I am being controlled.” The pleasure/meaning can come precisely from maintaining that contradiction without resolving it. Here, splitting can be understood (non-pathologizingly) as a **compartmentalization of agency**: one part of the self endorses the scene and monitors safety, while another inhabits the “as if programmed” persona. A fetish-like stabilizer may appear as a trigger cue, a costume element, a specific phrase, a ritual posture—anything that functions as a token that allows the “drone attitude” to switch on while the “chooser attitude” remains intact in the background. In Freudian terms, the stabilizer prevents the contradiction from collapsing into either panic (“I’m losing control”) or disappointment (“this is obviously just pretend”). The “I know—nevertheless” structure is almost the point.

Deleuze’s apparatus reading is even more direct. Drone play is basically a dramatization of **apparatus** itself: identity produced by rules, sequences, and permissions. Deleuze’s contract→law→rite→myth pipeline maps cleanly onto drone scenes: negotiation establishes the contract (what is allowed, what isn’t, how entry/exit works), which generates law (constraints and authorizations), which produces rites (entry sequences, maintenance behaviors, termination sequences), which then yields a mythic transpersonalization (“dronehood” as an impersonal role-world with its own necessity). Under this lens, the “drone” is not primarily a psychological trait but a **state in a rule-governed machine** —a lived demonstration that personhood can be modulated by juridical-ritual form.

The contradiction “law obeyed / law overturned” is also visible. Everyday autonomy norms are “overturned” (the drone obeys), but only by obeying scene-law that protects autonomy at a higher level (the capacity to choose, revise, exit). Deleuze helps you see why drone play can feel unusually intense: it is not just sensation; it is a mythic reconfiguration of selfhood as procedure. That’s exactly where ethical risk concentrates. If the apparatus becomes sovereign—if the “drone identity” is treated as more real than the person’s fluctuating needs—mythic intensity can slide into rigidity. Freud would flag the risk of splitting maintenance becoming chronic avoidance of integration (“I only feel safe as non-self”); Deleuze would flag capture (“the script expands; renegotiation becomes taboo; the role-world colonizes daily life”). The key separator is whether the apparatus remains **playable and revisable** —a staged world—or becomes destiny that overrides feedback.

Drone play thus becomes a modern lab for comparing Freud and Deleuze: Freud clarifies the intrapsychic double attitude of agency; Deleuze clarifies the procedural production of that agency-state via contract, law, and rite. Both models converge ethically on the same marker: does the stabilizer serve the persons, or do the persons become fuel for the stabilizer?

## 1) Two attitudes, four plays: explicit dyads and what anchors their coexistence

Freud’s “two attitudes” (two contrary, independent stances held at once) becomes most legible when you treat kink scenes as *designed contradictions*: a person can both “know” something at the level of reality-testing and “live” an incompatible stance within the scene’s frame. Sigmund Freud theorizes this coexistence as a defensive organization (splitting/disavowal) that can stabilize conflict rather than resolve it. Gilles Deleuze, by contrast, effectively externalizes contradiction into a scene apparatus—contract → law → rite → myth—so the “double structure” is enacted procedurally and socially rather than merely held intrapsychically. Mapping “two attitudes” across different play types is therefore less about diagnosing motives and more about identifying (i) the *contradiction being staged* and (ii) the *stabilizer* (token/object vs procedure/sequence vs speech-act law) that keeps the contradiction coherent.

Below is an explicit mapping for the four practices you specified, written in a non-instructional way (no “how-to”), focusing on phenomenological structure and anchors.

### CBT (cock and ball torture)

**Two attitudes (explicit):**

- **A: Safety / integrity is binding.** “This is bounded, chosen, and constrained by care.”
- **B: Vulnerability / jeopardy is ‘real enough’ to bite.** “I am exposed; potency/agency is precarious; consequences feel near.”

**Anchor(s) of coexistence:**

- **Procedural anchor (Deleuzian):** negotiated constraints and “prerequisite” sequencing create a law-world in which the jeopardy-feel is authorized precisely because safety-law is obeyed (a controlled inversion).
- **Token anchor (Freudian-leaning):** a highly specific bodily focus or implement can become a “non-substitutable” sign that gates the scene’s reality-effect—present/absent, charged/uncharged—making contradiction feel stable by concentrating it. (This is a structural inference from fetish-token logic, not a claim that CBT *is* fetishism in any clinical sense.)

### Bondage

**Two attitudes (explicit):**

- **A: Autonomy remains true.** “I consent; I can choose; I remain a person with agency.”
- **B: Autonomy is suspended.** “I cannot; I am held; I am governed by constraint.”

**Anchor(s) of coexistence:**

- **Rite-sequence anchor:** bondage often creates clean “state transitions” (before/after restraint; entry/exit), so contradiction becomes temporal: autonomy is affirmed in the *contractual entry* and bracketed in the *ritual state*.
- **Speech-act anchor:** titles, permissions, and role declarations can function like micro-laws; they don’t just describe a state but institute it.
- **Token anchor:** particular materials, positions, or visual cues can become fetish-like switches that intensify the “as if helpless” attitude without erasing the “I chose” attitude.

### Erotic humiliation and degradation

**Two attitudes (explicit):**

- **A: Dignity / worth is intact.** “This is play; I am respected; the scene is bounded.”
- **B: Dignity / worth is negated (within the frame).** “I am reduced, shamed, or despised (in-role).”

**Anchor(s) of coexistence:**

- **Juridical-linguistic anchor:** humiliation is often mediated by naming; the utterance is the act. The anchor is therefore the scene’s law of speech—who may say what, in what frame, with what repair norms.
- **Rite anchor:** sequencing matters: the “degradation attitude” is made coherent when it is bracketed by entry/exit rituals that reinstall ordinary dignity.
- **Token anchor:** symbolic objects or phrases can serve as “reality tags” that keep the negative self-evaluation from metastasizing outside the frame.

### Drone play

**Two attitudes (explicit):**

- **A: I am choosing and monitoring.** “I am consenting; there is a meta-self tracking boundaries.”
- **B: I am programmed / depersonalized.** “I function as a role with reduced individuality, governed by rules.”

**Anchor(s) of coexistence:**

- **Algorithmic (procedural) anchor:** drone play is almost a demonstration of apparatus: rules and transitions generate a “state” that feels impersonal, sustained by sequence integrity.
- **Token anchor:** cues (visual, verbal, or contextual) can operate like a switch that turns on the drone-state while preserving the meta-self’s reality-testing.

#### Table: Two attitudes and stabilizers by play type (high-level)

| Play type | Two attitudes (A / B) | Dominant stabilizer candidates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CBT | Safety/integrity / Vulnerability-jeopardy | Procedure (constraints, sequencing); sometimes token-gating |
| Bondage | Autonomy / Suspended autonomy | Rite transitions; speech-act law; sometimes token cues |
| Humiliation/degradation | Dignity intact / Dignity negated in-frame | Speech-act law; bracketing rites; tokens as “frame markers” |
| Drone play | Choosing meta-self / Programmed self | Algorithmic rules + transitions; cue tokens |

The educational payoff of writing it this way is that “two attitudes” is not a moral verdict; it is a *design feature* of many kink scenes. The real question becomes: what anchor keeps the contradiction coherent, bounded, and revisable?

## 2) Freud’s “Splitting of the Ego…”: can kink scenes be controlled two-attitudes without pathology?

Freud’s “Splitting of the Ego in the Process of Defence” generalizes what fetishism dramatizes: the mind may maintain incompatible attitudes rather than integrating them, and this can appear not only in fetishism and psychosis but in neurosis more broadly. In Freud’s metapsychology, this splitting is a defensive response to conflict; it avoids intolerable affect by partitioning rather than synthesizing. The crucial pedagogical challenge—especially when bringing Freud into contemporary BDSM contexts—is to avoid a lazy equivalence: “two attitudes = pathology.” Instead, the more precise educational question is whether BDSM scenes can function as *deliberately bounded, reality-tested compartmentalizations* that resemble “two attitudes” structurally while differing ethically and clinically in function.

A helpful way to test this is to compare **splitting-as-defense** to **splitting-as-staging**. Defense splitting is typically marked by rigidity, loss of choice, and impaired reality testing: the contradiction is not *played with* but *suffered as compulsory*. By contrast, staged two-attitudes can be understood as a controlled oscillation or bracketed coexistence: “I know it’s a scene” and “I feel it as real-enough.” The difference is not metaphysical—it is operational: does the person retain meta-level governance, and does the contradiction remain revisable?

Freud’s framework gives you several discriminators. First, **independence of the attitudes**: in pathological splitting, the two attitudes can become walled off so that one does not inform the other (e.g., knowledge does not moderate behavior). In healthy staged play, you often want the opposite: the “scene attitude” is intense, but the “safety attitude” remains functionally in command at a higher level. This is conceptually akin to maintaining a strong “as if” without losing the “is.” The fact that BDSM communities often emphasize negotiation phases, consent norms, and post-scene processing can be read (non-romantically) as cultural techniques for ensuring that the safety attitude is not merely present but operative. Empirical and conceptual work on consent and negotiation in BDSM underscores that consent is not a one-time switch but a constructed, interactional process, which—when done well—functions like a meta-regulator over intense states.

Second, **cost of integration**. In Freud, splitting defends against an integration that would be too painful; the mind chooses partition to avoid collapse. In BDSM, the “two attitudes” may be sought not because integration is impossible, but because *non-integration is aesthetically and affectively productive*: the pleasure/meaning lies in keeping autonomy and surrender, dignity and negation, selfhood and programmed persona in a tense coexistence. That looks like Freud’s structure, but its function differs: it is not necessarily an avoidance of truth; it can be a structured exploration of affect under controlled conditions.

Third, **persistence outside the frame**. A Freudian suspicion of pathology grows when the split becomes sticky—when the “scene attitude” leaks into daily life and starts overriding ordinary self-respect, bodily care, or relational reciprocity. Here the earlier ethical distinction (mythic intensity vs mythic rigidity) becomes clinically relevant: rigidity is when renegotiation is punished, scope creeps, and the scene’s law becomes sovereign. This is also where the Deleuzian language of capture (rules colonizing more domains) and the Freudian language of splitting (contradiction maintained compulsively) begin to converge: a rigid apparatus can reinforce a split, and a split can recruit an apparatus to avoid integration.

Fourth, **repair and symbolization**. Psychoanalysis has long treated symbolization—putting experience into language and narrative—as a route toward integration. If BDSM play is followed by reflection, repair, and mutual recognition, the two attitudes may be metabolized rather than frozen. Conversely, if the scene becomes the only route to regulation and the person cannot tolerate ordinary affect without it, that looks closer to defensive organization. Contemporary scholarship on BDSM consent repeatedly notes the complexity of consent practices, including negotiation and community norms, while also warning against simplistic “contract solves everything” stories. That warning aligns with Freud’s broader point: mechanisms that stabilize contradiction can be adaptive in one context and constrictive in another.

So can these plays be “controlled two attitudes” without pathology? Freud’s own concepts allow the possibility, but only if you keep the locus clear: Freud is describing an ego defense; BDSM practice can instantiate a *cultural technique of bracketing* that resembles splitting structurally while remaining ethically distinct when it preserves choice, reality-testing, and reparability. The educational move is to treat “two attitudes” as a diagnostic *shape* and then ask what regulates it: compulsion and avoidance, or bounded staging and integration.

## 3) Reception lens: which critiques of Deleuze matter most when applying him to contemporary BDSM?

Applying Deleuze’s masochism model to contemporary BDSM is tempting because it seems to “fit” modern kink culture’s emphasis on negotiation, consent, and explicit role/world construction. Yet that very fit triggers the most relevant reception critiques: **category realism**, **over-dichotomization**, and **gendered/sexuality assumptions** embedded in his source material and typology.

Start with category realism. Alison Moore’s critique argues that Deleuze, while depathologizing masochism, can treat sexological/psychiatric categories too “realistically,” abstracting them from their historical production and thereby reifying “sadism” and “masochism” as if they were natural kinds rather than historically contingent constructs. This is highly relevant to BDSM application because contemporary kink communities are not organized around nineteenth-century clinical taxonomies; they are organized around practices, identities, and communities with shifting vocabularies, often self-defined and intersectional. If you import Deleuze’s apparatus wholesale, you risk mapping lived diversity onto a pre-shaped grid—exactly the kind of over-ontologizing Moore warns about.

Second, over-dichotomization: Deleuze’s famous rejection of a “sadomasochistic unity” is powerful as a conceptual corrective to sloppy psychologizing, and it has been defended as recovering difference. Yet critics and commentators have asked whether the disjunction is overstated, partly because Deleuze’s evidence base is heavily literary (Sade/Masoch) and conceptual rather than empirical ethnography of kink communities. The danger in BDSM application is not merely scholarly pedantry: if a practitioner’s lived experience blends dynamics that Deleuze wants to keep non-communicating, the theory can become prescriptive (“you can’t be that”) rather than descriptive (“you might be doing a hybrid apparatus”). That is a point where theory can harm understanding by policing identity categories rather than illuminating structure.

Third, gendered assumptions. Moore and related discussions (including Beckman/Blake threads) highlight that Deleuze’s reconfiguration of paternal law in favor of maternal rule can still carry problematic imaginaries of “the female beloved” and gendered roles. This matters acutely in contemporary BDSM, where gender configurations are diverse and not reducible to a male masochist/female dominatrix template. The critique here is not “Deleuze is wrong,” but that his typology may be tethered to a historically specific pornological archive. Applying it today requires either translating his operators (contract, law inversion, rite, myth) into **role-agnostic** and **gender-plural** terms, or else acknowledging the mismatch.

Fourth, the “contract” problem. Deleuze’s apparatus privileges contract as a generator of law and rite; this resonates with BDSM’s popular imagery of contracts, checklists, and safewords. But BDSM scholarship also problematizes overly liberal, simplistic notions of consent-as-contract: consent is dynamic, relational, embedded in communities and power gradients, and not fully captured by a one-time agreement. This critique becomes a direct warning about Deleuzian “capture”: if you treat contract as magically legitimating everything that follows, you can miss how renegotiation costs rise, how coercive drift occurs, or how a “law world” can become rigid. In other words, Deleuze’s own apparatus language can help diagnose the ethical risk, but only if you resist using “contract” as a moral shield.

Fifth, empirics and pluralism. Contemporary BDSM is not one apparatus; it is a set of overlapping microcultures. Deleuze gives a high-level machine; ethnographic and empirical work gives situated practices (negotiation phases, norms, disputes, and consent troubles). The reception critique that Deleuze’s “empirical knowledge enters primarily as anecdote” (as some commentators put it) becomes most relevant here: you can use Deleuze productively if you treat him as a **formal vocabulary** for patterns (law inversion, rite sequencing, mythic transpersonalization), not as an authoritative taxonomy that overrides lived hybridity.

The most educational stance, then, is “Deleuze as operator-set, not category police.” Use contract/law/rite/myth to describe *how* a scene produces intensity (and how it risks rigidity), but keep Moore’s warning in view: do not smuggle historical clinical kinds back in as naturalized truths. The same goes for the sadism/masochism disjunction: treat it as a heuristic that illuminates different scene logics, while allowing contemporary practice to exhibit composites and transitions that the original archive may not have captured.
