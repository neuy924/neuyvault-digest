## Abstract (English)

Erotic disgust poses a puzzle for the sociology of sexuality and cultural theory: disgust typically functions as an avoidance affect that polices bodily and moral boundaries, yet in kink and BDSM it can be actively sought and eroticized. This paper argues that the phenomenon becomes intelligible when we treat scenes as scripted events that recruit two distinct affective operations—boundary-work and status-work—rather than as isolated acts or private preferences. We formalize a scene-level base plane with Disgust (D) and Humiliation (H) as separable axes, and extend it in two minimal ways tailored to different analytic tasks. For practice-facing ethics and harm reduction, we add objective Risk (R) to distinguish symbolic “dirt” from biomedical hazard (D×H×R). For theory-facing explanation, we add Symbolic Distance (S), capturing the degree to which dirtying and degradation are held as role-bounded “as-if” performance versus literal truth-claims (D×H×S). Drawing on abjection theory, purity/pollution, stigma, and sexual script theory, we show how historically classed and racialized “dirtying” tropes can collapse symbolic distance and amplify scene leakage into shame residue and stigma spillover even when risk is low. We conclude with testable hypotheses and a mixed-method roadmap combining vignette manipulations of framing and identity proximity with dyadic diary designs and narrative coding of abjection markers, enabling empirical adjudication between trait-only, discourse-only, and dual-use accounts.

## 1\. Introduction: the paradox of erotic disgust and the problem of explanation

Disgust is ordinarily recruited to keep bodies and selves intact. It is the felt signal that something is contaminating, boundary-violating, or “not-me,” a fast affective verdict that organizes avoidance and restores order. Erotic life, by contrast, is often imagined as the domain of attraction and approach. Erotic disgust in kink and BDSM therefore appears as a paradox only under a narrow premise: that aversion and arousal must be opposites, and that sex is primarily the pursuit of pleasant sensation rather than the manipulation of meaning, power, taboo, and affective intensity. Once we treat sex as a socially scripted practice, and affects as culturally charged rather than merely private, the paradox becomes a research problem with structure: how does disgust become erotically usable, socially legible, and ethically governable within particular scenes, communities, and histories?

A strongly individualist account tends to collapse erotic disgust into either pathology (“something is wrong”) or preference (“people like what they like”). Both moves miss what is most sociologically distinctive: erotic disgust is rarely encountered as raw sensation alone. It is typically staged through negotiated roles, genre conventions, language registers, and closure practices that make it intelligible as play rather than panic, and as erotic rather than merely repellent. These are sexual scripts in the classic sociological sense—culturally available templates that become interpersonal negotiations and intrapsychic fantasies. They are also cultural technologies for managing the intensity of boundary affects. Disgust is uniquely powerful in this regard because it is boundary-dense: it does not just communicate dislike, but threatens contamination and category disorder. That potency is why disgust is frequently mobilized in stigmatization and hierarchy-making, and it is also why disgust can be repurposed as erotic charge under consent.

This paper approaches erotic disgust as boundary-play under conditions of framing and containment, and it does so by separating two affects that are often conflated in everyday talk about “dirty” kink: disgust and humiliation. Humiliation is not simply disgust with a social accent; it is a distinct threat to the social self—face, rank, worth, and the felt gaze of others. Disgust marks “keep away”; humiliation marks “you are lowered.” They can co-occur, but they need not. A scene can be rich in contamination cues while remaining affectionate and non-degrading, and a scene can be profoundly humiliating while remaining “clean” in any sensory sense. Treating these as separable axes does more than tidy terminology; it changes what we can explain. It allows us to model why superficially similar practices yield divergent experiences across partners, and why the same words can feel like playful filth one day and identity injury the next.

The core analytic device is a scene-level coordinate system: Disgust (D) × Humiliation (H). The D×H plane identifies the affective and semiotic content of a scene—the kind of boundary-work and status-work being done. Yet two additional sources of variance repeatedly destabilize discussion and policy: hazard and framing. First, “dirty” is frequently conflated with danger. But objective physical and biomedical hazard does not map cleanly onto felt disgust or humiliation; symbolic disgust can be high when hazard is low, and hazard can be high in scenes with minimal disgust content. Second, “dirty” is frequently conflated with truth. Yet the same cue can be experienced as theatrical “as-if” or as a literal statement about the person’s worth, identity, and place in a wider social hierarchy. The latter is where stigma histories and racialized/classed “dirtying” tropes matter: they arrive with inherited social charge that can collapse the play frame unless carefully bounded.

To keep these problems analytically distinct, the paper formalizes two minimal third-axis extensions tailored to different audiences. For applied ethics and harm-reduction, the most useful third axis is Risk (R): an estimate of objective hazard profile independent of subjective intensity. For cultural-theoretical explanation, the most useful third axis is Symbolic Distance (S): the degree to which the scene is processed as role-bounded performance rather than literal truth-claim (the “truthiness” of degradation or dirtiness). These two 3D models are not competitors. They are translation devices between the governance of safety and the theory of meaning. Together, they enable descriptions that ordinary discourse blurs: “high disgust, low humiliation, low risk, high symbolic distance” is not the same social object as “high disgust, high humiliation, moderate risk, low symbolic distance,” even if outsiders would label both simply “disgusting.”

The theoretical architecture draws on several traditions that converge on boundary affects and social order: purity/pollution as classificatory maintenance (associated with Mary Douglas), abjection as disturbance of subject/object borders (associated with Julia Kristeva), stigma as the management of discrediting attributes (associated with Erving Goffman), liminality as structured passage and reintegration (associated with Victor Turner), and affect as the circulation and “stickiness” of emotions that attach to bodies and signs (associated with Sara Ahmed). The point is not to stack citations like bricks, but to use these frameworks to specify mechanisms: how disgust and humiliation become charged, how they are staged, how they stick (or fail to stick) after the scene, and how communities build norms that contain both hazard and meaning.

The contribution is therefore dual. Substantively, it treats erotic disgust not as a curiosity but as a privileged site where modern boundary politics—classed and racialized cleanliness, sexual respectability, moral disgust, and the governance of risk—are condensed into intimate practice. Analytically, it offers a compact model that keeps meaning and hazard separable while remaining empirically tractable. The introduction stakes a claim that will be tested later: outcomes such as shame residue, relationship “scene leakage,” concealment motivation, and intra-community boundary work should vary systematically not merely with intensity, but with where a scene sits in D×H space and with how symbolic distance (S) and objective risk (R) modulate what the scene is allowed to mean and what it is allowed to do.

## 2\. Disgust and humiliation as social affects: boundary-work and status-work

Disgust and humiliation are frequently bundled together in everyday talk about “dirty” sex, yet they operate as different social affects with different objects and different social consequences. Disgust is oriented toward boundary integrity: it marks substances, proximities, or categories as contaminating, out of place, or dangerously intimate in the wrong way. Humiliation is oriented toward the social self: it marks a person as lowered, exposed, discredited, or made to appear unworthy in the eyes of an actual or imagined audience. Both are negative-valence affects that can be erotically recruited, but they recruit different cultural materials and they do different work within scenes. Treating them as separable is therefore not just conceptual hygiene; it is an analytic decision that makes visible the social machinery that turns “yuck” and “shame” into erotic intensity.

Work in emotion research often describes disgust as domain-structured rather than monolithic. A pragmatic translation into sociological terms is that disgust is an adaptable boundary system with multiple entry points: pathogen or contamination cues, sexual boundary cues (especially around fluids, mixing, and bodily excess), and moral boundary cues (where “disgusting” becomes a way of enforcing group norms). The point is not to claim that these domains are biologically fixed compartments, but that they are socially available routes through which disgust can be elicited, narrated, and managed. This is why the same word—dirty—can mean “germy,” “sexually excessive,” “low-class,” “immoral,” or “taboo,” sometimes all at once. The domain idea matters because it provides a vocabulary for teasing apart what kind of boundary is being played with in erotic disgust: bodily contamination, sexual propriety, or moral respectability. In the sociology of sexuality, these are not mere psychological preferences; they are historically and institutionally organized boundary regimes that circulate through hygiene education, religious discourse, pornography genres, law, and the everyday policing of respectability.

Humiliation, by contrast, is not primarily about contamination but about rank, face, and legibility. It is the affect that accompanies being made “less” in a social sense—laughed at, scolded, exposed, demeaned, or forced into a position of dependence and submission that feels witnessed. Even in private, humiliation often carries an implicit audience because social selves are built for being seen. This makes humiliation uniquely sensitive to language, tone, and staging: a phrase can function as playful degradation in one script and as a cutting truth-claim in another; a gesture can be erotic as a ritual of surrender or harmful as a performance of contempt. The difference is not reducible to content alone. It hinges on what the scene asks the person to believe about themselves and what it asks the relationship to ratify.

In BDSM contexts, disgust and humiliation are often braided because “dirtying” someone is a culturally efficient way of lowering them, and lowering someone is a culturally efficient way of making their body feel available for boundary crossing. Yet the braid is contingent rather than necessary. A scene can be high in disgust salience and low in humiliation when contamination cues are approached as intense intimacy under care—where “dirty” is aesthetic rather than evaluative, and the partner is not treated as lesser. Conversely, a scene can be high in humiliation and low in disgust when the erotic charge is status reversal, exposure, or discipline without any emphasis on filth or abjection. This separability is precisely what the D×H model captures: Disgust (D) indexes boundary-work; Humiliation (H) indexes status-work. Together they define a base plane of erotic meaning that can be compared across scenes without prematurely moralizing them.

The D×H plane is a methodological choice as much as a theory claim. It shifts the unit of analysis from isolated “acts” to scenes as scripted events. In practice, acts do not carry fixed affective meanings. Spitting, kneeling, being called “dirty,” being made to clean, being denied pleasure—these can register as contamination, devotion, comedy, punishment, tenderness, or identity injury depending on the script, the relationship, the cultural charge of the cues, and the degree of symbolic framing. By anchoring analysis in D and H, we can ask better questions about why a cue functions erotically in one context and aversively in another, and we can separate disagreements that are otherwise mystified (“I thought this was hot” versus “I felt degraded”) into more specific misalignments in boundary-work and status-work.

This move also creates a bridge between sociology and cultural theory. Disgust, in high-D scenes, is the affective signature of abjection and pollution logics; humiliation, in high-H scenes, is the affective signature of face-work, stigma threat, and social visibility. Their intersection is where erotic practice becomes most sociologically revealing, because it brings private arousal into contact with public hierarchies: classed cleanliness, racialized “dirtying,” gendered respectability, and the moral economies that decide which bodies are allowed transgression without punishment. The aim in formalizing D×H is therefore not to reduce erotic life to two numbers, but to build a parsimonious map that can carry richer theories—scripts, stigma, abjection, liminality—without collapsing back into vague talk of “taboo.”

A final note concerns measurement and translation. Emotion research offers tools for describing disgust sensitivity and moral disgust tendencies; sociology offers tools for stigma, concealment, and community norm adherence; cultural theory offers tools for reading abjection and boundary disturbance. Section 2’s contribution is to align these toolkits by clarifying what D and H are meant to represent at the scene level, so later sections can specify moderators (symbolic distance, identity proximity, audience, closure) and outcomes (shame residue, scene leakage, stigma management) without conflating hazard with disgust or degradation with disgust.

## 3\. Abjection and purity/pollution: why disgust carries erotic charge

Disgust is not merely a sensory dislike; it is an interpretive force that draws borders around bodies and persons. This is why it travels so readily between the material and the moral, between the literal and the symbolic. Abjection theory and the anthropology of purity/pollution provide two complementary accounts of that border-drawing power. Together they explain why disgust can become erotically generative in kink and BDSM, and why it is often experienced as more than “gross”: it can feel like an encounter with the edge of personhood.

Abjection names a particular kind of boundary disturbance. It is not simply that something is unpleasant, but that it threatens the coherence of the subject by blurring distinctions between inside and outside, self and other, person and thing, human and animal, clean and soiled. Fluids, wastes, smells, and bodily excess are paradigmatic because they are liminal: they are part of us until they are not, and their ambiguous status activates the anxious labor of keeping the self intact. In this account, disgust is the affective signature of the abject; it is the recoil that protects the subject from being pulled toward the not-quite-object that destabilizes identity. The eroticization of disgust can therefore be read as a controlled approach to what ordinarily must be expelled. The scene becomes an experiment in the permeability of the self: the participant chooses to dwell at the border rather than flee it, often discovering that the self can be loosened without being destroyed, especially when the loosening is held by a trusted other.

Purity/pollution theory adds a social grammar to this subjective drama. “Dirt” is not simply matter; it is matter out of place, a sign that a classificatory system has been disturbed. Pollution rules—what may touch what, who may enter where, what may be spoken, what must be washed—are ways societies stabilize categories that matter. Sex is an obvious site for such rules because it involves contact, fluids, and the redistribution of bodily boundaries, but also because it reorganizes kinship, gender, and social rank. The eroticization of disgust in BDSM can thus be seen as a deliberate disturbance of purity regimes. It is not only sensory transgression; it is symbolic boundary play. The intensity comes from approaching what is culturally marked as polluting, and from doing so in a context that simultaneously acknowledges the rule and suspends it.

This is where disgust’s erotic charge becomes legible as a cultural technology rather than a private quirk. Disgust is high-gain: it produces fast attentional capture and “stickiness,” saturating a moment with salience. In scenes, this can function as an intensity multiplier. A small cue can reorganize the entire affective field: attention narrows, time thickens, and the relational dynamic becomes vivid. Disgust, in other words, can be used to make the scene feel consequential. The question is not whether disgust is “really” erotic, but how erotic practice recruits disgust’s boundary power under a script that contains its dangers.

The D×H model helps specify the different ways abjection and pollution logics enter BDSM scenes. High-D/Low-H configurations often resemble “abjection without contempt.” The script leans toward tender containment: the participant is brought to the edge of contamination while being held as valuable, sometimes with a ritualized or almost clinical care that keeps the abject from becoming a verdict about personhood. Here, purity/pollution is played as a controlled disorder that is then restored, and the restoration can itself be erotically meaningful—washing, cleaning, re-clothing, re-naming, praise. High-D/High-H configurations intensify the abject by attaching it to status and social self: “dirtying” becomes a means of lowering. The scene may stage the participant as waste, animal, object, or disposable—forms of abjection that are no longer merely about substances but about the subject’s social existence. This can be erotically powerful precisely because it dramatizes the unmaking of personhood under the sovereign control of another, and because it creates the possibility of a return: to be re-admitted as a person through deliberate reintegration.

The social and political stakes enter because purity regimes are not neutral. They are historically saturated, and the figure of “the dirty one” has often been classed and racialized. When scenes borrow “dirtying” language or imagery with historical charge, abjection is no longer purely scene-internal; it resonates with wider hierarchies and can collapse the “as-if” distance that keeps play from turning into injury. This is not an argument for moral panic, but for analytic precision: the same abject trope can function as consensual intensity in one script and as stigma reenactment in another, depending on identity proximity, symbolic framing, and the aftercare mechanisms that restore personhood.

Ritual structure becomes crucial at this point. If purity/pollution is about restoring order after boundary disturbance, then kink scenes that eroticize disgust often require explicit closure to prevent lingering contamination feelings and social-self damage. The most stable scenes, in this theoretical vocabulary, resemble bounded ordeals: preparation, crossing, containment, and return. The return is not an afterthought. It is the phase where abjection is metabolized into meaning—where “I was dirt” becomes “we played with dirt,” and where “I was lowered” becomes “I chose surrender and was cared for.” This is also where the later Symbolic Distance axis will matter: the more the scene frames abjection as theatrical and role-bounded, the more easily the return can restore the ordinary self. When symbolic distance collapses—when the abject feels literal, true, or socially endorsed—the return is harder, and the scene is more likely to leak into shame residue or relational contamination.

The theoretical payoff of this section is a reframing: erotic disgust is best understood as the deliberate, scripted manipulation of boundary affects. Abjection theory explains why disgust feels existentially potent, why it can threaten the coherence of the self, and why it can therefore be erotically charged when chosen and contained. Purity/pollution theory explains why “dirty” is culturally meaningful, why it is available as a moral and status weapon, and why its eroticization inevitably touches social order even in private. Together they set up the paper’s later moves: a stigma-focused account of how “dirty” is distributed across class and race, a script-focused account of symbolic distance, and the dual-use modeling that separates meaning (D×H) from hazard (R) and from interpretive containment (S).

## 4\. Stigma, class/race, and the politics of “dirty”: from social sorting to erotic speech

If abjection and purity/pollution explain why disgust can feel existentially potent, stigma theory explains why “dirty” is never only a private sensation. “Dirty” is a social predicate with consequences: it sorts persons and groups, it organizes distance and exclusion, and it provides a moral vocabulary for declaring who is fit to touch whom. The social force of disgust is precisely that it masquerades as self-evident. When people say “that’s disgusting,” they often present an evaluation as if it were a fact of nature—hiding the historical labor by which certain bodies, practices, neighborhoods, and occupations were made to carry contamination. This matters for kink and BDSM because erotic disgust does not start from a neutral baseline. It starts from an already-politicized lexicon and an already-stratified distribution of “clean” and “dirty” in social life.

A classical sociological insight is that stigma is not simply an opinion; it is a mechanism of devaluation that produces practical constraints on intimacy, work, and public standing. “Dirty” stigma is especially effective because it couples moral judgment to bodily metaphors. It does not merely say “I disapprove”; it says “you contaminate.” In sexual life, this logic is amplified because sex already sits near the fault lines of purity regimes, respectability politics, and gendered expectations of control. In this sense, BDSM communities do not merely “have stigmas”; they are forced to develop sophisticated stigma management practices—selective disclosure, code-switching, norm codification, and the invention of legitimacy talk—to create spaces where boundary-play does not collapse into social expulsion. The ethics discourse that circulates in kink worlds can be read not only as safety practice but also as a counter-stigmatic infrastructure: it produces forms of respectability internal to the subculture while contesting external disgust.

Class and race complicate the picture because the modern meaning of “cleanliness” has been entangled with projects of discipline, refinement, and governance. “Clean” is not only hygienic; it is also a marker of self-control, proper taste, and legitimate personhood. This is why dirt metaphors travel so easily into humiliation: to call someone dirty is often to imply they are unrefined, undisciplined, low-status, and socially disposable. When erotic scripts mobilize “dirtying,” they therefore mobilize a pre-existing hierarchy, whether or not the participants intend it. The same holds for racialization. Colonial and postcolonial histories repeatedly framed certain populations as inherently dirty, disease-bearing, or uncivilized, using hygiene as a rationale for segregation and control. In such contexts, the “dirty” predicate becomes a historically loaded weapon. When that weapon is brought into erotic play—especially via language—it can generate intensity through taboo and transgression, but it can also reproduce injury by reactivating social abjection that is not contained by the scene.

This is where the Symbolic Distance axis (S) becomes sociologically indispensable. A scene’s content may be transgressive, but its consequences depend on whether its signs are processed as theatrical (“as-if”) or as truth-claims about the person. Historically racialized and classed “dirtying” tropes tend to erode symbolic distance because they arrive already anchored in real-world hierarchies. They can make humiliation feel less like role-play and more like social fact, turning high-H play into a form of identity devaluation even when everyone formally consented to “dirty talk.” Consent, in other words, is necessary but not sufficient as a sociological explanation; we need a model of how words do social work and how histories attach to them.

Dirty talk is a privileged site for seeing this process because language is not merely descriptive in sex; it is performative. It assigns roles, gives permissions, produces rank, and marks the body as clean or filthy in real time. A useful analytic distinction is between generic filth metaphors and historically loaded “dirtying” tropes. Generic filth metaphors operate primarily within the scene’s local script: they intensify boundary-play (D) and/or status-play (H) while remaining comparatively portable across partners because they are less tightly tied to specific social groups. Historically loaded tropes, by contrast, can convert the scene into a miniature reenactment of social sorting: they pull in a wider audience of norms, even if no one else is present, because the words carry an implied public history. This does not mean such language is never used consensually; it means its use is structurally higher-stakes and demands more explicit negotiation of meaning, speaker-position, and post-scene reintegration. The “return” phase—praise, re-naming, reassurance, debrief—functions here as a deliberate repair of personhood, attempting to prevent social abjection from sticking.

Stigma dynamics also operate inside kink communities themselves. Subcultures develop their own boundaries, and “dirty” can become an internal sorting mechanism: some practices are treated as low-status, unsafe, or disreputable, while others are coded as refined, aesthetic, or “responsible.” This internal hierarchy can be understood as a politics of respectability: a way to protect the community’s public face by displacing disgust onto an internal other. The D×H framework can track this process empirically by asking which practices are labeled “too much” and whether the label maps to actual hazard (R) or to moralized disgust and classed taste. Where the label maps primarily to taste and respectability rather than hazard, we should expect stronger links to stigma and concealment rather than to injury rates.

## 5\. Sexual scripts and symbolic distance: how “as-if” becomes erotic containment

Erotic disgust does not emerge from cues alone. It emerges from cues as interpreted through scripts—shared understandings of what is happening, who the participants are within it, what counts as permissible intensity, and how the event ends. Sexual script theory provides a vocabulary for this: cultural scenarios supply templates; interpersonal scripts translate them into negotiated interaction; intrapsychic scripts anchor them in fantasy, memory, and private meaning. BDSM scenes are unusually script-explicit. They often contain clearly marked roles, negotiated boundaries, recognizable genres, and ritualized openings and closings. This explicitness is not merely subcultural style; it is a practical solution to a structural problem: boundary affects like disgust and humiliation are volatile, and they need containment if they are to be erotically usable rather than destabilizing.

The concept of Symbolic Distance (S) formalizes that containment. S refers to the degree to which the scene is processed as “as-if” performance rather than literal truth-claim about the person. It is not the same as sincerity—participants can be deeply sincere about their desire while still holding the content as role-bounded. Nor is it the same as consent—people can consent to an act or word without sharing the same sense of what it means. S is instead a feature of framing: the interpretive gap between the utterance or act and the participant’s enduring identity. When S is high, the scene’s degradations, dirtying cues, and role claims are metabolized as theatrical intensifiers. When S is low, the same content is more likely to leak into self-concept and relationship belief: the line between “we played at contempt” and “you truly contempt me” becomes thin.

S matters because both disgust and humiliation are boundary/status affects that become dangerous when they generalize. Disgust can leave residue as lingering contamination or moral self-disgust; humiliation can leave residue as shame, mistrust, or a diminished sense of worth. Symbolic distance is the mechanism that prevents generalization. It works through multiple channels: explicit negotiation (“this is a role”), genre cues (protocol, costumes, ritual language), tonal markers (exaggeration, stylization, playful cadence), and closure practices that reassert ordinary personhood. In script-theoretic terms, S is the interoperability layer between cultural scenarios and interpersonal enactment: it ensures that the enacted script does not overwrite the person outside the scene.

Language is the most efficient carrier of S because words not only intensify D and H but also specify whether the scene speaks in metaphor or in verdict. Dirty talk functions as a speech-act technology: it assigns status, marks bodies as clean or filthy, and activates taboo charge. The same lexicon can behave differently depending on whether it is delivered as playful caricature, ritual incantation, disciplinary instruction, or sincere insult. This is why the earlier distinction between generic filth metaphors and historically loaded dirtying tropes is, in script terms, a distinction about default symbolic distance. Generic filth metaphors are often easier to keep inside the “as-if” bracket; historically loaded tropes arrive pre-literalized because they have been used in the world as tools of dehumanization. Without explicit counter-framing, they tend to collapse S, pushing a scene toward low-distance humiliation even when the participants intend high-distance play.

S also clarifies why audience and publicness are so consequential. Humiliation is structurally linked to being seen; even private humiliation often summons an imagined public. When a scene includes actual audience, recording, or the felt possibility of discovery, symbolic distance can shrink because the scene becomes legible not only to the partners but to a broader social gaze that is not under their control. This matters sociologically because stigma is organized around that gaze: the fear that the scene’s meanings will be reinterpreted through hostile scripts of deviance. In other words, S is not only a psychological variable but a social one. It depends on the availability of protective frames—privacy, community norms, trusted spaces—that allow the “as-if” to remain credible.

The analytic payoff is that S gives a principled way to explain variance within the same D×H region. High disgust and high humiliation can be experienced as cathartic transformation in one scene and as corrosive injury in another, not because the participants are different species, but because the scene’s script either maintained distance or demanded truth. When distance is maintained, the scene can operate like liminal play: roles dissolve and return, abjection is approached and then cleaned, status is inverted and then repaired. When distance collapses, the scene can become a stigma amplifier: the participant is not merely “lowered” in play but positioned as genuinely lesser, and the abject becomes less a chosen edge than a sticky social verdict. This is why symbolic distance will function as the third axis in the theoretical model: it translates cultural-theoretical concerns about abjection and stigma into a scene-level variable that can be operationalized and tested.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| **Stylized genre cues** (protocol, ritual language, exaggeration) | Increases S | Signals “performance mode”; stabilizes frame | Higher tolerance for high D/H intensity |

Symbolic distance is therefore neither a decorative concept nor a purely subjective mood; it is a structurally necessary condition for turning boundary affects into erotic material without collapsing into injury. It is produced by scripts, stabilized by community norms and privacy infrastructures, and threatened by historically charged language that carries stigma into the room. By formalizing S, the analysis gains a lever that can be used both to interpret cultural meaning and to generate hypotheses: how framing choices and identity proximity shift outcomes for scenes with similar D and H. In the next section, this lever will be paired with objective risk (R) to produce the paper’s dual-use model: one extension designed for theory (D×H×S), the other for practice (D×H×R), with a translation bridge that shows how hazard and framing jointly shape experienced threat and post-scene residue.

## 6\. The dual-use model: D×H×S and D×H×R as translation devices

The central claim of this paper is methodological: erotic disgust becomes analytically tractable when we separate the affective content of a scene from its hazard profile and from its interpretive framing. The D×H plane introduced earlier is the base layer. It locates a scene in terms of two distinct affective operations: boundary-work (Disgust, D) and status-work (Humiliation, H). This does not assume that scenes are reducible to numbers; it assumes that scenes can be compared by identifying which affective levers they most actively recruit. A scene rich in “dirty” cues may be high D without being high H; a scene centered on exposure, discipline, or degradation may be high H without being high D; and the intersection (high D and high H) marks the intensification of abjection through social lowering.

The problem is that D×H alone cannot adjudicate the questions that matter most in both theory and practice. Theoretically, the same D×H coordinates can yield different meanings and different residues depending on whether the scene is experienced as role-bounded performance or literal truth. Practically, the same D×H coordinates can correspond to very different hazard profiles, since disgust cues are not reliable proxies for biomedical risk. To address both problems without building an unwieldy multi-dimensional cube, the paper proposes two minimal third-axis extensions, each optimized for a different task.

The applied extension is D×H×RISK (R). Here R indexes objective physical or biomedical hazard independent of how “dirty” or humiliating the scene feels. It is the axis that consent frameworks, safety education, and harm-reduction governance implicitly care about, yet it is often obscured by moralizing language. By treating R as orthogonal to D and H, the model prevents a common category error: equating disgust with danger. A high-disgust scene can be low-risk if its cues are symbolic or carefully managed; a low-disgust scene can be high-risk if its techniques carry nontrivial hazard despite minimal “dirty” aesthetics. In practice-facing contexts—education, negotiation tools, community guidelines—this separation allows ethical discussion to remain concrete. Participants can decide what levels of D and H they desire while also negotiating what level of R they accept, rather than smuggling risk judgments through shame-laden vocabularies of “gross” or “degenerate.”

The theoretical extension is D×H×SYMBOLIC DISTANCE (S). Here S indexes the scene’s “as-if” framing: the degree to which dirtying cues and degradation are held as theatrical, role-bounded, and reversible rather than literal and identity-defining. S is the axis most directly connected to the cultural-theoretical concerns developed earlier: abjection, stigma stickiness, and the social history of “dirty” as a declassifying predicate. Symbolic distance is not merely an internal belief; it is produced and stabilized by scripts, genre cues, tone, privacy, and closure rituals. When S is high, the same D×H content can be metabolized as consensual liminal play—intense, even destabilizing in the moment, yet bounded and reparable. When S is low, the content is more likely to leak into the social self, producing shame residue, relational contamination, or stigma spillover, especially when humiliation targets identity-proximal wounds or when language imports historically loaded dirtying tropes.

The two extensions are designed to interconvert through a bridging construct: experienced threat and residue. “Threat” here is not simply fear; it is the degree to which the scene recruits the body’s avoidance systems (boundary alarm) and the social self’s defense systems (face alarm) in ways that persist beyond the event. R tends to increase threat by increasing objective hazard and the plausibility of harm. S tends to decrease threat by stabilizing the “this is play” bracket and by making transgression reversible in meaning. D and H specify which threat channel is active: D amplifies boundary alarm and contamination salience; H amplifies status alarm and social-self vulnerability. The same scene can thus be intense and safe in one configuration (high D/H, high S, low-to-moderate R) and intense and destabilizing in another (high D/H, low S, even at modest R), because the latter collapses the interpretive insulation that prevents abjection from becoming verdict and humiliation from becoming contempt.

This translation device matters because it shows why “symbolic” does not mean “safe,” and why “safe” does not mean “symbolically neutral.” Objective hazard can remain even under strong theatrical framing; conversely, symbolic framing can fail even when objective hazard is low, leaving psychological and relational residue. A model that keeps both extensions visible avoids the tendency of some cultural analyses to treat risk as mere discourse and the tendency of some practice guides to treat meaning as mere preference. It provides a shared language for cross-talk: cultural theory can specify how historical dirtying reduces symbolic distance; harm-reduction can specify how risk remains even when a scene is framed as play; sociology can specify how community norms create infrastructures that support both R awareness and S maintenance.

### Table 6. Two minimal 3D extensions and what each is for

| Model | Third axis | Primary question it answers | What it prevents | Typical outcomes it predicts best |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **D×H×R** (practice) | **R** = objective hazard profile | “How risky is this, independent of how it feels?” | Conflating disgust with danger; moralizing safety | Physical adverse events, boundary breaches, safety-relevant near misses |
| **D×H×S** (theory) | **S** = symbolic distance / truthiness | “Will this stay bounded as play, or leak into identity?” | Conflating consent with shared meaning; ignoring history/charge | Shame residue, scene leakage, stigma spillover, relational contamination |
| **Bridge** | experienced threat/residue | “How do hazard and framing jointly shape aftermath?” | Treating either risk or meaning as epiphenomenal | Next-day affect, repair efficacy, partner concordance on meaning |

Operationally, the model invites paired measurement at the level of scenes rather than persons. D and H can be rated immediately after a scene as perceived salience of boundary cues and status cues. R can be coded using an a priori hazard taxonomy that is independent of subjective intensity. S can be captured through ratings of truthiness (“How literal did it feel?”), role-boundedness (“Did it stay inside the scene?”), and concordance (“Did we agree on what it meant?”). The resulting coordinates can be used to generate hypotheses about which configurations produce pleasure, bonding, transformation, concealment pressure, or residue. Importantly, the model predicts not only main effects but interactions: high humiliation becomes more hazardous for the social self when symbolic distance collapses; high disgust becomes more likely to produce contamination residue when closure is weak; identity-proximal dirtying cues are most destabilizing when they reduce S in scenes already high in H.

Section 6 therefore provides the conceptual infrastructure for the final step of the paper: a hypothesis set and empirical roadmap that can test whether this dual-use model outperforms simpler accounts that treat erotic disgust as a trait, a pathology, or a generic “taboo.” By separating meaning from hazard and by formalizing framing as an axis, the model aims to make erotic disgust legible as a social practice: one that is intensified by boundary affects, patterned by stigma histories, stabilized by scripts, and governed by communities that must continually translate between what the scene means and what it can do.

## 7\. Hypotheses and empirical roadmap: testing a dual-use model

The dual-use model proposed in the preceding section is intended to do two things at once: translate cultural-theoretical mechanisms (abjection, purity/pollution, affective stickiness) into variables that can be measured at the scene level, and translate harm-reduction concerns (objective hazard, boundary breaches) into a form that does not moralize through disgust. This section therefore treats model-testing as an act of disciplinary bridge-building. A sociology of sexuality audience should be able to read the hypotheses as claims about scripts, stigma management, disclosure, and community governance; a cultural theory audience should be able to recognize abjection and boundary politics as mechanisms rather than metaphors. The empirical program is designed to detect not only whether D, H, R, and S matter, but how they interact to produce different types of residue: bodily contamination feelings, shame residue, relational mistrust, and stigma spillover.

### 7.1 Hypotheses (scene-level, dyad-aware)

The first family of hypotheses concerns the separability of axes and the non-reducibility of danger to disgust. If D and H are distinct forms of boundary-work and status-work, then they should vary independently across scenes, and they should predict different outcomes. If R is orthogonal to affective intensity, then objective hazard should predict safety-relevant adverse events even when controlling for D and H. These are basic validation hypotheses that prevent the model from degenerating into a new vocabulary for “intensity.”

The second family concerns symbolic distance as the key moderator for meaning leakage. If S captures how “as-if” the scene is held, then it should buffer the relationship between humiliation and shame residue, and between disgust and contamination residue. Importantly, this buffering should be strongest in the high D×H region where abjection is attached to status: the scene is doing the most radical work on personhood and therefore needs the strongest framing to remain play rather than verdict.

The third family concerns stigma and identity proximity. If historically loaded “dirtying” language and identity-proximal content carry inherited social charge, then they should reduce symbolic distance and predict increased concealment motivation, anticipated stigma, and internal boundary-work within communities. These effects should appear even in low-risk scenes, demonstrating that psychological and social residue are not reducible to hazard.

A compact hypothesis set that captures these families is:

1. **Axis separability:** D and H show partial independence across scenes; their covariance is context-dependent rather than fixed.
2. **Risk independence:** R predicts safety-relevant adverse events and near-misses over and above D and H.
3. **Symbolic buffering of humiliation:** At matched H, lower S predicts higher next-day shame residue and greater relational contamination (mistrust, withdrawal).
4. **Symbolic buffering of disgust:** At matched D, lower S predicts higher contamination residue (persistent “dirty” feelings, avoidance, rumination).
5. **Abjection interaction:** High D×H predicts more abjection-coded phenomenology in post-scene narratives (object/waste/boundary-collapse language), especially when S is low.
6. **Identity proximity effect:** Identity-proximal dirtying cues predict lower S and higher stigma spillover (concealment motivation, self-stigma, anticipated rejection), net of D, H, and R.
7. **Governance effect:** Stronger endorsement of community consent/risk norms predicts lower adverse outcomes at matched R by improving negotiation quality and closure practices.
8. **Translation test:** S moderates the effect of R on experienced threat and residue; high S reduces subjective threat more effectively when R is low-to-moderate than when R is high.

These hypotheses are intentionally framed as scene-level and interactional rather than trait-only. They allow a sociology of sexuality approach to treat outcomes such as concealment and boundary-work as dependent variables, while allowing cultural theory to treat abjection and purity/pollution as explanatory mechanisms that become visible through narrative markers and framing dynamics.

### 7.2 Study designs: triangulation rather than single-method proof

A single method will not capture erotic disgust cleanly. The roadmap therefore uses triangulation: experimental control to identify causal leverage, diary methods to observe ecology, and qualitative coding to detect abjection phenomenology and script logic.

**Study A: vignette experiment (script manipulation).** Participants read short, non-explicit vignettes that systematically vary symbolic distance cues (explicit role-bounding vs ambiguous truthiness), identity proximity (generic vs identity-adjacent “dirtying”), and publicness (private vs audience implied), while holding nominal D and H content constant. Outcomes include predicted D/H ratings, perceived S, perceived harm/benefit, and willingness to enact or consent. This study tests whether S is manipulable through scripts and whether identity proximity reliably collapses S.

**Study B: dyadic diary / experience sampling (scene ecology).** Couples or regular partners complete brief paired surveys after scenes over a period (e.g., 4–8 weeks). Each entry captures D, H, S, perceived threat, closure practices, and next-day outcomes (shame residue, contamination residue, intimacy, regret, concealment motivation). R is coded by researchers from activity descriptors using an a priori taxonomy or participant checklists that map to hazard categories. This study tests interaction hypotheses in real life and allows estimation of partner concordance on S and meaning.

**Study C (optional): narrative coding and/or ethnography (meaning infrastructure).** Post-scene debrief narratives are coded for abjection markers (boundary collapse, objectification metaphors, purification/return motifs) and for stigma-management talk (secrecy, respectability, norm policing). Complementary ethnography of community workshops and online discussions can examine how consent frameworks function as legitimation and how internal taste hierarchies distribute “dirty” stigma within subcultures.

### 7.3 Analytic strategy: testing interactions and meaning concordance

The primary analytic focus is moderation and concordance. Moderation tests whether S changes the slope from H to shame residue and from D to contamination residue, and whether identity proximity changes the slope from D×H to abjection-coded narratives. Concordance tests whether partner agreement on S predicts better outcomes independent of mean S, treating mismatch in framing as a mechanism of misfire. Multilevel modeling is suitable because observations are nested within persons and dyads, and because individuals differ in baseline disgust sensitivity and shame-proneness. These trait measures are not rejected; they are treated as background parameters that interact with scripts and community infrastructure. This is the point where sociology and cultural theory meet: traits shape what is possible, but scripts and histories shape what is likely.

This empirical roadmap also allows adjudication between rival explanations. A trait-only account predicts that outcomes depend mostly on individual disgust sensitivity; a script account predicts large framing effects in vignettes and strong S moderation in diaries; a stigma-centered cultural account predicts that identity proximity and “historical charge” predict concealment and leakage even when R is low. The dual-use model predicts all of the above in a structured way: D and H organize content; R organizes hazard; S organizes leakage; identity proximity and closure govern how social history and return rituals change what sticks.
