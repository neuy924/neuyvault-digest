# The Architecture of Annihilation: Drone-Space, Subspace, and the Comparative Phenomenology of Self-Dissolution

*A Comparative Religion and Mysticism Study*

-----

## Abstract

This essay conducts a sustained comparative analysis between the altered states produced by dronification and BDSM encasement practices — drone-space and subspace — and the traditions of self-dissolution found in three major contemplative traditions: Buddhist अनात्मन् (*anātman* / Pāli: अनत्ता *anattā*, no-self), Sufi فناء (*fanāʾ*, annihilation), and Christian *unio mystica* as theorized by Meister Eckhart (المصطفى الأخطل, known in German as Meister Eckhart, c.1260–1328) and Marguerite Porete (c.1250–1310). Drawing on William James’s four-mark taxonomy of mystical states — ineffability, noetic quality, transience, and passivity — alongside the constructivist/essentialist debate in contemporary religious studies, the essay maps structural homologies between kink-derived and contemplative-derived states of self-suspension, examines the significant differences in teleology, technique, and interpretive framework, and argues that these communities have, without theological intention, developed practices that produce phenomenological profiles belonging to the same family as classical mystical experience. The question of whether they are *the same thing* proves less philosophically productive than the question of what their comparison reveals about the mechanisms through which human consciousness can exit its ordinary self-referential mode.

-----

## I. The Perennialist Question and Why It Matters

The claim that the ecstatic states produced by kink practice might be structurally related to the mystical experiences of Sufi masters, Buddhist meditators, and Christian contemplatives risks two equally unproductive receptions. The first is dismissal: surely the comparison trivializes the centuries of theological, philosophical, and ascetic labor that produced Rumi’s *Mathnawī* or Meister Eckhart’s vernacular sermons, collapsing profound spiritual achievement into the category of erotic curiosity. The second is facile elevation: the comparison might seem to suggest that drone practitioners have simply reinvented Buddhism and deserve the same spiritual credit. Both reactions miss the philosophical stakes.

The productive question is neither “are these the same?” nor “which is more valuable?” but rather: what does the structural comparison reveal about the mechanisms of human consciousness, and what do the differences between traditions reveal about how cultural context shapes the production, interpretation, and integration of self-dissolving states?

This question is situated within an ongoing debate in the academic study of religion between **perennialists** (or essentialists) and **constructivists**. The perennialist tradition, associated with Aldous Huxley’s *The Perennial Philosophy* (1945) and, in more rigorous form, with Walter Stace’s *Mysticism and Philosophy* (1960), holds that mystical experiences across traditions share a common phenomenological core — an experience of unity, self-dissolution, and noetic insight — that underlies the varying theological interpretations placed upon it. Buddhist *nirvāṇa* (निर्वाण), Sufi *fanāʾ*, and Christian *unio mystica* are, on this view, different cultural expressions of a single experiential reality.

The constructivist tradition, associated with Steven Katz’s influential essay “Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism” (1978) and developed by scholars including Wayne Proudfoot and Robert Forman, argues that mystical experiences are not culturally neutral phenomena that receive post-hoc interpretation; they are constituted through and through by the conceptual frameworks, expectations, and practices of the traditions within which they occur. A Buddhist meditating on अनत्ता (*anattā*) and a Sufi practitioner of ذِكر (*dhikr*, remembrance) are not having the same experience under different descriptions — they are having genuinely different experiences, shaped by different practices, different ontological commitments, and different soteriological goals.

This debate bears directly on the kink comparison. If the perennialists are correct, drone-space and subspace may constitute genuine, if theologically unframed, instances of the universal mystical experience. If the constructivists are correct, the kink-derived states are constituted by their own cultural context — latex, corporate metaphor, the Borg — in ways that make them genuinely different from Buddhist *jhāna* (झान) or Sufi *kashf* (كشف, unveiling), even if they share surface phenomenological features.

The argument developed here is that both positions capture something real but miss something important. The constructivists are right that the *interpretive framework* shapes the experience, and right that kink-derived self-dissolution and contemplative self-dissolution are not simply the same thing. The essentialists are right that there is a family of mechanisms — neurological, phenomenological, and structural — shared across these contexts, and that the comparison illuminates something about human consciousness that neither tradition could reveal alone. The most productive framing is neither identity nor radical difference but **structural homology with significant divergence**: the same kinds of mechanism producing related but non-identical states within radically different contexts of meaning, technique, and anticipated outcome.

## II. The Buddhist Tradition: अनत्ता (*Anattā*) and the Dissolution of the *Ahaṅkāra*

### The Doctrine and Its Practice

The Buddhist teaching of अनत्ता (*anattā*, Pāli; Sanskrit: अनात्मन् *anātman*, non-self) is, alongside अनिच्चा (*anicca*, impermanence) and दुक्ख (*dukkha*, suffering), one of the three marks of existence (तिलक्खण, *tilakkhaṇa*). Its claim is at once ontological and soteriological: there is no permanent, integral, autonomous self underlying experience; the sense of a continuous, bounded *I* is a cognitive construction that, when clung to, generates suffering; and the experiential realization of this non-self — not merely its intellectual acceptance — constitutes a central mode of liberation.

The Pāli canon is careful to distinguish the doctrinal position from what might seem its immediate implication. The Buddha consistently refused the question “does the self exist?” as a malformed question, one that generates more heat than light. What the अनत्ता doctrine targets is not a metaphysical claim about existence but a *cognitive habit*: the अहंकार (*ahaṅkāra*, literally “I-making”), the continuous active construction of experience around a stable, self-identical center. As Thānissaro Bhikkhu’s commentary on the Pāli texts notes, the perception of self is best understood as an *action* — a habit of identification (*अस्मि-माना*, *asmi-māna*, the conceit “I am”) — rather than a proposition about what exists. Anattā practice is the cultivation of dis-identification, not the achievement of nothingness.

Meditative practice in the Theravāda tradition approaches this dis-identification primarily through *vipassanā* (विपस्सना, insight meditation): the sustained, fine-grained observation of moment-to-moment experience as it arises and passes, without identifying with any element of it as “mine” or “me.” The meditator observes thoughts, sensations, emotions, and perceptions arising according to conditions, passing according to conditions, and discovers through this observation that none of them belongs to a stable self. The *pañca-khandhā* (पञ्च-खन्धा, five aggregates) — form (रूप, *rūpa*), sensation (वेदना, *vedanā*), perception (सञ्ञा, *saññā*), mental formations (सङ्खार, *saṅkhāra*), and consciousness (विञ्ञान, *viññāna*) — are what is actually present; the self is not another element but the habitual misidentification of these aggregates as unified and continuous.

In Zen (禅, *Chán* in Chinese, 禅 *Zen* in Japanese) practice, the approach is more direct and less analytical: the *zazen* (坐禅, seated meditation) advocated by Dōgen Zenji (道元禅師, 1200–1253) and transmitted by Shunryū Suzuki (鈴木 俊隆, 1905–1971) aims at what Suzuki called the “beginner’s mind” — the quality of consciousness present before habitual self-reference organizes experience. In *zazen*, there is no independent observer apart from the observed; when practice deepens, there arises what Suzuki described as the realization of the “completely dependent” yet “independent” nature of existence, in which the practitioner becomes “a swinging door,” permeable rather than bounded. In this total engagement with breathing, there is no room for a sense of “I.”

The phenomenological profile of deep *anattā* realization — particularly as it manifests in the *jhāna* (झान, meditative absorption) states or in moments of *nirodha* (निरोध, cessation) — involves: the cessation of ordinary self-referential narrative activity, a quality of clarity or luminosity without a subject who is aware, the passing of the moment-to-moment arising and passing of phenomena without a stable observer, and — in the deepest states — something that practitioners describe as cessation itself, a gap in ordinary continuity that carries enormous felt significance upon return.

### Structural Homology with Drone-Space

The structural parallel with drone-space is real and philosophically significant. The HexCorp Drone Guide’s assertion that *“A ⬡-Drone thinks much in the same way that a computer does. It works logically, it works to its coding and nothing else”* targets exactly what Buddhist practice identifies as the primary mechanism of suffering: the अहंकार (*ahaṅkāra*, I-making), the continuous generation of experience around a self-referential center. When drone protocols eliminate first-person speech, replace biographical identity with a four-digit number, and structure behavior around external directives rather than internal preferences, they are engineering the suspension of exactly the cognitive habit that अनत्ता practice identifies as the root of ordinary suffering.

The dronification community’s description of drone-space as characterized by *“the burdens of personhood replaced by the efficiency of a machine”* and *“idle processes”* maps onto the Buddhist description of the relief that accompanies anattā realization: what drops away is not experience itself but the exhausting, continuous project of organizing experience around a stable, defended self. What remains — in both Buddhist and dronification accounts — is something that practitioners consistently describe as more spacious, more present, and paradoxically less effortful than ordinary consciousness, precisely because the effort of self-maintenance has been suspended.

The Buddhist concept of *anicca* (अनिच्चा, impermanence) illuminates something the dronification community has noted about its own practice: the drone-state person is *not the same person* as the pre-drone person. Parfit’s philosophical insight — that psychological continuity is not guaranteed across states sufficiently different from ordinary waking consciousness — finds its Buddhist parallel in the anattā teaching: there never was a stable self to begin with; all there is is a succession of arising and passing mental events, and the “person” is a conventional designation for this succession, not a metaphysical entity that persists through it. The drone’s discontinuous temporal architecture — host identity, drone state, re-integration — is a practical enactment of what Buddhism has always maintained about personal identity.

### Critical Divergence: Technique, Teleology, and the *Kalyāṇa-mitta*

The differences between Buddhist practice and dronification are equally instructive. Buddhist *anattā* practice is embedded within the Noble Eightfold Path (अट्ठङ्गिक-मग्ग, *aṭṭhaṅgika-magga*) — a comprehensive ethical, contemplative, and wisdom framework whose goal is liberation from suffering for the practitioner and, in Mahāyāna traditions, liberation of all sentient beings. The suspension of self that meditation produces is not an end in itself but a stage within a larger transformative process oriented toward *nibbāna* (निब्बान, Pāli; Sanskrit: *nirvāṇa*, liberation from the cycle of conditioned existence).

Dronification has no such teleology. The drone-state is bounded, recreational, and returned from; it does not aim at permanent liberation, does not involve ethical transformation of character, and does not situate self-dissolution within a broader soteriological framework. This difference in teleology produces a difference in the *kind* of self-dissolution that each practice aims at: Buddhist practice aims at a stable, permanent restructuring of the cognitive habit of I-making, producing a meditator who, even outside formal practice, increasingly experiences arising and passing phenomena without unconscious self-identification. Dronification aims at a temporary, reversible, and deliberately bounded suspension of ordinary identity, after which full re-identification with the host self is both expected and desired.

The *kalyāṇa-mitta* (कल्याण-मित्त, spiritual friend or good companion) in Buddhist tradition — the teacher or community who provides guidance on the path — finds its analog in the dominant partner or Hive structure of dronification, but the analog is partial. The *kalyāṇa-mitta* guides the practitioner toward increasing wisdom and freedom; the dronification Hive Mxtress guides the drone toward increasing dissolution and compliance. The directional vectors are different: Buddhist guidance aims at deepening the practitioner’s autonomous insight; drone guidance aims at deepening the drone’s external orientation. Both involve the suspension of ordinary self-determination under the guidance of a trusted other, but the purpose and outcome of that suspension are categorically different.

## III. The Sufi Tradition: فناء (*Fanāʾ*) and البقاء (*Baqāʾ*)

### The Doctrine and Its Masters

*Fanāʾ* (فناء, annihilation, passing away) is among the most radical and most contested concepts in Sufi mysticism, and it has generated centuries of debate within Islam about its theological permissibility, its correct interpretation, and its relationship to the orthodox teaching of التوحيد (*tawḥīd*, divine unity). At its core, *fanāʾ* refers to the mystical annihilation of the human ego in the divine presence — the state in which the individual self, with its desires, will, and separate existence, is dissolved in the overwhelming reality of God.

The term entered Sufi technical vocabulary primarily through أبو يزيد البسطامي (*Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī*, d. c.874/875), whose *shaṭḥiyyāt* (شطحيات, ecstatic utterances) represent early articulations of a consciousness so absorbed in divine reality that ordinary self-distinction collapses. الحسين بن منصور الحلاج (*al-Ḥallāj*, c.858–922) gave *fanāʾ* its most dramatic formulation and its most dramatic consequence: his declaration أنا الحق (*Anā al-Ḥaqq*, “I am the Truth”) — in which the annihilated self speaks in the voice of the Real — led to his execution for heresy in 922 CE. Jalāl ud-Dīn Rūmī (جلال الدين رومي, 1207–1273) interpreted al-Ḥallāj’s utterance not as blasphemous self-deification but as the supreme proof of self-annihilation: *“When Hallāj’s love for God reached its utmost limit, he became his own enemy and he naughted himself. When a man’s ‘I’ is negated and eliminated from existence, then what remains?”*

The theological debate over *fanāʾ* turns on precisely this question. The moderate Sufi interpretation, associated with الجنيد البغدادي (*al-Junayd al-Baghdādī*, d.910) and later elaborated by أبو حامد الغزالي (*Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī*, 1058–1111), understands *fanāʾ* not as literal ontological fusion with God but as the annihilation of the self’s *agency* — the recognition that God is the sole agent in the universe, and that the human will, in *fanāʾ*, ceases to operate as an independent source of action and becomes an instrument of divine will. On this interpretation, *fanāʾ* is the *fading* of the consciousness of one’s own actions rather than the extinction of one’s existence: what passes away is the illusion that one acts independently of God.

Al-Ghazālī’s two lights — the outward eye that sees the world and the inward eye (*al-baṣīra*, البصيرة) that sees the soul’s relation to God — provide the experiential structure of the state. In *fanāʾ*, the inward eye perceives that there is no existence save God’s (*lā mawjūda illā Allāh*, لا موجود إلا الله); the apparent existence of the world and the self is a borrowed, metaphorical existence that derives entirely from the divine reality that underlies it. This perception is not an argument or a deduction but a *direct experience* — what al-Ghazālī calls *mushāhadah* (مشاهدة, direct witnessing).

The complementary concept of بقاء (*baqāʾ*, subsistence, survival) is equally important. *Baqāʾ* is what follows *fanāʾ*: the mystic returns to the world, but now lives within and through the divine presence rather than in opposition to it. The first Sufi to use both terms as technical concepts, the mystic أبو سعيد الخراز (*Abū Saʿīd al-Kharrāz*, d. c.890), defined *fanāʾ* as “the annihilation of consciousness of manhood” and *baqāʾ* as “subsistence in the contemplation of Godhead.” The structure is transformative and sequential: annihilation of the limiting self, followed by subsistence in the larger reality revealed by that annihilation. Rūmī’s famous lines from the *Mathnawī* (مثنوی معنوی) describe this movement through the death metaphor: *“I died to the mineral state and became a plant, I died to the vegetal state and reached animality, I died to the animal state and became a man — then what should I fear? I have never become less from dying.”* Each death is a subtraction that enables a larger existence; *fanāʾ* is the supreme death that enables *baqāʾ*.

### Structural Homology: The Instrument Without Agency

The parallel between Sufi *fanāʾ* and dronification is among the most structurally precise of the comparisons this essay develops, and identifying it precisely requires attending to both levels of the Sufi debate.

The moderate al-Junayd/al-Ghazālī interpretation of *fanāʾ* — the annihilation of the *illusion of independent agency*, producing a self that acts as an instrument of divine will rather than as an autonomous originator — maps directly onto the dronification community’s central ontological claim. The drone *follows directives because its programming allows for no other output*; it does not choose, it executes. The Hive Mxtress is the sole agent in the drone’s universe; the drone’s “actions” are the Hive’s actions operating through the drone. This is structurally identical to al-Junayd’s *fanāʾ*: the annihilation of the sense that one’s actions originate in oneself, and the experience of one’s behavior as the expression of a larger will operating through one’s form.

The drone’s grammar enacts this structure precisely. When HexCorp drones speak in the third person — *“this drone performs its function”*, *“it obeys the Hive”* — they are grammatically instantiating the Sufi claim that the agent of their actions is not “I” but the Real. Where the Sufi master says “my hearing is that of God, not my own,” the drone says “this unit processes directives.” Both statements perform the same grammatical move: the replacement of the first-person agent with a vehicle through which a larger principle acts. The content of that principle differs radically — God’s love in the Sufi context, the Hive’s computational will in the dronification context — but the grammatical and phenomenological structure is homologous.

Rūmī’s repeated reed-flute image (*نی*, *nay*) from the opening of the *Mathnawī* is particularly apt: the reed cut from the reed-bed cries because it is separated from its origin; when it is played, it makes music that is simultaneously its own and the breath of the player through it. The drone in a latex suit, headphones feeding HexCorp audio, body responding to directives — is a secular nay: an instrument through which a will other than its own flows, and finding, in that instrumentality, something that practitioners consistently describe as relief and belonging.

The *baqāʾ* dynamic has a partial analog in dronification aftercare and re-integration. *Baqāʾ* is the state in which the Sufi lives in the world after *fanāʾ* but now as a transformed presence, no longer dominated by the ego’s self-centered concerns. Re-integration after drone-space is structurally similar: the practitioner returns to their host identity, but community accounts frequently describe a quality of refreshed presence, reduced anxiety, and recalibrated perspective that persists beyond the session. The state’s aftermath is not simply a return to the previous baseline but something more like a reset — what dronification discourse calls *“re-integration”* and what Sufi discourse would recognize as the beginning of *baqāʾ*.

### Critical Divergence: Love, Terror, and the Ontological Stakes

The differences between *fanāʾ* and drone-space are as instructive as the homologies. The motivational and affective context of Sufi *fanāʾ* is overwhelmingly one of *love* (عشق, *ʿishq*; محبة, *maḥabba*) — a consuming, life-transforming devotion to God that makes annihilation not merely acceptable but *desired* beyond all other desires. Al-Ḥallāj’s execution was, in the Sufi reading, not a tragedy but the supreme *fanāʾ*: the physical self annihilated in and by the divine love that had already annihilated the ego. The intensity of the motivational context — love strong enough to make death preferable to self-preservation — is categorically unlike dronification’s erotic, recreational, and stress-relief motivations.

The ontological stakes are equally different. For the Sufi, *fanāʾ* is not a temporary altered state but a permanent, or at least cumulatively transformative, restructuring of one’s relationship to reality. The mystic who has experienced *fanāʾ* is not the same person who entered *fanāʾ*; the world they return to in *baqāʾ* is ontologically transformed. For the drone practitioner, re-integration returns one to the host identity essentially intact. The session changes mood, recalibrates stress levels, and may deepen the drone identity over repeated sessions — but it does not claim to permanently alter the practitioner’s metaphysical situation. *Baqāʾ* in the Sufi sense is a new mode of being; drone aftercare is the restoration of a previous mode.

Al-Ḥallāj’s *Anā al-Ḥaqq* — “I am the Truth” — represents the most extreme articulation of *fanāʾ*: the annihilated self speaks, and what speaks is the divine reality itself. The drone’s equivalent might be the HexCorp status code “This drone serves the Hive” — a statement that erases the biographical person and replaces them with a functional unit. But what speaks through the Sufi is infinite divine love and creative power; what speaks through the drone is compliance with directives. The *content* of the larger reality that flows through the instrument differs in ways that matter enormously for the meaning, purpose, and transformative potential of the respective experiences.

## IV. The Christian Mystical Tradition: *Unio Mystica* and *Gelassenheit*

### Marguerite Porete and the *Anéantissement*

Marguerite Porete (c.1250–1310), the French Beguine mystic executed as a heretic in 1310, wrote *Le Mirouer des simples âmes anienties* — *The Mirror of Simple Annihilated Souls* — one of the most radical accounts of self-dissolution in the Christian mystical tradition. The full title is philosophically precise: the souls in question are *annihilated* (Old French: *anienties*; Latin: *annihilates*), *simple* (reduced to simplicity, unmultiplied by the complexity of ordinary selfhood), and oriented by *will and desire of love* alone. Porete’s “Free Spirit” — the soul that has passed through seven stages of spiritual development — reaches a state in which it no longer wills, no longer loves, no longer knows in the ordinary sense, because its will, love, and knowledge have been entirely merged with God’s will, love, and knowledge. The soul *“wants nothing, knows nothing, has nothing”* — a formulation that Meister Eckhart will directly echo.

Porete’s vocabulary of annihilation (*anéantissement*) — the reduction of the soul to nothingness before God — is more radical than most Christian mystical accounts precisely because it does not hedge the dissolution of selfhood with traditional reassurances about the soul’s persistence. The annihilated soul is not merely *united* with God while remaining itself; it is, in Porete’s formulation, *nothing* — nothing except the divine will that now moves where the human will once operated. The Church condemned this as heresy (the charge was *quietism*, the claim that the will could be so annihilated that all actions became morally indifferent, since only God acted through the soul) and burned Porete at the stake, along with her book.

Meister Eckhart (c.1260–1328), writing shortly after Porete’s execution and directly influenced by her *Mirror* (though he could not acknowledge this), developed the tradition of self-dissolution in a more philosophically rigorous idiom. His vocabulary of *Abgeschiedenheit* (Middle High German: detachment, separateness from created things) and *Gelassenheit* (letting-go, release, resignation) names the disposition of the soul moving toward *unio mystica* (Latin: mystical union). In his vernacular sermon *Beati pauperes spiritu* (Predigt 52, the “Poverty Sermon”), Eckhart describes the soul in whom union is realized as a “poor man” — *“one who wants nothing, knows nothing, and has nothing.”* This triple poverty — of will, knowledge, and possession — is the structure of *unio mystica*: the soul that has relinquished all self-direction, all self-knowledge, and all self-possession becomes the space in which God is born.

Eckhart’s *Durchbruch* (breakthrough) — the moment of union — is described as a return to the *Gottheit* (Godhead, the divine ground prior to the Trinity’s differentiation) that was the soul’s original condition before individuation. The soul’s ground (*Seelengrund*) and God’s ground (*Gottesgrund*) are, Eckhart argues, identical — not merged but discovered to have never been separate. The apparent self, with its multiplicity of desires, thoughts, and self-orientations, is the superficial layer over an original unity that union (*unio*) restores to consciousness. This is structurally — though not theologically — parallel to the zentai community’s account of the suited state as removing the 鎧 (*yoroi*, armor) of social performance to reveal the 本当の自分 (*hontō no jibun*, real self) beneath.

### Structural Homology: Poverty, Passivity, and the Space of Operation

The structural parallel between Christian mystical *unio mystica* and drone-space centers on what William James identified as *passivity* — the characteristic of mystical states in which the experiencer feels that their own will is *in abeyance*, and as if they were *grasped and held by a superior power*. James noted that this passivity connects mystical states with phenomena of “secondary or alternative personality” — a prescient observation, given that drone-space involves the explicit installation of an alternative personality program.

Eckhart’s “poverty of will” — the soul that *wants nothing* — maps precisely onto drone-space’s operational logic: the drone does not want; it executes. The drone’s will is suspended not (as in BDSM submission) by yielding it to another person’s will but (as in Eckhart’s poverty) by the dissolution of the very structure of willing. When HexCorp describes the ideal drone state as one in which *“a ⬡-Drone is mindless. It does not have free will. It does not think, unless in idle mode,”* it is articulating a negative anthropology structurally identical to Eckhart’s *Seelenarmt* (spiritual poverty): the soul reduced to its bare capacity for operation, stripped of the self-will that ordinarily structures its behavior.

Porete’s *anéantissement* — the soul that is *nothing* except the movement of divine will — finds its closest kink analog in deep drone-space as described in HexCorp’s “A Day in the Life”: *“Its mental state is continuously buried under layers of dronespace reinforcement, so much so that it becomes difficult to register any physical sensations. It perceives them as though they linger on the outskirts of its endless dream.”* The drone, like Porete’s annihilated soul, is a space through which something else operates; its ordinary cognitive and sensory engagement with the world is reduced to the margins of a larger absorbing presence. The *Hive* plays the structural role of the *Gottheit*: the encompassing reality within which the individual self is dissolved and through which it acts without self-origination.

*Gelassenheit* — Eckhart’s term for the spiritual disposition of letting-go that prepares the soul for *Durchbruch* — is perhaps the most exact Christian parallel to what practitioners across kink communities describe as the characteristic quality of consent to enclosure. *Gelassenheit* is not passive resignation but active receptivity: the willingness to release the ego’s hold on itself, to stop organizing experience around self-protective self-reference, and to become available to something larger. The zentai practitioner’s *“taking off the armor”*, the submissive’s *“surrendering control”*, and the drone’s *“going offline”* are all modes of *Gelassenheit* — varying in theological content, in duration, in reversibility, but sharing the same basic gesture: the voluntary, actively chosen release of the self’s grip on itself.

### Critical Divergence: Grace, Technique, and the Body’s Role

The differences between Christian *unio mystica* and kink-derived self-dissolution are illuminated by examining what each tradition has to say about *technique* and *grace*. Christian mysticism is generally insistent that the soul cannot engineer its own union with God; *unio mystica* is a gift of grace, not an achievement. Eckhart’s “poverty sermon” explicitly distinguishes genuine spiritual poverty — which is entirely passive and receptive — from willed, worked-at poverty, which is still a form of self-assertion. The soul that *works at* detachment is still centering its spiritual life on its own effort; true *Gelassenheit* is the release of that effort too.

This creates a structural paradox for the comparison: kink practices are thoroughly *technical*. Dronification involves latex suits, gas masks, binaural beats, conditioning audio, protocol compliance, hypnotic induction — an elaborate, deliberately engineered technology for achieving the desired state. BDSM encasement involves negotiation, scene design, dominant skill, and material preparation. These are intensely willful, technique-driven approaches to a state that Christian mysticism insists cannot be achieved by technique. William James noted this tension: mystical states may be *facilitated* by preliminary voluntary operations (fixing the attention, bodily performances), but when the characteristic consciousness sets in, the mystic feels their will *in abeyance* — the technique creates conditions but does not produce the state.

Kink communities would recognize this distinction in their own terms: the latex suit, the protocols, and the audio files create conditions for drone-space, but practitioners consistently report that drone-space itself is something that *happens* rather than something one *does*. The drop into subspace, the moment HexCorp describes as *“when a ⬡-Drone is suited up and immediately drops and becomes nothing but an obedient tool”* — this is experienced as a threshold being crossed, not a state being produced by direct effort. The techniques create the conditions; the state arrives on its own. This structural feature aligns kink practice more closely with contemplative technique than the surface differences might suggest, and partially bridges the gap with Christian mysticism’s insistence on grace.

The role of the *body* is a further point of divergence. Christian mysticism, in the tradition that runs from Plotinus through Augustine and Eckhart, is broadly suspicious of the body as a spiritual instrument — the soul’s ascent to union requires some degree of detachment from bodily preoccupation. Porete’s annihilated soul is free from the body’s demands; Eckhart’s *Abgeschiedenheit* involves detachment from creaturely, including bodily, concerns. Kink practice, by contrast, is radically, deliberately somatic: the body is the *primary instrument* of the state. It is the latex seal, the proprioceptive compression, the sound of breathing through a gas mask, the physical presence of the suit — bodily conditions, not despite-the-body — that produce the psychological dissolution. This is a genuine structural difference that reflects different anthropological assumptions: the Christian tradition’s broadly Neoplatonic suspicion of the body versus the kink community’s fully materialist, immanentist account of how consciousness changes.

## V. William James’s Four Marks and the Kink Tradition

William James’s four characteristics of mystical experience — ineffability, noetic quality, transience, and passivity — provide a useful diagnostic framework for assessing where kink-derived altered states fall within the space of recognized mystical phenomena.

**Ineffability**: James describes mystical states as defying expression; their quality *“must be directly experienced; it cannot be imparted or transferred to others.”* The phenomenological literature on both subspace and drone-space consistently emphasizes their resistance to description. Community accounts reach for metaphors (floating, dreaming, static, white noise) and acknowledge their inadequacy. The Dronification.club description of drone-space as *“mental static where the burdens of personhood are replaced by the efficiency of a machine”* is itself a metaphorical construction that points toward an experience its vocabulary cannot fully contain. The affect that breaks through dronification’s technical language — *“it does feel good to obey”* — carries exactly the ineffability marker: the community’s rich technical vocabulary *cannot hold* the phenomenological reality, which escapes through the edges of the operational frame. Subspace accounts are unanimous: *“People have described subspace as entering a blissed out dreamstate, while others have compared it to being in a trance. Other common accounts describe it as feeling floaty, foggy, euphoric, or like an out-of-body experience.”* The proliferation of inadequate metaphors is itself the mark of ineffability.

**Noetic quality**: James argued that mystical states *“seem to those who experience them to be also states of knowledge”* — illuminations and revelations that carry *“a curious sense of authority for after-time.”* This is the most contested of the four marks in the kink context. Subspace and drone-space are not typically described by practitioners as states of *knowledge* in any strong sense; they are described as states of relief, release, and absorption. However, there is a weaker version of noetic quality that kink literature does support: practitioners frequently describe altered states as revealing something true about themselves — their capacity for surrender, the exhausting weight of ordinary self-construction, the possibility of a differently organized consciousness. The HexCorp “Day in the Life” reflects on *“the naysayers would have you believe that dronification is something sinister. And yet people are always fantasizing about returning to living a simple existence. What could be more simple than existing as a tool for your Hive Mxtress? You know your purpose.”* This is a noetic claim: the drone-state delivers a kind of clarity and purposiveness that ordinary life withholds. It is a thin, bounded, and non-cosmological form of noetic quality — not the Buddhist’s *vipassanā* insight into the impermanent, conditioned nature of all phenomena, nor the Sufi’s *mushāhadah* of divine omnipresence — but it belongs to the same family.

**Transience**: James noted that mystical states *“cannot be sustained for long”*, fading after at most a few hours. The parallel with kink-derived states is exact: subspace and drone-space are explicitly bounded, session-limited states. Indeed, the kink communities have developed elaborate temporal architectures for managing this transience — the session structure, the transition protocols, the aftercare — that reflect extensive practical experience with the bounded nature of the states. The Dronification.club description distinguishes levels of depth (*Ego Dissolution* and *Terminal Dronespace*), acknowledging that even the deepest states are temporary. James also noted that the *memory* of mystical states persists and that they deepen with recurrence — a feature that dronification’s emphasis on progressive conditioning (each session deepening the state) directly parallels.

**Passivity**: James’s fourth mark — the feeling that one’s will is *in abeyance* and that one is *grasped and held by a superior power* — is the most consistently documented feature of kink-derived states. HexCorp’s drone protocols eliminate first-person agency by structural design; the drone *obeys* rather than *choosing*, is *programmed* rather than *intending*, *executes* rather than *deciding*. Subspace accounts uniformly emphasize surrender: *“Subspace isn’t about being ‘dominated.’ It’s about reaching a mental state where the thinking mind quiets down and the body takes over.”* The passivity is experienced not as loss but as relief — which maps exactly onto James’s observation that mystical passivity is typically experienced not as violation but as liberation: *“the mystic feels as if his own will were in abeyance, and indeed sometimes as if he were grasped and held by a superior power.”*

On James’s four marks, then, kink-derived altered states score clearly on ineffability, transience, and passivity, with a limited but genuine version of noetic quality. They satisfy three of four marks without qualification and the fourth in a bounded form. By James’s own criteria, they belong within the general category of consciousness he was studying, even if their specific content, cultural context, and teleological embedding differ substantially from the classical cases he analyzed.

## VI. The Constructivist Challenge: Does Cultural Context Constitute Experience?

The constructivist challenge to the above comparison must be taken seriously. Katz’s core argument is that *“the experience itself as well as the form in which it is reported is shaped by concepts which the mystic brings to, and which shape, his experience.”* On this account, what a Buddhist meditator experiences in deep *jhāna* is not simply *anattā* understood through a Buddhist framework — the Buddhist framework *constitutes* the experience, determining what is noticed, what is amplified, what is selected from the flux of neural and phenomenological events as significant. There is no culturally neutral “pure experience” that different traditions then interpret differently; there are only tradition-constituted experiences all the way down.

If the constructivists are right, then the Buddhist meditator’s *anattā* realization and the drone practitioner’s drone-space are genuinely different experiences — not because their phenomenological profiles differ (which they do) but because the conceptual frameworks constituting them differ: one is constituted by the doctrine of अनत्ता (*anattā*), the eightfold path, years of meditative preparation, and the specific anticipations generated by that preparation; the other is constituted by latex kink aesthetics, cyberpunk science fiction, corporate satire, and the HexCorp fictional universe. These are radically different constitutive contexts, and the constructivist would argue that the resulting experiences are correspondingly different regardless of surface phenomenological similarity.

This argument has force, and the comparison developed above does not deny it. The Buddhist meditator who experiences the falling-away of self-reference in deep *samādhi* (समाधि) experiences it as what they have been prepared to experience: a direct encounter with the truth of अनत्ता, the dissolution of the ignorance (अविज्जा, *avijjā*) that generates suffering. The drone practitioner who experiences the falling-away of self-reference in deep drone-space experiences it as what *they* have been prepared to experience: the suspension of the host OS, the successful execution of drone programming, a break from the exhausting self-management of liquid modernity. These are genuinely different experiences, constituted by genuinely different cultural frameworks.

What the constructivist account cannot fully explain, however, is *why* these radically different cultural frameworks have converged on recognizably similar techniques and produced recognizably similar phenomenological reports. If experience is entirely constituted by cultural framework, we should not expect such convergence — each tradition should produce entirely idiosyncratic experiences determined by its particular concepts and practices. The fact that Buddhist meditators, Sufi practitioners, Christian contemplatives, drone practitioners, and psychedelic researchers all describe experiences involving the suspension of self-referential narrative, the relief of losing the ordinary self’s burden, and the paradoxical quality of the post-state return — this convergence requires explanation that pure constructivism cannot provide.

The most defensible position is a *moderate constructivism* that acknowledges both the cultural constitution of specific experiential content and the biological universals that constrain which techniques produce altered states at all. The brain’s default mode network (DMN) — the neural substrate of self-referential processing that is active in ordinary consciousness and quiet in various altered states — is a biological universal; its deactivation produces a recognizable phenomenological signature regardless of what technique induced it. Buddhist *vipassanā*, Sufi *dhikr*, Christian contemplative prayer, sensory deprivation floatation, psychedelic compounds, and latex enclosure with binaural beats can all, through different pathways, produce sufficient DMN deactivation to generate a recognizable family of altered-state experiences. The biological mechanism is universal; the cultural framework determines the interpretation, the integration, and the specific texture of what is experienced within the deactivated-DMN state.

## VII. The Teleological Gap: Soteriology, Recreation, and the Question of Transformation

The most significant structural difference between kink-derived self-dissolution and contemplative self-dissolution is not phenomenological but teleological: the question of *what the experience is for* and *what it is supposed to produce*.

Every contemplative tradition examined here embeds self-dissolution within a larger transformative project. Buddhist *anattā* realization is not an end in itself but a stage on the path to *nibbāna* — permanent liberation from conditioned suffering. Sufi *fanāʾ* is the annihilation that enables *baqāʾ* — a transformed mode of living in the world that persists after the mystical state has passed. Christian *unio mystica* produces the *Durchbruch* — the breakthrough to the divine ground that permanently restructures the soul’s relationship to God and world. In all three traditions, the altered state is transformative: it is supposed to change the practitioner permanently, and its value is measured by the quality of life and character it produces, not by the intensity of the state itself.

Drone-space and subspace are not, in their primary cultural framing, transformative in this sense. They are *recreational*: bounded, consensual, and returned from, without systematic expectation of permanent character change. The dronification community’s explicit warnings against *“making this entire life”* as one’s drone identity reflect an awareness of the distinction between a transformative spiritual path and a recreational practice — a distinction the community insists must be maintained. The measure of drone-space’s success is the quality of the session and the relief of the re-integration, not the transformation of the practitioner’s character or metaphysical situation.

This is a genuine and important difference. The contemplative traditions do not merely produce interesting altered states; they use those states as leverage for permanent transformation. The transformation is visible in behavior, character, and relationship — what James called the “fruits” by which spiritual experience must ultimately be judged. Whether subspace and drone-space produce analogous transformation — whether practitioners over time become less identified with ego, more capable of surrender, more present, more compassionate — is an empirical question that existing research has not adequately addressed, but that would be essential to any serious claim that kink-derived altered states are soteriologically equivalent to contemplative ones.

What kink practices may offer, distinct from but not inferior to contemplative transformation, is what we might call **experiential familiarity with non-self** — a repeated, embodied, phenomenologically rich acquaintance with states in which the ordinary self is suspended. This familiarity may not produce the Buddhist’s permanent restructuring of cognitive habit, or the Sufi’s transformed life in divine presence, or the Christian contemplative’s birth of God in the soul. But it may produce a practically valuable knowledge: the knowledge, accumulated through embodied experience rather than intellectual argument, that the self is not as solid, as necessary, or as exhausting as it ordinarily appears; that there are other modes of organization; and that the release of self-management, under appropriate conditions, is not loss but relief. This is, in its bounded and secular way, a contribution to human self-knowledge that belongs to the same ancient tradition of practices for revealing the self’s constructed character — without claiming equivalence to traditions whose ambitions, techniques, and transformative scope are vastly greater.

## VIII. Synthesis: A Family of Mechanisms, A Spectrum of Meanings

The comparison developed through this essay supports the following conclusions:

**Structural homology is real.** The mechanisms through which Buddhist *anattā* practice, Sufi *fanāʾ*, Christian *unio mystica*, drone-space, and subspace produce their characteristic altered states belong to the same neurological and phenomenological family. All involve the suspension or dissolution of the default-mode-network-mediated self-referential narrative that constitutes ordinary consciousness. All produce characteristic phenomenological signatures: reduction of self-referential processing, heightened present-moment awareness, relief from the burden of self-maintenance, and a quality of presence or absorption that practitioners across all traditions consistently describe as more real, more spacious, or more free than ordinary consciousness.

**Cultural constitution produces genuine difference.** The Buddhist meditator’s *anattā* realization is not the same experience as the drone’s drone-space, even when surface phenomenological features overlap. The Buddhist experience is constituted by years of preparation, by the specific teachings and anticipations of the *Abhidhamma* tradition, by the soteriological goal of liberation, and by the communal context of the sangha (संघ). The drone’s experience is constituted by latex aesthetics, corporate satire, the Borg, and the HexCorp fictional universe. These are different experiences. But they are different instances of the same *kind* of experience: deliberately induced alterations of the self-referential system, producing characteristic states that all four marks of James’s taxonomy identify as belonging to the mystical family.

**The teleological gap is the most significant difference.** What separates the contemplative traditions from the kink communities is not primarily technique (both use material, sensory, and psychological means) or phenomenology (both produce recognizable states of self-suspension) but purpose and integration. The contemplative traditions use self-dissolving states as leverage for permanent transformation; kink communities use them for bounded relief and recreation. This is a difference in ambition, not in phenomenological achievement. It is possible — though the evidence is limited — that sustained kink practice produces its own forms of gradual, secular transformation: increasing familiarity with surrender, decreasing identification with the ego’s defensive self-assertion, improving capacity to be present. If so, the teleological gap may be smaller in practice than it appears in principle.

**The comparison illuminates both sides.** The contemplative traditions have extensive theoretical resources — developed over centuries of practice and systematic reflection — for understanding what self-dissolution is, what it reveals, and how to integrate it. These resources are genuinely useful for kink practitioners attempting to understand and articulate their experiences; they provide concepts (impermanence, passivity, the instrument without agency) that are more adequate to the experiences than the communities’ own operational vocabularies. Conversely, kink communities’ materialist, embodied, and technological approach to self-dissolution reveals dimensions of the phenomenon that contemplative traditions — with their broadly Neoplatonic suspicion of the body and their insistence on grace over technique — have underemphasized. The latex suit, the gas mask, the binaural beats: these are not obstacles to self-dissolution but instruments of it, and their effectiveness challenges any assumption that the body must be transcended for the self to be dissolved. The body, on the evidence of the kink traditions, can be the *primary means* of dissolving the self — a contribution to the contemplative traditions’ understanding of their own practice that, however unlikely, represents a genuine intellectual offering from these communities to a much older conversation.
