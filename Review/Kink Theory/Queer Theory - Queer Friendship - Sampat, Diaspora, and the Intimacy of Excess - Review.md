# Title Options

1. **The Uses of Sampat**
2. **Too Much, in the Best Way**
3. **What We Call the Friend Who Knows Too Much**

**Subtitle:** *What a Malaysian slang word can teach us about queer friendship, diaspora, and the strange intimacy of adult life.*

## Abstract

This essay begins with a Malaysian colloquial term—*sampat*—and uses it to examine a form of adult queer friendship that is comic on the surface and structurally serious underneath. Moving across sociolinguistics, queer theory, medical humanities, diaspora, and life-course analysis, it argues that some of the most important relationships in modern adult life remain underdescribed because our available vocabulary is too heavily organized around the couple form. *Sampat* names more than overreaction or melodrama. It points to a style of social excess—too much fuss, too much attention, too much emotional weather—that can become a vernacular medium of intimacy.

The essay explores how such friendships operate as high-context linguistic systems, how they overlap with but differ from camp, and how they become especially significant for queer, diasporic, professionally successful adults whose lives are often overmanaged by competence. Medicine appears here not as the whole explanation but as one revealing example of emotional discipline: the oncologist in Ireland and psychiatrist in Malaysia are treated as figures through whom to think about composure, burnout, and the private need for socially excessive forms of relief. The essay argues that friendship can become central without becoming romance, that ambiguity is not always a failure of maturity, and that diaspora is as much a linguistic condition as a geographic one. In the end, *sampat* emerges as a small but powerful word for the people with whom one is allowed to be too much.

# I. A ridiculous word for a serious bond

There are relationships that become central to a life without ever acquiring the vocabulary one might expect for something so consequential. They do not arrive with the grandeur of romance, the social sanction of marriage, or even the tidy emotional dignity of what people like to call a “deep friendship.” They arrive instead under cover of silliness. They are disguised by bad jokes, by overreaction, by improbable amounts of texting, by mutual exasperation so theatrically performed that it begins to resemble a style of devotion. They are held together, in other words, by language that sounds too unserious for the burden it carries.

One of the better words for this, in Malaysian colloquial speech, is *sampat*.

It is not a glamorous word. It does not flatter. Nobody uses it to suggest poise, restraint, or the admirable virtues of adulthood. Depending on tone and context, *sampat* can mean dramatic, excessive, ridiculous, over-the-top, socially overinvested, slightly unhinged, and—often crucially—funny enough to be forgiven. It is the word one reaches for when someone is behaving as though a minor inconvenience were a civilizational collapse, when a group chat has achieved an intensity unjustified by its content, when a person’s level of fuss begins to feel less like proportionate reaction than a lifestyle. It is a word for too muchness.

And yet “too much” is not always a criticism. Sometimes it is the point.

What interests me about *sampat* is not merely its comic utility, though it has plenty of that. It is the way the word can slide, almost without warning, from social judgment into social tenderness. A person may be called *sampat* because he is annoying, overdramatic, shamelessly extra, or incapable of behaving in a measured fashion. But he may also be called *sampat* because he has crossed into a special category of intimacy, one in which disproportion becomes a kind of proof. Too many messages. Too much concern. Too much reaction. Too much nonsense. The behavior looks absurd from the outside and entirely coherent from within the relationship that sustains it.

This is one of the quieter problems of adult life: some of the bonds that matter most to us are not described very well by the respectable language available to describe them. “Friend” is often too thin. “Best friend” can sound juvenile, or else oddly formal, like something engraved in a glitter pen. “Chosen family” is useful, but broad. “Soulmate” is hopelessly overburdened, either embarrassingly sincere or too immediately romantic. The vocabulary of importance in modern life remains heavily biased toward the couple. We still assume, even now, that the relationships most likely to shape a person’s emotional world will eventually announce themselves in certain recognizable forms: romance, partnership, marriage, domesticity, public naming. What falls outside those forms is often made to seem supplemental, however intense it may actually be.

But many adult attachments are not supplemental at all. They are infrastructural. They regulate mood, absorb panic, carry memory, interrupt loneliness, witness career change, survive migration, outlast romance, and become the place where certain versions of the self are still legible. They are not always dignified. In fact, their very importance may depend on their freedom from dignity. They are sustained by rituals too repetitive or too embarrassing to look profound in retrospect: the friend who reacts to every complaint as though he has been personally wronged by history; the friend who can turn minor irritation into a three-act farce; the friend who appears in one’s day not with solemn insight but with exactly the wrong tone at exactly the right moment.

This is where *sampat* becomes more than slang. It becomes a clue.

The phrase that first caught my attention was this one: how an oncologist in Ireland and a psychiatrist in Malaysia can become *sampat* friends. On the surface, it sounds like the beginning of a joke, or perhaps the title of an essay that has mistaken specificity for depth. It contains too many seemingly incompatible things at once. An oncologist in Ireland suggests migration, expertise, professional seriousness, perhaps a life of high-functioning emotional discipline. A psychiatrist in Malaysia suggests another kind of trained seriousness, another profession built around bearing witness to distress without becoming engulfed by it. And then, without apology, the phrase swerves into the absurdly colloquial. *Sampat* friends. Not confidants. Not intimates. Not a transnational case study in queer diasporic attachment. Just *sampat*.

The tonal whiplash is part of what makes the phrase so useful. It stages a conflict between the respectable surfaces of adulthood and the far less respectable forms through which intimacy is often lived. Two men may become highly educated, professionally accomplished, socially mobile, geographically dispersed. They may acquire polished English, clinical authority, and the outward markers of adult competence. And still, the best word for the relationship that matters most may turn out to be a Malaysian colloquialism suggesting dramatic overinvestment and mildly embarrassing chaos.

That mismatch is not accidental. It tells us something about how intimacy works, particularly in adulthood, and particularly in queer life, where the socially sanctioned scripts for closeness have never been sufficient to describe the full range of attachment. It tells us that seriousness and ridiculousness are not opposites. It tells us that some people become central to us not through formal declarations, but through accumulated scenes of disproportion: the theatrical annoyance, the immediate overreading of tone, the mutual assumption that no complaint is too petty to deserve a five-minute monologue and no emotional state too unpresentable to be dressed up as a bit.

It also suggests that language matters in a more intimate way than we usually admit. There are words that do not merely describe relationships; they make certain relationships thinkable. *Sampat* is one of those words. It belongs to a social world in which excess is readable, in which annoyance can be affectionate, in which verbal overreaction is less a sign of instability than a vernacular art form. To call someone *sampat* is not only to say that he is too much. It is sometimes to admit that one has become accustomed to that excess, perhaps dependent on it, perhaps even quietly moved by it.

What interests me, then, is not just the word itself, but the structure of feeling it reveals. What kind of bond hides inside such language? What does it mean when a relationship’s deepest truth is carried not by solemnity but by comic exaggeration? Why do certain adult friendships—especially queer ones, especially diasporic ones, especially those between people who spend much of their lives being useful and composed—come to depend on modes of closeness that sound unserious from the outside? And why do the words we inherit for those bonds still feel so inadequate?

This essay begins with *sampat* because it seems at first too small, too local, too ridiculous a word to bear much analytical weight. But perhaps that is exactly why it can bear so much. Grand language often arrives after the fact, once a relationship has already been made respectable. Slang gets there earlier. It catches people in the act of becoming necessary to each other.

The question is not simply what *sampat* means. The question is what kinds of adult attachment become visible once we take the word seriously.

# II. Why *sampat* matters more than “chaotic” or “extra”

The first temptation, when faced with a word like *sampat*, is to translate it too quickly. One reaches for the usual contemporary substitutes: chaotic, extra, dramatic, unhinged, too much. These are not wrong, exactly. They catch part of the mood. They point toward the same basic social spectacle: a person reacting at an intensity level not justified by the facts on the ground, or behaving with an almost athletic disregard for proportion. But they do not quite get there. They flatten the word into a generic modern register of excess, and in doing so they miss the thing that makes *sampat* interesting in the first place.

Because *sampat* is not just a description. It is a social reading.

To call someone chaotic is often to describe his behavior from a slight distance. The word can be affectionate, but it remains observational. “Extra” is closer to judgment by style. It notices embellishment, intensity, overperformance. “Dramatic” can suggest heightened feeling, but it is still relatively clean. Each of these words works reasonably well in internet English because each is flexible, portable, and blunt enough to travel. *Sampat* does something else. It carries not only excess, but a whole little world of implied sociality around the excess. It suggests that the behavior is not merely large. It is inconveniently, hilariously, perhaps embarrassingly large in a way that other people must now manage, endure, laugh at, or be folded into.

That is why the word feels less like a trait and more like an event. A person may be *sampat*, but so can an interaction, a group dynamic, a friend’s response to a delay, the general atmosphere produced when feeling acquires too much volume and too little shame. The term is social not just because it is colloquial, but because it implies an audience. Somebody is always witnessing the excess, rolling their eyes at it, indulging it, or secretly enjoying it. *Sampat* is not simply internal disorder. It is disorder made relational.

This matters because intimacy often depends on exactly that kind of relational overrun. Some friendships are built on steadiness and tact. Others are built on a more dramatic economy: too much attention, too many reactions, too much interpretive labor expended on minor things. What looks from the outside like pointless escalation becomes, from within the bond, a sign that the relationship has achieved a special level of permission. The point is not that the reaction is reasonable. The point is that it is available.

That is where English approximations begin to fail. “Chaotic” is too aesthetic. It can even sound flattering, in the thin internet way that turns disorder into a personality accessory. “Extra” is too polished, too self-aware, too close to performance as surface. “Unhinged” can be funny, but it often pushes too far toward pathology or meme language. “Dramatic” lacks density. *Sampat* contains more social grain than any of them. It suggests fuss, overinvolvement, disproportion, mild shamelessness, and a level of emotional or behavioral overflow that has become, in some settings, almost a mode of care.

That last point is crucial. A friend who is *sampat* is not just behaving badly. He may be caring badly, or caring excessively, or caring in a way that disregards proportion but not attachment. He reacts too fast, worries too loudly, complains too elaborately, shows up in the wrong tone at the right time. From the outside, this can look exhausting. From the inside, it can feel oddly precise. Some people do not know how to love without a little overreach. They cannot simply “be there.” They must arrive noisily, with commentary.

The word also matters because it resists the smoothness of imported categories. Much of contemporary emotional life is increasingly described in a flattened global vocabulary of vibes. People are avoidant, chaotic, toxic, extra, triggered, iconic, delusional. Some of these words are useful; many are not. All of them travel very easily, and because they travel easily they often lose the local textures from which emotional life is actually made. *Sampat* slows that process down. It insists, however briefly, on a specific social world: one in which excess is not simply a meme but something shaped by local tone, family styles, urban multilingualism, gossip culture, and the ordinary theatricality of everyday speech.

This is not a sentimental argument for authenticity. The point is not that local slang is purer or morally superior to global vocabulary. It is that some words preserve forms of life that cleaner language obscures. When a Malaysian Chinese gay man in Ireland describes a certain kind of friendship as *sampat*, he is not just choosing a more colorful adjective. He is declining a whole set of more respectable or internationally legible alternatives. He is choosing a word that keeps the relationship tied to a lived social texture rather than translating it upward into the language of polished adulthood.

And polished adulthood, one begins to suspect, is part of the problem. The more serious and accomplished a life becomes, the less language it seems to have for bonds that are central without being institutionally confirmed. Adult life has a rich vocabulary for success, competence, resilience, partnership, and family. It has a much thinner one for the friend who is emotionally infrastructural but publicly unserious. That gap is partly why *sampat* feels so useful. It names a form of closeness without dressing it up. It allows a relationship to remain slightly comic and therefore resistant to premature dignity.

There is also something refreshingly indiscreet about the term. Many adult relational vocabularies are built around restraint. We admire what is measured, reciprocal, well-bounded, emotionally literate. We are suspicious of what seems excessive, overinvested, too immediate. Yet some of the most sustaining relationships in a life are not especially restrained. They are the ones in which a person has earned the right to overreact on your behalf, to fuss too much, to assume importance before being invited. *Sampat* names this embarrassment of involvement.

It is, in that sense, a word about entitlement. Not entitlement in the moralizing sense, but relational entitlement: who gets to take up space in your emotional life without ceremony. A stranger who behaves this way is intolerable. A merely casual friend would seem intrusive. But when a certain threshold has been crossed, the same behavior becomes not only acceptable but expected. The friend who does not overreact may begin to seem oddly absent. The chaos becomes part of the contract.

This is why *sampat* matters more than its rough equivalents. It captures the shift by which excess moves from flaw to intimacy. It allows a relationship to be simultaneously ridiculous and serious. And it hints at something more difficult to name: that there are bonds in adult life whose truth lies precisely in the fact that they are a little embarrassing to describe in polished terms. “We are very close” says almost nothing. “He is so *sampat*” may say much more.

The word also matters because it helps resist the tendency to over-theorize too soon. Before a relationship becomes a case study in queer attachment, diasporic recognition, or chosen kinship, it is often just lived as a series of interactions with a certain unmistakable tone. Too many messages. Too much interpretation. Too much fuss. Too much delight in each other’s overreaction. Slang catches this early, before analysis tidies it up. It preserves the vulgar intimacy of the thing.

And vulgar intimacy may be exactly what we need to pay more attention to. Not because every overdramatic friendship is profound, but because modern adulthood has become very good at recognizing importance only once it has acquired a respectable form. *Sampat* offers a small rebellion against that. It tells us that a relationship can be shapeless, excessive, faintly absurd, and still carry a great deal of emotional truth.

If the first section’s claim was that some of the most consequential bonds hide inside unserious language, the deeper claim here is that not all unserious language is interchangeable. Some words carry more social intelligence than others. *Sampat* does not merely tell us that someone is too much. It tells us what kind of too muchness is being tolerated, enjoyed, and perhaps quietly needed. That is why it is a better guide than “chaotic.” It is messier, sharper, more embarrassing, and closer to life.

# III. Friendship as a high-context language

One of the more misleading assumptions people make about intimacy is that it is primarily a matter of disclosure. We imagine closeness as something built through revelation: the confession, the vulnerable conversation, the clear statement of feeling. Sometimes it is. But many relationships, perhaps especially the most durable ones, are built less through explicit declaration than through a much subtler achievement: shared calibration. Two people become close not because they say the deepest things first, but because they come to hear the same words at the same pitch.

This is where sociolinguistics becomes unexpectedly useful. A friendship is not only a bond between personalities. It is also a small speech community.

Every speech community develops its own norms of proportion, timing, permissible exaggeration, irony, interruption, silence, and repair. The same sentence can function as insult, affection, panic, flirtation, boredom, or mere phatic noise depending on who says it, how often, in what sequence, and under what prior history. Intimacy, in this sense, is not simply emotional. It is pragmatic. It depends on whether two people know what a line is doing beyond what it literally says.

A “sampat” friendship is a particularly vivid example of this. Its texture lies not in content alone, but in how meaning is managed socially. Consider what such a bond often involves: exaggerated complaint, mock-annoyance, overinterpretation of trivial delays, melodramatic escalation, strategic nonsense, affectionate scolding, abrupt tonal switches from absurdity to seriousness and back again. None of this works unless both people share a fairly sophisticated sense of the rules. When is overreaction funny rather than burdensome? When is mockery tender rather than cruel? When is “What is wrong with you?” an expression of fond recognition rather than actual alarm? A friendship like this requires not less skill than sober emotional discourse, but more.

That is because it is high-context language. Much of its meaning depends on what is already known and therefore need not be said. A low-context exchange aims for clarity on the surface. A high-context exchange relies on shared history, assumed tone, prior episodes, mutually legible habits of escalation, and the ability to infer emotional truth from apparently unserious signals. In this kind of friendship, the literal sentence is often the least important part of the utterance. The real message lives in timing, rhythm, and scale.

Take the simple fact of reply speed. In ordinary communication, a delayed reply may mean almost nothing. In a high-context friendship, however, response time becomes part of the language itself. A three-hour silence may prompt theatrical outrage not because anyone truly believes a grave betrayal has occurred, but because the delay has entered an already established economy of attention. The ensuing overreaction is not just feeling; it is a ritual performance of relational priority. The complaint says, in effect: I am entitled to notice your absence. The other person, by recognizing the performance, confirms that entitlement. A tiny event becomes socially dense.

This is why it makes sense to say that such friendships are built from pragmatic fluency. The friends become skilled readers of implicature, stance, and indexicality. They know that “die lah,” “hello???,” “you are impossible,” “why are you like this,” or any number of ordinary-seeming phrases can operate as finely differentiated social acts. Depending on context, these lines can signal irritation, pleasure, invitation, performative jealousy, affection, disbelief, emotional checking-in, or the desire to prolong contact under cover of complaint. The sentence does not contain the meaning by itself. The relationship does.

The sociolinguistic term indexicality is especially useful here. Language does not only convey information; it also points to social identities, relationships, stances, and worlds. A single word can index class, ethnicity, city, generation, sexuality, intimacy, or emotional posture. *Sampat* is powerful partly because it is indexical in exactly this way. It does not only mean excessive. It points toward a Malaysian social world, toward a recognizable style of being “too much,” and toward a form of judgment that can easily slide into affection. When someone uses the term, he is not merely describing behavior. He is locating the interaction inside a shared cultural field.

This is particularly important in multilingual or code-switching environments, where linguistic choice is never neutral. For Malaysian Chinese speakers, English, Malay, Mandarin, Cantonese, Hokkien, and internet slang can all function not just as lexical resources but as social positioning devices. A friendship may operate mainly in English and yet be saturated with the pragmatic force of Malay particles, Chinese kinship textures, or local habits of exaggeration. The result is not simple multilingualism. It is layered indexical speech: language whose social meaning depends as much on what cultural register is being invoked as on the literal content of the words.

That layered speech matters intensely in diasporic settings. When a Malaysian Chinese gay man in Ireland uses *sampat* rather than “dramatic” or “chaotic,” he is doing more than preserving a colorful term from home. He is activating a whole pragmatic frame. He is summoning a social world in which excess is read in a certain way, where teasing travels at a certain speed, where overreaction can be shameless without becoming catastrophic, where absurdity can carry care. He is, in effect, deciding that this relationship will not be translated all the way into globalized, flattened English. Some of its emotional truth will remain locally encoded.

And that local encoding is part of what makes the friendship feel dense. High-context relationships reduce the need for explanation. They are efficient not because they are simple, but because they compress so much meaning into so little surface. A person who understands your family jokes, your tone shifts, your ironic panic, your strategic melodrama, and your preferred forms of mock-offense can grasp you with much less overt narration. In adulthood, that kind of fast reading can feel almost luxurious. One no longer needs to establish the whole setting each time. The smallest cue is enough.

This is where the usual opposition between “banter” and “real intimacy” begins to collapse. Banter is often treated as the avoidance of sincerity, especially in male friendship, as though joking were what people do instead of saying something true. But sociolinguistically, this is too crude. Banter is often one of the ways truth is organized. It allows speakers to test alignment, register degrees of claim, manage face, soften exposure, and maintain contact without requiring every emotional movement to appear in its most naked form. In a “sampat” friendship, banter is not a detour around intimacy. It is one of intimacy’s main grammars.

Indeed, it may be a particularly queer grammar, though not only queer. Many queer friendships rely on stylized speech, exaggerated reaction, tonal play, and camp-adjacent forms of overstatement as ways of sustaining closeness without constant declarative solemnity. But the more general sociolinguistic point comes first: friendships become deep when they develop a stable system for interpreting each other’s linguistic excess. The friends know which exaggerations are conventional, which are diagnostic, which are bids for attention, and which are real alarms hidden inside jokes. This is why someone outside the bond often misreads it. They hear chaos; the friends hear differentiation.

One might even say that a mature friendship is partly a matter of becoming mutually bilingual in each other’s overreactions. Not because the overreactions are fake, but because they are patterned. They are part of an interactional repertoire. The friendship acquires its own prosody, its own expected forms of escalation and softening, its own little rituals of complaint and reassurance. A message thread begins to resemble not random exchange but a highly compressed social drama in serial form.

Phatic communication is important here too. Much of what sustains closeness is not informationally rich. It is there to maintain the channel. Tiny bursts of commentary, redundant reactions, repeated jokes, apparently pointless checking-in: these do not advance a conversation so much as confirm a shared world. To an outsider, this can seem trivial. To the participants, it is often the infrastructure of attachment. “Sampat” behavior is frequently phatic in this sense. The point is not the issue ostensibly being discussed. The point is the maintenance of heightened relational presence.

Seen this way, the phrase “sampat friends” names not only a type of personality but a type of interaction order. It points to a friendship in which social excess has been normalized into a communicative mode. Proportion is deliberately distorted; attention is dramatized; annoyance is converted into attachment-signaling; emotional density is carried by tone rather than exposition. The friendship becomes, in effect, a language whose most important features are learned only from the inside.

And this may be why such relationships are so difficult to describe once one steps outside them. Their truth does not reside in a single dramatic statement. It resides in dozens of tiny pragmatic accomplishments: the right kind of overreaction, the precisely calibrated insult, the knowing misuse of seriousness, the speed with which someone hears panic beneath the joke or affection beneath the complaint. All of this looks, from the outside, like people merely talking too much in the wrong tone. From the inside, it is a highly developed social intelligence.

To say that friendship is a high-context language, then, is not metaphor alone. It is a fairly literal description of how certain bonds work. Two people become close by constructing a small world in which words carry more than they would elsewhere, and in which apparently excessive styles of speech become reliable vehicles of care. A “sampat” friendship simply makes this process easier to see, because the excess is so visible. It takes what many intimate relationships do quietly and performs it at volume.

That volume, for all its absurdity, is often a form of precision.

# IV. Camp and *sampat*: cousins, not twins

At a certain point, any serious discussion of a friendship like this begins to drift toward camp. The drift is understandable. The overreaction, the stylization, the theatrical complaint, the pleasure in saying too much in the wrong tone at the right time—these are all recognizably camp-adjacent features. If two queer men become intensely attached through a shared language of exaggerated annoyance and melodramatic care, it is hard not to hear camp somewhere in the background, clearing its throat. But the temptation to collapse *sampat* into camp should be resisted. The two overlap. They are not the same thing.

Camp is a more theorized term, and a more portable one. It arrives already trailing a history of queer interpretation: stylization, theatricality, irony, aesthetic excess, seriousness performed through unseriousness, feeling routed through artifice rather than direct declaration. Camp notices when something is too much, but it notices in a very specific way. It is interested not only in excess, but in the form excess takes—its performance, its self-consciousness, its elegance, its irony, its refusal to be merely sincere in a straightforward register. Camp turns emotion into style.

*Sampat*, by contrast, is a more ordinary social word. It belongs less to aesthetics than to judgment, less to theory than to everyday speech. It is what one says when a person has become a lot. Too dramatic, too invested, too noisy, too overinvolved, too shamelessly reactive to be left undescribed. Its force is more colloquial, more practical, more interactional. Where camp asks us to notice stylization, *sampat* asks us to notice social disproportion.

This distinction matters because modern readers, especially English-speaking ones, are often tempted to convert every interesting form of queer excess into camp. The temptation is flattering but flattening. It turns local emotional textures into globally recognizable theory objects. One gains a neat conceptual vocabulary, but one risks losing the grain of the thing itself. *Sampat* carries more embarrassment than camp, more social inconvenience, more of the sense that other people are now being dragged into your emotional weather. Camp can be exquisitely poised in its excess. *Sampat* is often less graceful and more alive.

Put differently, camp is often about how something is performed; *sampat* is often about how something lands. Camp can be arch, polished, knowingly theatrical. *Sampat* can be inelegant, loud, and just a little bit socially unruly. A person in a fully camp mode may seem highly composed inside the performance. A person being *sampat* may seem only partly in control of the escalation. The effect may still be funny, but the humor is not necessarily grounded in aesthetic self-command. It is grounded in an almost civic failure of proportion.

And yet the two clearly meet. They meet especially in queer friendship, where style and social excess are often intertwined. Two gay men do not need to stage a formal performance to produce a camp-inflected atmosphere. A line reading is enough. A dramatic “hello???” after a delayed reply. A mock-furious monologue over something objectively trivial. A complaint delivered with so much flourish that it becomes a scene. This is not camp in the grand museum sense. It is camp reduced to the scale of daily interaction, living inside friendship rather than onstage. What *sampat* contributes is the ordinary social permission for such scenes to proliferate without needing to announce themselves as aesthetic objects.

That permission is what makes *sampat* so useful. Camp, because it is so closely tied to stylization, can sometimes sound too refined for the messier realities of adult attachment. It can overemphasize polish, wit, distance, control. But many important friendships are not especially controlled. Their vitality depends on overreach, fuss, too many messages, too much interpretation, too much reaction. They are not elegant all the time. They are simply alive at a higher intensity than social norms usually recommend. *Sampat* preserves that quality. It makes room for care to be socially excessive rather than aesthetically perfected.

This is especially important if one wants to understand queer intimacy without forcing it into a choice between dignity and flamboyance. Camp has often been read, rightly, as a queer method of survival: a way of transmuting stigma, pain, and constraint into style, irony, and wit. It allows people to inhabit seriousness without becoming trapped in solemnity. But queer life is not only made through style. It is also made through local habits of speech, family-inflected forms of mockery, community-specific registers of overreaction, and the mundane social ways people become ridiculous in front of those they trust. *Sampat* points toward this second archive.

It is an archive of vernacular excess.

That phrase matters because camp, in much of its best-known history, emerges from queer worlds already attentive to performance, spectacle, citation, and coded reading. It is often exquisitely sensitive to surfaces. *Sampat* is less citational and more domestic. It can happen in the kitchen, in the group chat, in the voice note, in the running complaint over small things that somehow become emotionally charged. It does not need sequins, divas, or classic melodrama to exist, though it can coexist with all of those. It needs only a social setting in which exaggeration is legible and tolerated. In that sense, *Sampat* is camp’s less polished cousin: more colloquial, more embarrassing, less interested in aesthetic coherence, and perhaps therefore closer to the rhythms of ordinary intimacy.

The difference can be stated more sharply. Camp transforms feeling by stylizing it. *Sampat* transforms feeling by socializing it. Camp says: let us make this theatrical enough to bear. *Sampat* says: let us make this noisy enough that you now have to deal with me. The first often works through form. The second often works through volume, interruption, and overinvolvement. One may well contain the other. But the emphasis shifts.

This shift becomes especially important in adult friendship, because so much of adult life depends on emotional management. People become polished. Their language tidies itself. Their relationships are expected to remain bounded, literate, and proportionate. Camp can interrupt that polish by reintroducing wit and stylized excess. *Sampat* interrupts it more rudely. It says, in effect, that I am not only going to feel this; I am going to make the feeling socially inconvenient in a way that confirms you matter. If camp is partly about the intelligence of performance, *sampat* is partly about the intelligence of overreach.

The distinction also clarifies why the word *sampat* is so useful in a Malaysian context. Camp is transnational. It travels. It can be recognized across queer worlds precisely because it has already been abstracted into a broader aesthetic sensibility. *Sampat* does not travel as cleanly. It retains a local friction. It sounds a little harder to polish into theory, and that is part of its value. It keeps the relationship tied to a specific linguistic and social world. It prevents the analysis from floating too far above the lived texture of speech.

In the friendship between a Malaysian Chinese gay oncologist in Ireland and a Malaysian Chinese gay psychiatrist in Malaysia, that localness matters. Camp may connect them to a wider queer language of excess, irony, and stylized feeling. *Sampat* keeps the bond anchored in a more specific world of recognition: Malaysian humor, Malaysian proportion, Malaysian social messiness, Malaysian ways of converting annoyance into care. Camp gives the friendship a queer form of legibility. *Sampat* gives it a vernacular home.

What emerges, then, is not a rivalry between the two terms but a division of labor. Camp explains how sincerity can survive by becoming theatrical. *Sampat* explains how attachment can survive by becoming excessive. Camp refines; *sampat* thickens. Camp gives a friendship shape in the language of queer sensibility; *sampat* gives it texture in the language of everyday social life. If they feel like cousins, it is because both understand that being too much is sometimes the most honest possible register. They differ in what they believe “too much” is for.

The attraction of camp, especially in writing, is that it can make mess look intelligent. The attraction of *sampat* is that it keeps intelligence from becoming too tidy. It reminds us that some of the most important bonds in a life are not beautifully stylized from the start. They are awkward, repetitive, overreactive, mildly embarrassing, and socially loud. They may eventually become legible to theory, but first they are simply lived.

This is one reason queer adulthood so often resists the vocabulary offered to it. Not because queer people are uniquely mysterious, but because the forms of attachment that sustain them have often had to develop outside officially dignified scripts. Camp has been one way of making that fact beautiful. *Sampat* is a way of making it ordinary. The first gives us a sensibility. The second gives us a habit of life.

And habits of life are usually where intimacy hides.

So yes, camp is in the room. But *Sampat* is doing different work. It names the relationship not at the level of aesthetic interpretation, but at the level where people actually inconvenience each other with care. That difference is small enough to miss and large enough to matter. It is the difference between recognizing a style and recognizing a bond.

# V. The strange seriousness of adult queer friendship

Modern life remains embarrassingly conservative about where seriousness is supposed to live. It knows how to recognize gravity when it appears in romance, marriage, domestic partnership, caregiving, co-parenting, or bereavement. It knows, in other words, how to honor attachment once that attachment has been routed through the couple form. Friendship, by contrast, is still treated as emotionally important but structurally optional: lovely to have, sad to lose, not quite the thing on which a life is meant to hinge. This is not a neutral oversight. It is one of the ways adulthood keeps disciplining intimacy into acceptable shapes.

Queer life has always exposed the poverty of this arrangement.

Not because queer people are uniquely good at friendship in some sentimental sense, but because queer worlds have long had to build intimacy with fewer guarantees that the state, the family, or public language would ratify the most important bond in the room. The result is not simply “more friendship.” It is a much stranger and more interesting social field, one in which friendship can absorb functions that normative culture prefers to reserve for lovers, spouses, and kin. Witnessing. Priority. Emotional first resort. Shared archive. Ritual dependence. Sustained interpretive attention. The point is not that friendship replaces romance. It is that friendship can become central in ways romance does not fully monopolize.

This matters because the phrase “deep friendship” is often too gentle for what is actually at stake. Some adult queer friendships are not merely deep. They are structurally decisive. They organize mood, attention, memory, and daily meaning. They may involve no formal declarations, no shared home, no public ritual of confirmation, and yet they exert a degree of force that would be immediately legible if redistributed into a romantic narrative. One of the stranger accomplishments of modern relational culture is that it can watch two people become indispensable to each other and still hesitate to grant the bond full seriousness unless sex, cohabitation, or official naming eventually appears to tidy things up.

A “sampat” friendship makes this problem unusually visible because it looks unserious while doing serious work. The overreaction, the fuss, the melodrama, the ridiculous amount of emotional traffic—these can all be dismissed as comic texture. But comic texture often functions as camouflage. It lets a relationship accumulate density without immediately becoming available for audit under the usual categories. The bond can become central before anyone has agreed on what to call it.

Queer theory is useful here precisely because it has long been suspicious of the idea that relational truth must culminate in stable naming. Not every important attachment wants to become a couple. Not every central bond is heading toward romance. Not every form of intimacy is clarified by being pinned to a familiar endpoint. Sometimes naming does not reveal the relationship. Sometimes it domesticates it.

This is why the distinction between friendship and romance, though socially convenient, is often analytically lazy. It assumes that friendship is one thing, romance another, and that emotionally intense bonds must belong properly to one side or the other. But queer life repeatedly produces relations that are less obedient than that. There are friendships thick with erotic surplus but resistant to romance. There are quasi-sibling bonds with flirtatious edges. There are emotionally pair-like attachments that never become domestic. There are chosen kinships more binding than many marriages. And there are friendships whose force lies precisely in their refusal to submit to the demand for final categorization.

The culture’s response to such bonds is revealing. It often reaches first for diminishment. “They’re just very close.” “It’s basically platonic.” “It’s like sibling energy.” These are not always wrong. But they often function less as descriptions than as containment devices. They restore comfort by reassigning intensity to a category the culture already knows how to tolerate. Friendship, once domesticated by the phrase “just very close,” can remain emotionally vivid without becoming conceptually threatening.

But some friendships are threatening—at least to the tidy hierarchy that puts couplehood at the center of adult significance. They threaten because they redistribute weight. They suggest that the person who knows you best, unsettles you most, monitors you most closely, and interrupts your solitude most effectively may not be your spouse, your lover, or anyone you could easily introduce at dinner under a sanctioned label. He may simply be the one who has become linguistically, emotionally, and temporally indispensable.

That is where queer friendship becomes most interesting: when it stops looking like support and starts looking like form.

Form matters. The question is not only how two people feel, but what shape their attachment takes in the world. Do they become each other’s first call? Do they monitor each other’s moods with unusual precision? Does reply-time acquire moral texture? Do ordinary disruptions trigger disproportionate emotional weather? Do they build a running archive of jokes, references, grievances, and tiny observations unintelligible to outsiders? Does one person’s reaction begin to matter too much? These are not minor details. They are the infrastructure of centrality.

And centrality is where friendship begins to trouble the neat opposition between platonic and romantic. One does not need overt sex or explicit romantic aspiration for a bond to become pair-like in structure. What matters is not whether the two people cross some symbolic line, but whether the relationship begins to perform functions that dominant culture assumes will eventually be monopolized by the couple. If so, the question is no longer “are they secretly in love?” That is the wrong question, and usually a dull one. The more interesting question is what kinds of life become thinkable once intimacy is allowed to remain central without becoming romantically regularized.

This is where anti-romance enters.

Anti-romance is not the absence of feeling. It is not fear of intimacy dressed up as sophistication. At its best, anti-romance is a refusal to let the couple form dictate the only legitimate shape of seriousness. It asks why every bond with enough intensity, tenderness, exclusivity, or interpretive depth must be pressured toward a familiar narrative arc. Meet, disclose, define, escalate, stabilize. Anti-romance resists this sequence not because it denies attachment, but because it suspects the sequence of narrowing what attachment can be.

For queer adults, this suspicion is especially reasonable. The history of queer life is not just a history of exclusion from normative institutions; it is also a history of improvising relational forms outside them. Chosen kinship, strategic ambiguity, affective households, enduring ex-intimacies, friendships that outlast romance and absorb its discarded functions—all of these belong to a queer archive that mainstream adulthood still struggles to read without either romanticizing or trivializing it. Anti-romance, in this sense, is not a mood. It is an analytic refusal of compulsory relational legibility.

A “sampat” friendship can easily become one of anti-romance’s most telling forms. It allows two people to be emotionally overinvested, mildly possessive, theatrically attentive, and structurally central to each other without immediately collapsing the bond into a courtship narrative. The comic surface is part of what makes this possible. Excess can travel under the cover of humor. Jealousy can be performed as annoyance. Need can appear as fuss. Tenderness can be routed through mockery. The relationship remains livable because it is neither wholly disavowed nor entirely declared.

This is not merely evasion. It is also technique.

Strategic ambiguity has a bad reputation among people who prefer relational clarity at all costs. Sometimes rightly so. Ambiguity can hide cowardice, asymmetry, or the indefinite deferral of responsibility. But ambiguity can also be a way of keeping a bond open, breathable, and resistant to premature reduction. Not every relation is improved by being forced to answer the question “What are we?” Sometimes that question is less a search for truth than a demand for administrative compliance.

Queer friendship has long depended on this more generous ambiguity. A bond may contain affection, identification, desire, protectiveness, rivalry, camp performance, and kin-like loyalty all at once. To insist that it become either “only friendship” or “really romance” is often to misunderstand the very density one is trying to interpret. The bond may be powerful not because it is secretly one thing, but because it remains dynamically several things.

That plurality is part of its seriousness.

The culture tends to imagine seriousness as a function of consolidation. A serious relationship is one that has chosen its lane. But queer relational life often suggests the opposite: that seriousness can reside in a sustained refusal to let one lane dominate. The adult queer friend who is also your witness, your favorite nuisance, your emergency contact in spirit if not in law, your homeland echo, your stylistic accomplice, your emotional irritant, your archive, your near-constant referent—this figure is serious precisely because no single category can hold him without loss.

And this is where the “sampat” part matters again. The word prevents theory from becoming too elegant. It reminds us that all this high-minded talk about relational plurality often cashes out, in daily life, as something much less dignified: too many messages, too much reading into tone, too much reaction to silence, too much delight in being irritatingly important to each other. The social form may be theoretically rich. The behavior is still, quite often, a little embarrassing.

Good. It should be.

Embarrassment is one of the signs that a bond has escaped ready-made language. The polished categories of adulthood are supposed to spare us that feeling. They tell us which loves are respectable, which dependencies are mature, which intensities can be narrated cleanly. Queer friendship has always been one of the places where that smoothness breaks down. It asks us to admit that people can become central to each other in ways that are neither minor nor properly containable. It asks us to recognize that intimacy does not always want the destiny offered to it by romance. Sometimes it wants recurrence, attention, overreading, mutual derailment, and a durable right to be ridiculous in each other’s lives.

A culture organized around the couple is bound to find such bonds confusing. That confusion is not evidence that the bonds are immature. It is evidence that the culture’s map is incomplete.

So the strange seriousness of adult queer friendship lies here: it can do nearly everything our emotional culture reserves for romance while refusing the story that romance is therefore what it must secretly be. It can be central without being canonical. It can be binding without being formalized. It can contain desire without being organized by the promise of a future together. And it can look, from the outside, like two people simply being too much.

Sometimes that is exactly what they are.

Sometimes “too much” is the form seriousness takes once it has slipped the categories designed to tame it.

# VI. Mid-life loneliness in a successful life

There is a particular kind of loneliness that arrives not with failure, but with competence.

It does not look like the loneliness people are trained to notice. It is not dramatic isolation, not social abandonment, not the conspicuous ache of having no one to call. It can coexist with a full calendar, a respected career, a long contact list, a decent social life, and the outward signs of being firmly established in adulthood. If anything, it is made easier to miss by precisely those things. The life appears functional. The person appears integrated. From the outside, everything seems to have worked.

And yet one of the quieter effects of professional success is that it can make a person increasingly legible in all the wrong ways.

The more accomplished someone becomes, the more likely he is to be encountered through function. He is known as the competent one, the reliable one, the clinician, the consultant, the specialist, the person who gets things done, the one who can absorb pressure without spectacle. His time becomes partitioned. His language becomes cleaner. His social presentation becomes more economical. Even his distress, when it appears, is often translated into administratively acceptable forms: tired, busy, stretched, under a lot on at the moment. The successful adult becomes a highly edited version of himself, and the editing is often mistaken for stability.

This is one reason adulthood can feel so curiously thin just when it appears most solid. There is less room for mess that has no project attached to it. Less room for emotional excess that cannot be redeemed as insight. Less room for relational irrationality, for repetitive nonsense, for the kind of need that does not improve anyone’s character and cannot be filed under growth. One becomes, in effect, increasingly efficient at being known superficially and decreasingly likely to be encountered in one’s unproductive forms.

A “sampat” friend matters here because he violates that efficiency.

He is not impressed by competence in the correct way. He does not allow success to become a complete style of personhood. He continues to address the parts of the self that professional life has no use for: the melodramatic, the repetitive, the petulant, the socially excessive, the absurdly overreactive, the parts that do not scale well into adult respectability. He permits a person to be not merely vulnerable—which has itself become a suspiciously polished modern virtue—but inconveniently alive.

This is what makes mid-life loneliness so difficult to describe. It is not simply the absence of company. It is the gradual reduction of opportunities to be encountered outside one’s managed role. A successful life can become crowded with recognition and starved of legibility. People admire you, need you, trust you, defer to you, consult you, perhaps even envy you. But admiration is not the same as being read quickly. Need is not the same as being known in your worst tempo. Trust is not the same as being allowed to be ridiculous without loss of status. One begins to miss not people in the abstract, but certain conditions of being seen.

This is especially true in mid-life, when the glamorous fiction of adulthood begins to wear thin. Youth is often imagined as the time of excessive feeling, unstable identity, experimental attachment. Later adulthood is supposed to bring consolidation: a steadier self, a smaller but more meaningful social circle, a life less prone to emotional melodrama. There is truth in this. But there is also a cost. Consolidation often means that fewer and fewer relationships remain available for irrational intensity. One becomes better organized and, not infrequently, lonelier in ways good organization cannot solve.

The loneliness of mid-life is often a loneliness of asymmetry. Outward life moves toward coherence; inward life remains untidy. Public identity sharpens; private need becomes harder to place. The world increasingly encounters a person as finished while he continues to experience himself as partial, repetitive, occasionally absurd, and in need of social forms that do not flatter maturity too much. A friendship built around *sampat* becomes valuable because it allows adulthood to remain unmastered.

This is one place where the culture’s relentless praise of success becomes intellectually lazy. Success is treated as though it were a general solvent for relational difficulty. Find your place, become good at something, acquire mobility, cultivate discernment, build a stable life: once these things are in place, the rest is presumed to follow. But success often reorganizes rather than resolves loneliness. It reduces some forms of precarity while intensifying others. It may bring status and travel and authority and a refined sense of one’s own capacities. It may also leave a person increasingly surrounded by people who know how to interact with him as a role.

And roles are terrible substitutes for intimacy.

For the kind of men under discussion here—diasporic, professionally accomplished, queer, emotionally overmanaged—the problem may be sharper still. The successful gay professional is one of modernity’s favorite reassuring figures. He is evidence that things can be made to work. He is articulate, mobile, disciplined, and, if all goes well, just transgressive enough to seem interesting without becoming socially destabilizing. But that polished figure can conceal how exhausting it is to remain perpetually interpretable. To be successful is, among other things, to become easier for institutions to read. The question is what kinds of friendship remain available for the parts institutions do not read at all.

A “sampat” friendship can become one answer. Not the only answer, and not always a healthy one, but a revealing one. It offers a zone in which proportion is relaxed. A person can return from his day of being sensible and become briefly ridiculous again. He can overread a delayed message, spiral theatrically, send a long complaint about something trivial, become childish, overfamiliar, embarrassingly invested. None of this is morally noble. That is partly why it matters. It is not self-improvement. It is relief.

Relief, at mid-life, is often more intimate than insight.

This may be why certain adult friendships become disproportionately important precisely when they would appear most unnecessary. The person in his twenties, unsure of everything, is expected to need others intensely. The person in mid-life, settled and competent, is expected to have learned moderation. But moderation is not the only adult achievement. So is knowing where one can afford to be immoderate. A life organized entirely around proportion becomes emotionally sterile. The friend who can draw excess back out of you is not sabotaging your maturity. He may be preserving it from hardening into mere management.

There is also a temporal dimension to this. Mid-life does not only bring success; it brings repetition. The same meetings, the same clinic days, the same forms, the same explanations, the same small negotiations of time and energy. Even a meaningful career can acquire a repetitive frame. Under such conditions, a friendship marked by exaggerated reaction and recurring nonsense becomes a way of interrupting the flattening effects of routine. The friend does not merely offer companionship. He restores variation. He destabilizes the deadening efficiency of an over-managed week.

This is one reason the bond can feel more important than it appears. From the outside, what one sees are fragments: excessive texts, overreactions, mock arguments, ongoing bits. From the inside, what those fragments do is more serious. They reintroduce unpredictability into a life organized by obligation. They keep mood from becoming too private. They make recurrence feel inhabited rather than merely endured. In a successful life, this can matter more than dramatic declarations ever do.

The phrase “mid-life loneliness” can sound sentimental, as though one were talking about a soft-focus sadness brought on by aging and hindsight. That is not quite the point. The sharper point is that adult life produces forms of recognition that are highly functional and often emotionally thin. The very achievements that make a life admirable can also make it harder to encounter the self outside its useful arrangements. Under those conditions, a friendship that looks unserious may become one of the few places where seriousness is actually felt.

The “sampat” friend is important, then, not because he solves loneliness, but because he changes its texture. He converts isolation from a private atmosphere into a shared event. He notices. He intrudes. He reacts too much. He refuses the clean surface of your successful life. He reminds you that being known does not always arrive as respect. Sometimes it arrives as disproportionate fuss.

There is, of course, a danger in romanticizing this. Not every mid-life attachment with a lot of emotional traffic is profound. Some are simply habits. Some are evasions. Some are ways of avoiding more difficult questions about partnership, dependency, or unmet need. But even that risk is instructive. It suggests how starved adult life can become for forms of closeness that are neither formalized nor trivial. When such a bond appears, it is often asked to do too much because so little else is structurally available to do the same work.

This is what the culture misses when it treats friendship as the decorative fringe of adulthood. Friendship is not always supplementary. Sometimes it is the place where adulthood is prevented from collapsing into role-play. Sometimes it is the site where success is punctured, where competence stops being the dominant aesthetic of the self, where the person who looks most composed from the outside can become, for a while, gloriously excessive.

A “sampat” friendship is one way that happens.

It may not look like maturity. It may look, in fact, like its opposite: regression, overreaction, too much emotional weather over too little factual ground. But perhaps maturity has been described too narrowly. Perhaps one of the marks of an adult life well-lived is not merely restraint, but the possession of a few relationships in which restraint is no longer mandatory. A successful life without such relations can become extremely well-run and spiritually underfurnished.

Which is another way of saying: competence is not intimacy, and admiration is not relief.

The friend who gives you relief may be the one who is just a little too much.

# VII. Medicine as one example of emotional overmanagement

Medicine belongs in this story, but it should not dominate it.

There is a temptation, whenever doctors appear in an essay about intimacy, to make their emotional lives seem unusually noble, as though proximity to suffering automatically confers depth. That temptation is usually a mistake. Doctors are not moral symbols. They are workers in institutions, trained into highly specific habits of speech, posture, and feeling. If oncology in Ireland and psychiatry in Malaysia matter here, it is not because these professions make friendship more profound than other professions do. It is because medicine offers a particularly sharp view of a much wider modern problem: the management of feeling under conditions of relentless usefulness.

To practice medicine is, among other things, to be trained in proportion.

One learns how not to overreact in front of fear. How not to become too visibly entangled in grief. How to speak with clarity under uncertainty. How to deliver gravity without melodrama, seriousness without collapse, compassion without too much emotional spill. The content differs between specialties, but the tonal demand is familiar. A person must remain readable when the situation itself is not.

This is especially true in fields like oncology and psychiatry, though in different ways. Oncology asks clinicians to live in close proximity to mortality, risk, treatment burden, recurrence, and the repetitive labor of managing hope without fantasy. Psychiatry asks for a different discipline: the ability to remain coherent in the face of psychic incoherence, to listen without being swallowed, to absorb intensity without either overidentifying or retreating into sterile distance. The two specialties do not mirror each other neatly, but both require a great deal of affective editing. One cannot simply feel at full volume in either setting and continue to function.

That is why a friendship built around *sampat* acquires such force. It reverses the tonal discipline of work.

In the clinic, proportion must be maintained. In the friendship, proportion can be abandoned. The oncologist and psychiatrist can become excessive, melodramatic, repetitive, mildly irrational, socially loud. They can overread a delayed reply, turn a small inconvenience into a scene, or perform exasperation at a scale that would be absurd anywhere else. From the outside, this may look unserious. From the inside, it is one of the few zones in which emotional life is not required to arrive already professionally tidied.

This is where the language of burnout sometimes fails. Burnout is a useful term up to a point, but it often flattens everything into exhaustion. It captures depletion and overwork, but not always the forms people invent in order to remain intact under those conditions. The more interesting question is not only why clinicians become tired, but how they continue to feel without being destroyed by the requirement to feel correctly. Formal support structures can help. Reflection groups can help. Time off can help. But many people survive, more quietly, through informal relational forms that do not look therapeutic at all. They survive through banter, camp, overreaction, nonsense, and the right to be ridiculous in front of someone who already understands the genre.

That last phrase matters: understands the genre.

A friend who is not simply sympathetic but genre-literate can hear the difference between an ordinary complaint, a stylized performance of annoyance, a request for attention, and real distress hidden under exaggeration. That is one reason close friendship often succeeds where more official forms of support feel thin. It does not require the speaker to convert feeling into the language of insight. It does not require a clean account of what is wrong. It knows that sometimes the most accurate form of communication is not a direct statement but an overblown one.

Camp helps here by making burden theatrical enough to carry. A bureaucratic absurdity becomes a monologue. A difficult week becomes a running bit. Emotional pressure is given style before it can become mere deadness. *Sampat* does something slightly different. It does not refine distress so much as socialize it. It lets feeling overflow into the bond through fuss, interruption, mock outrage, too many messages, too much commentary. If camp makes suffering narratable, *sampat* makes it shared.

This distinction is useful because medicine rewards controlled narration. Clinicians are expected to be reflective, yes, but in measured ways. They may describe strain, but usually in a register still compatible with competence. They may acknowledge emotional burden, but preferably not so messily that it disrupts the role. The result is a culture in which distress is often allowed only once it has been rendered legible in administratively tolerable prose. One is overwhelmed, but articulate about it. One is exhausted, but still composed. One is hurting, but in the correct tone.

A “sampat” friendship interrupts that arrangement. It permits suffering to become emotionally inefficient. No lesson is required. No self-improvement arc must emerge. The feeling does not have to be useful. It can simply be excessive, badly proportioned, repetitive, and temporarily impossible to contain. This is not always beautiful. That is part of its value. It is one of the few places where the successful adult professional does not need to curate himself for institutional consumption.

Medical humanities, at its best, has always been interested in this remainder: what medical life cannot fully absorb into professionalism, evidence, ethics, or official reflective practice. It asks what happens to clinicians as narrating, suffering, culturally located persons. It asks what forms of self become available or impossible under professional pressure. And it suggests, often quietly, that the emotional life of medicine is sustained not only by formal virtues like empathy and resilience, but by less respectable things: gossip, humor, complaint, stylized overstatement, intimate witness, and private modes of nonsense.

This is particularly relevant for queer clinicians, who are often already practiced in tonal calibration. To be a gay man in professional medicine is often to learn how to remain legible without becoming too legible. To be witty but not unserious, warm but not overfamiliar, distinct without becoming risky, socially fluent without disclosing too much. A friend who shares one’s cultural and queer grammar can puncture that careful calibration with unusual speed. He hears when the polished account is too polished. He recognizes the consultant voice arriving where the real self has not yet shown up. He can drag feeling back out into a more embarrassing, and therefore often more honest, register.

The embarrassment matters. Modern professional life has become oddly committed to the fantasy that good adulthood means seamless regulation: proper boundaries, good processing, tactful disclosure, no excessive dependence, no inappropriate intensity. Some of this is necessary. Some of it is simply loneliness with better branding. A “sampat” friend restores the possibility that adulthood may still need forms of social excess that are unflattering, noisy, and alive.

To say this is not to romanticize dysregulation. A friendship cannot bear infinite emotional dumping without consequence. Nor should nonsense be mistaken for care simply because it is vivid. The point is narrower and sharper. Under conditions of emotional overmanagement, people often need one or two places where they do not have to sound well-managed. Medicine makes this need especially visible because it trains people so rigorously into composure. The friend who reacts too much, reads too much into tone, fusses too loudly, and refuses to let one remain entirely polished may be doing a kind of work no official system can quite authorize.

He returns scale to feeling.

That may be the most important thing. Clinical life demands constant scaling: what matters now, what can wait, what must be named, what must be held back, what level of alarm is appropriate. A close friendship built around *sampat* allows a person to overscale the minor and underscale the decorous. It lets small things become large simply because one is tired, or lonely, or needing witness, or not wanting to remain proportionate for one more hour. This is not irrational so much as compensatory. The overreaction restores a range of emotional motion that professional life keeps narrowing.

Medicine, then, is one example of emotional overmanagement because it makes legible a broader truth about adult life. The highly functional self is not the whole self. The calm voice at work is not the only truthful voice. The culture of competence is not neutral; it edits personality toward usefulness. A friendship shaped by *sampat* does not abolish that editing, but it offers temporary amnesty from it.

Not healing, exactly. Not transcendence.

Just a place where the person trained all day to hold the line can stop pretending that the line is his natural shape.

# VIII. Diaspora, language, and the hunger to be read quickly

Diaspora is often described in the language of place. One leaves, arrives, settles, returns, belongs, does not belong, becomes divided between here and there. All of that is true, as far as it goes. But diaspora is also a linguistic condition. It changes not only where a person lives, but how much of himself must be translated before he can be properly understood.

This is one reason migration can produce such a peculiar kind of fatigue. A person may be entirely fluent in the language of his new life and still experience himself as living in partial translation. He can explain himself, certainly. He can tell the story. He can provide context, supply the right references, adjust his humor, flatten certain tones, sharpen others, convert one set of emotional assumptions into another. Over time, he may become extremely good at this. In fact, the better he gets, the easier it is for others to forget that any translation is happening at all.

But fluency is not the same as being read quickly.

To be read quickly is a different luxury. It means that one does not have to gloss one’s proportions before speaking. The joke lands at the intended pitch. The mockery arrives pre-understood as affection. The overreaction is heard in the right key. The word chosen carries not only semantic meaning but social atmosphere, and that atmosphere is received without explanation. Diaspora sharpens the value of such speed because so much of adult life, especially successful migrant life, proceeds by careful self-rendering.

This is where the linguistic dimension of a “sampat” friendship becomes especially clear. The Malaysian Chinese gay oncologist in Ireland may live most of his public life in English, and live it very well. He may be articulate, polished, professionally agile, perfectly capable of expressing himself in ways that colleagues, patients, and local friends can understand. But certain words do more than communicate. They reactivate a social world. *Sampat* is one of those words.

To choose *sampat* over “dramatic,” “chaotic,” or “extra” is not merely to prefer a more colorful term. It is to refuse total translation. It is to decide that the emotional truth of the relationship will remain anchored in a Malaysian register of judgment and affection. The word brings with it a whole pragmatic environment: the right degree of eye-roll, the right amount of indulgence, the faintly comic accusation, the sense that someone has become socially too much in a way that is annoying, funny, and somehow dear. That density does not survive cleanly in English equivalents. English can paraphrase. It cannot quite reproduce the social weather.

This is the quieter politics of diaspora. Publicly, migration is often narrated as adaptation, success, mobility, and broadened possibility. Linguistically, however, it can mean the steady outsourcing of nuance. A person keeps the grammar of his old life inside him while learning to present himself through the grammar of a new one. He is not lying, exactly. But he is constantly deciding which version of himself can be efficiently understood in a given setting. Some jokes travel; others do not. Some irritations can be voiced directly; others require too much cultural preparation to be worth the effort. Some forms of affection sound natural in one register and faintly odd in another.

This is why certain diasporic friendships feel less like conversation than like relief.

The relief does not come only from shared background in the abstract. Shared background is an overused phrase, often far too soft for what it is trying to describe. What matters more precisely is shared pragmatic instinct. Two people may not have the same exact biography and still know, almost instantly, how much irony is attached to a phrase, how much mockery can be tolerated before it becomes cruel, how family expectations haunt certain jokes, how complaint can function as intimacy rather than negativity, how saying too much and saying nothing can be adjacent styles rather than opposites.

For Malaysian Chinese speakers in particular, this kind of understanding is rarely confined to a single language. Even when a friendship is conducted mainly in English, it may still be textured by Malay particles, Chinese familial sensibilities, Cantonese or Hokkien rhythms, and the peculiar compressed expressiveness of urban multilingual life. The result is not just bilingualism or trilingualism. It is a kind of pragmatic layering in which the emotional force of an utterance depends on several linguistic worlds at once.

That layering is easy to underestimate if one thinks of language only in lexical terms. A person does not need to switch visibly into Malay or Cantonese for those systems to remain active. They can persist as timing, stance, cadence, implied posture. A sentence can be entirely English on the page and still feel unmistakably Malaysian in its aggression, softness, comic timing, or shamelessness. Diaspora often leaves this submerged. One sounds internationally competent while privately remaining organized by other tonal worlds.

A close friend from “home,” or at least from the same linguistic ecology, hears those submerged worlds immediately.

He hears not just what is said, but how much local life is packed into the saying. He knows when an English phrase is carrying Malay annoyance, Chinese familial melodrama, queer camp exaggeration, or some hybrid of all three. He does not require the speaker to flatten his tone into something globally legible. This is why such a friendship can feel like being returned to oneself without actually going anywhere. The geography matters, of course, but what is being restored is not simply homeland sentiment. It is homeland intelligibility.

That intelligibility is especially precious in queer life, where so much depends on calibration. Queer people often become experts in audience design: how to sound one way in one room and another way in another, how to signal without overexposing, how to let irony carry what sincerity might render too risky, how to remain visible only to the right readers. Add migration to that, and language becomes even more heavily managed. One is no longer only deciding what queerness sounds like; one is deciding what one’s whole self sounds like across multiple interpretive communities.

A “sampat” friendship pushes back against that management. It offers a space where one can be linguistically inefficient again. One can overdo it. One can use the wrong word in the right way. One can speak in a register that would be excessive, opaque, or faintly embarrassing elsewhere. This does not mean the friendship rejects cosmopolitan fluency. Quite the opposite. The bond often exists precisely because both people are highly fluent. But within that fluency, they reserve a protected zone of non-translation.

That zone matters because translation has costs, even when it succeeds. Every act of rendering strips out some density. One chooses an equivalent, smooths a tone, simplifies a social index, replaces a local judgment with a more internationally portable one. Over time, a person can begin to feel not exactly misunderstood, but under-read. Others understand enough, but not at the speed or thickness with which one knows oneself. The diasporic friend who shares one’s speech ecology repairs this. He does not necessarily know everything. He simply knows much more much faster.

And speed, in adulthood, is no small thing.

Adult lives are busy, partitioned, overcommitted. The relationships that survive are often the ones that minimize interpretive labor. This is one reason diasporic friendships shaped by shared language can become so disproportionately important. They save time, yes, but more importantly they save selfhood. They reduce the need to curate. They allow a person to show up at an emotional angle that would be too costly to explain elsewhere. The friendship becomes one of the few places where one can speak in one’s full thickness.

To call this nostalgia would be inaccurate. Nostalgia suggests yearning for a lost place. What is at stake here is more active and more present-tense. The friend does not merely remind you of where you came from. He permits you to continue sounding like yourself in ways migration would otherwise steadily sand down. He does not simply preserve memory; he preserves texture.

This is also why the Ireland–Malaysia background matters without needing to dominate the piece. Ireland in this context is not only a destination; it is a language environment, a social rhythm, a field of new legibilities. Malaysia is not only origin; it is an archive of tones, habits of overreaction, forms of intimacy, and practical shorthand. The friendship does not erase the distance between those worlds. It creates a corridor through which some of their linguistic force can continue to travel.

That corridor is narrower than people like to imagine and more consequential.

We are used to thinking that love travels through grand statements and friendship through shared experience. But a great deal of adult attachment is actually carried by micro-competence: knowing what a silence means, hearing what a phrase is indexing, understanding whether a complaint is really complaint, recognizing when a person has chosen a particular word because its nearest equivalent would be too clean. Diaspora turns these competencies into emotional capital. The friend who possesses them is not merely familiar. He is efficient in the oldest and most intimate sense: he gets there faster.

That may be the real hunger in diaspora—not simply to be accepted, admired, or even loved, but to be read at something like one’s native speed.

A “sampat” friendship satisfies that hunger because it allows language to remain socially thick. The joke does not need to be exported. The tone does not need to be neutralized. The relationship can keep some of its most important meanings in a register that is not fully available to outsiders. That privacy is not exclusion for its own sake. It is the condition under which certain forms of closeness remain vivid.

In the end, diaspora as language is not just about speaking differently in different places. It is about what kinds of self become audible under different regimes of understanding. The friend who shares your idiom gives you back a version of your voice that success abroad may never fully erase, but can easily make harder to hear.

Sometimes that is all intimacy is: someone who knows which word you would use if you were not trying so hard to be understandable.

# IX. Why some relationships stay underdefined

There is a modern superstition that clarity is always kindness.

In some domains this is obviously true. Bureaucracy improves when its language becomes less vague. Medical consent should not depend on poetic indirection. Employment contracts do not benefit from emotional nuance. But relationships are not administrative forms, and the demand that every significant attachment eventually answer the question What is this, exactly? is often less humane than it sounds. Sometimes the insistence on clarity is not a service to truth. It is a demand that the relationship make itself legible to a social order with limited categories and very strong preferences.

That order still prefers its intimacies ranked and named. Romance at the top, family somewhere alongside it, friendship in a subordinate but respectable tier, and everything else either immature, confused, or waiting to become one of the first three. Under this regime, ambiguity appears as a problem to be solved. If two people matter too much to each other, the culture becomes impatient. Are they just friends? Is there unresolved desire? Is this basically a relationship? Are they in denial? Questions like these are often posed as though they were sophisticated, when in fact they are usually signs of categorical laziness. They assume that relational seriousness must eventually reduce itself to one recognizable lane.

But some bonds remain underdefined not because they lack content, but because they contain too much of it.

That is the difference worth holding onto. Undefined is not the same as empty. Ambiguous is not the same as weak. In many adult relationships—especially queer ones, especially diasporic ones, especially those carried by wit, overreaction, and strategically unserious language—underdefinition is not a temporary failure on the road to full articulation. It is the condition under which the bond remains livable. Naming it too quickly, or too cleanly, can narrow what the relationship is allowed to do.

This is where respectable adulthood reveals its own odd insecurity. It likes to imagine that mature people know where they stand. They label things. They establish terms. They separate friendship from romance, dependence from independence, care from desire, history from future. But lived attachments rarely obey so neatly. A person can be the friend you call first, the one whose opinion changes your mood, the one whose delayed reply registers too sharply, the one before whom you are least curated, the one who holds the largest archive of your ridiculousness, and still not be your lover, your sibling, or your spouse. Such a figure is not hard to find in life. He is simply hard to place in culture.

A “sampat” friendship makes this difficulty difficult to ignore. It is too intense to fit comfortably into the sentimental language of “just friends,” yet often too comic, too indirect, too distributed across teasing and overreaction to submit neatly to romantic classification either. It lives in the socially inconvenient territory where one person becomes central through repetition, tone, timing, and absurd overinvestment rather than through declaration. The ambiguity is not decorative. It is structural.

Why might such ambiguity be useful?

First, because naming is not neutral. Every available label carries a script. “Friend” can flatten. “Best friend” can sound adolescent or quaint. “Chosen family” is often too broad. “Romance” introduces expectations of escalation, exclusivity, domestic trajectory, and public legibility. “Partner” solves some problems while creating others. To choose a label is not merely to describe a bond; it is to place that bond inside a social narrative with built-in assumptions about what comes next. Some relationships resist labels because they do not want the next thing the label brings with it.

Second, ambiguity can preserve density. The relationship between two people may draw energy precisely from the fact that it is allowed to contain kinship, irritation, flirtation, loyalty, rivalry, habit, and emotional dependence without being forced to sort these into separate bins. To define it too sharply would not necessarily clarify the bond. It might instead strip away the very overdetermination that makes it feel alive. One of the more boring habits of modern relational thinking is to treat mixed feeling as evidence that someone has failed to understand himself, rather than evidence that human attachment is often mixed by nature.

Third, ambiguity can be a mode of protection. This is not only true in societies where queerness remains more precarious, though it is certainly true there. It is also true in ostensibly liberal settings where explicit naming can still expose a bond to scrutiny it does not need. Once a relationship is named, it becomes available for evaluation. Is it reciprocal enough? Serious enough? Exclusive enough? Healthy enough? Headed somewhere? Adult enough? The underdefined bond escapes some of this audit. It is spared the demand to present itself as a finished moral object.

This does not make ambiguity innocent. It has costs.

One should not romanticize underdefinition simply because it frustrates the culture’s appetite for neatness. Ambiguity can shelter cowardice. It can conceal asymmetry. It can allow one person to enjoy intensity without accepting responsibility for the effect of that intensity on the other. It can become a way of permanent deferral, especially when one party wants a clearer account of what the bond means and the other prefers the pleasures of suspension. The line between richness and evasion is real. Not every refusal to define is brave. Some are merely convenient.

But this is precisely why the matter cannot be settled by a general rule in favor of clarity. The real question is not whether ambiguity is good or bad in the abstract. It is whether, in a given bond, ambiguity is functioning as space or as avoidance. Does it make the relationship more breathable, more capacious, less overdetermined by external scripts? Or does it simply redistribute uncertainty downward onto the person with less power to withstand it? These are not theoretical questions. They are practical ones, though theory helps ask them cleanly.

Queer life has long supplied reasons to be suspicious of the demand for immediate legibility. It has had to. A great deal of queer intimacy has historically depended on partial visibility, strategic indirection, coded recognition, and forms of closeness that exceeded public language. Even in more permissive contexts, that inheritance remains. Not every bond wants to become a public identity. Not every attachment is improved by being converted into a relationship status. There are ways of mattering deeply to someone that are less false when they remain slightly oblique.

This is part of what makes the “sampat” frame so revealing. The word is comic enough to deflect some of the pressure to define. It carries intensity without freezing it into solemnity. One can say, with a mixture of complaint and affection, that a person is *so sampat*, and thereby acknowledge an entire structure of overinvolved care without having to tidy it into a cleaner form. The slang does covert theoretical work. It preserves ambiguity by giving the bond a name that is not quite a category.

That is a useful distinction: a name that is not quite a category.

Categories tell us what a thing is in relation to other recognized things. Names often do something more local and less settled. They gather a texture without fully legislating an identity. *Sampat* is useful not because it explains the relationship once and for all, but because it lets the relationship remain itself while still being pointed to. It says: here is a recognizable kind of intensity. It does not say: therefore this must be romance, or kinship, or friendship in the thin polite sense. It holds the shape without overclassifying it.

This may be the closest adulthood gets to honesty in some cases. Not the honesty of explicit inventory, but the honesty of resisting false precision. The culture’s hunger for clean definitions often masks a deeper discomfort with attachments that exceed their assigned station. We are not unsettled by ambiguity because it is always painful. We are unsettled because it deprives us of our preferred hierarchy. It suggests that a life may be organized around people who do not fit properly into the ladder of sanctioned importance.

That suggestion is particularly sharp in mid-life, when one is expected to have settled the matter of who counts most. The adult life course, as commonly imagined, narrows and codifies. One builds the core and lets the peripheral remain peripheral. But many lives do not sort themselves so tidily. Some central figures remain central precisely because they never agreed to become official. They survive not by upgrading their label but by accumulating function. They become the person who knows. The person who notices. The person who can get away with too much. The person whose tone one can hear before the message is even read. Such centrality does not always need a better name. Sometimes the pressure to give it one comes mostly from the outside.

Still, the outside matters, because no relationship is lived outside interpretation entirely. Even underdefined bonds are shaped by what they refuse. To remain unnamed in a culture obsessed with naming is already a position. To refuse the couple script is already a kind of counterclaim. This is one reason anti-romance is not just a mood but a structure. It marks the decision, deliberate or not, to let a bond retain its force without being justified by the promise of conventional futurity.

The risk, again, is that anti-romance can look noble while functioning badly. One can hide irresponsibility inside a vocabulary of freedom just as easily as one can hide dependency inside a vocabulary of friendship. But one can also tell the truth more precisely by admitting that some relationships are central because they refuse simplification, not despite it. The point is not to celebrate vagueness. It is to defend complexity against the false clarity of categories too thin for the life they are meant to describe.

Perhaps that is why some of the most important relationships sound unserious when named out loud. The unseriousness buys them room. It keeps them from hardening prematurely into the social forms most available for inspection. A “sampat” friendship can carry loyalty, desire, irritation, recognition, relief, and habit without immediately becoming one moral object. It can remain untidy, and therefore accurate.

The culture tends to think adulthood means finally learning to define oneself cleanly. But perhaps adulthood, at least the better kind, also includes learning which realities become less true when they are made too neat. Some people are not hard to describe because they are unreal. They are hard to describe because they are too real in too many directions at once.

The question, then, is not why some relationships stay underdefined. The more interesting question is why the rest of us remain so committed to pretending that definition is always where seriousness goes to prove itself.

Sometimes seriousness proves itself by refusing a smaller shape.

# X. The relationships that look unserious from the outside

It is possible to live an entire adult life surrounded by the wrong language.

By wrong, I do not mean false in any simple sense. I mean language too coarse to catch what is actually happening, too respectable to admit the forms in which closeness often appears, too obedient to inherited hierarchies of value. We are given words for spouses, partners, marriages, households, families, professional peers, social networks, support systems. We are given no shortage of language for the relationships that public life already knows how to honor. What we are given much less often are words for the people who become indispensable through tone, repetition, mutual irritation, overreaction, and the embarrassing accumulation of emotional fact.

These are the relationships that do not always announce themselves in time for cultural recognition. They arrive sideways. They become important before anyone has explained why. They do not necessarily possess the grandeur of romance or the ceremonial weight of kinship. They may never become formally legible at all. And yet they alter the architecture of a life. They become the person one thinks with, the person one becomes ridiculous for, the person whose messages change the pressure in the day, the person who remains somehow outside the official map and yet very near the center of the lived one.

From the outside, such relationships can look faintly absurd. Too many messages. Too much fuss over too little. Too much reading into tone. Too much delight in mock-annoyance. Too much linguistic weather around small practical events. It is easy, from that vantage, to mistake the ridiculousness for triviality. But ridiculousness and triviality are not the same. Some bonds survive precisely because they are allowed to be comic rather than monumental, excessive rather than polished, socially messy rather than morally overexplained. The laughter is not evidence that nothing serious is happening. Very often it is the form seriousness takes when it refuses to become pompous.

That may be especially true in adult life, which has a way of becoming overcomposed. People get older. Their biographies harden. Their roles multiply. Their speech grows cleaner. They become easier to summarize and harder to know. Success, when it comes, often brings with it a refined public grammar of capability: one sounds sensible, measured, appropriately burdened, plausibly resilient. One becomes understandable to institutions and, sometimes, less available to more intimate forms of reading. A life can become coherent in all the right ways and still ache for someone before whom coherence is not mandatory.

This is one reason the unserious bond matters. It offers not escape from adulthood, but relief from its constant self-management. It gives a person one place where proportion can be suspended, where irritation can be affectionate, where dramatic overstatement can function as care, where the self does not need to arrive pre-edited into the style of mature competence. The bond may not look elevated. It may look, in fact, a little humiliating: one grown person becoming much too emotionally involved in another person’s reply speed, stress level, complaints, appetite, exhaustion, silence, or general behavioral nonsense. But humiliation has always been one of intimacy’s quieter conditions. To matter is often to become a little ridiculous.

The more one thinks about it, the stranger it seems that culture remains so reluctant to grant full seriousness to relationships that fail to follow the expected script. We are willing to concede that friendship is meaningful, of course, but often only in the manner one concedes that good weather is pleasant. What we are slower to admit is that friendship—especially queer friendship, especially adult friendship, especially friendship shaped by shared language and migratory distance—can become one of the primary forms in which a life is emotionally organized. Not backup. Not accessory. Not the lovely remainder once the real relationships have been sorted. Primary.

To say this is not to diminish romance, or family, or any other bond that structures a life. It is simply to refuse the old ranking system in which some forms of attachment are assumed to be profound and others must earn profundity by imitation. A friendship does not become serious only when it starts resembling a marriage. It may be serious in an entirely different way. Its depth may lie in recurrence rather than destiny, in witness rather than public claim, in tonal fluency rather than contractual clarity, in a thousand small acts of overattentive presence rather than one transforming declaration.

This is where the word *sampat* keeps proving useful. Not because it explains everything, but because it resists the pressure to over-sanitize what it names. It preserves excess. It preserves embarrassment. It preserves the fact that some people become central to us through ways of speaking and reacting that would look faintly unbearable if stripped from their context. It keeps intimacy tethered to social life as it is actually lived: interruptive, repetitive, overinvested, funny, difficult to translate cleanly upward into the language of proper adulthood.

And perhaps proper adulthood is not the best measure of truth anyway.

Perhaps the better measure is this: who can still make you legible to yourself when the rest of your life has become a sequence of roles? Who can hear the local weather still moving inside your polished sentences? Who knows which word you would use if you were not trying to be universally understandable? Who can tell, from one badly punctuated message or one overblown complaint, the exact scale of what is wrong? Who has earned the right to react too much?

For a Malaysian Chinese gay oncologist in Ireland and a Malaysian Chinese gay psychiatrist in Malaysia, those questions travel across more than geography. They travel across different public languages of adulthood, different social climates of legibility, different tempos of daily life. But the distance only sharpens the point. Not every crossing between countries produces intimacy. What produces intimacy is something both smaller and harder to manufacture: shared pragmatic instinct, shared tonal memory, a shared willingness to let ridiculousness carry emotional truth. Somewhere between Ireland and Malaysia, between migration and continuity, between polished professional life and the vernacular mess underneath it, a friendship can become the corridor through which a more complete self continues to move.

That corridor does not need to look noble to be real. It does not need to end in romance to be central. It does not need to be fully defined to have shape.

It may even be better, in some cases, that it is not.

Because the deepest problem here is not that we lack relationships. It is that we still lack adequate ways of honoring the ones that do not present themselves in approved forms. We know how to celebrate commitment when it becomes public and legible. We know less about how to speak of the person who becomes indispensable through accumulated private absurdity. We know how to grant dignity to formal bonds. We know less about how to dignify the friend who has seen too much, reacted too much, understood too quickly, and stayed.

So perhaps the task is not to force every such relationship into a cleaner category. Perhaps the task is to become less frightened of attachment that looks socially untidy. To admit that adult life is not only structured by the loves we can name without embarrassment, but also by those we still reach for in sideways language, in regional slang, in ironic complaint, in words that seem too comic for the weight they carry. Some of the most important relationships in a life do not arrive under a grand title. They arrive sounding faintly ridiculous.

And then, over time, one realizes that ridiculousness was never the opposite of seriousness. It was one of its vernacular forms.

If modern adulthood has become too fluent in the language of competence, perhaps what it needs are not simply better categories, but more tolerance for the bonds that exceed them. Not every central relationship wants to become a couple, a family, or a moral lesson. Some want only to remain what they are: excessive, sustaining, difficult to classify, impossible to dismiss. Some want to be the person on the other end of the line who can still make you sound, for a moment, less translated.

That may be the broader claim after all: a life is not held together only by its official attachments. It is also held together by the people with whom one is allowed to be too much.
