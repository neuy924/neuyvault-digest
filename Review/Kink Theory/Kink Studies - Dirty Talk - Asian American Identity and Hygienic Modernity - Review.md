## Abstract (English)

Eroticized disgust and humiliation pose a theoretical problem: affects that ordinarily police boundaries and status can be sought, scripted, and metabolized as erotic intensity in kink and BDSM. This paper offers a translation grammar that makes the phenomenon commensurable across sexuality studies, cultural sociology, and comparative Daoist–Buddhist thought without asserting equivalence of ends. It formalizes a scene-level base plane with Disgust (D) as boundary-work and Humiliation (H) as status/face-work, then adds Symbolic Distance (S) to capture whether dirtying and degrading signs are held as role-bounded “as-if” performance versus literal truth-claims, and Risk (R) to keep objective hazard analytically orthogonal to aesthetic “dirt.” The paper then explicitly separates two Daoisms: philosophical 道家 (榮辱 and 面子 as valuation traps that illuminate humiliation’s volatility) and religious 道教 (齋／醮 as boundary-governance technologies that clarify how “pollution” is contained and repaired). It stages two Buddhist strategies as a productive tension: asubha as re-categorization of embodiment to loosen attachment/aversion, and 方便 (upāya) as disciplined inversion of pure/impure binaries under stringent framing. Finally, a micro-historiography contrasts East Asian hygienic modernity with Asian American racialization and respectability politics to show why “dirty” becomes a portable, historically charged predicate whose stickiness is intensified or buffered through language choice and bilingual code-switching (髒／污／穢). The result is a theory of when erotic boundary-play loosens purity and rank, and when it reproduces stigma through leakage beyond the scene.

## 1\. Introduction: why bring 道家 and 佛教 into eroticized disgust and humiliation—and why compare Asian Americans with East Asians?

Eroticized disgust and eroticized humiliation sit at an awkward crossroads of sensation, social hierarchy, and meaning. Disgust typically announces “keep away”: a boundary alarm that treats some substances, proximities, or categories as contaminating. Humiliation typically announces “you are lowered”: a status alarm that makes the social self feel watched, diminished, or exposed. Yet in kink and BDSM, both alarms can be solicited, stylized, and transformed into erotic intensity. The phenomenon is rarely illuminated by accounts that treat it either as a private idiosyncrasy (“some people are wired this way”) or as a purely discursive trick (“taboo is constructed, therefore anything can be erotic”). Erotic disgust and humiliation are better approached as scene-level achievements: they are produced through scripts, negotiated frames, ritualized openings and closings, and lexicons that carry histories of purity and devaluation.

This paper proposes a hybrid analytic: a sociology of sexuality and cultural sociology of affect, translated through comparative philosophy. The aim is not to spiritualize kink, nor to eroticize religion. Instead, Daoist and Buddhist traditions are treated as unusually refined theories of what happens when persons take evaluative categories—honor/disgrace, pure/impure—as ultimate truths about the self. Kink scenes that eroticize disgust and humiliation are structured encounters with those same categories, made legible by the fact that they must be framed, governed, and repaired if they are to remain play rather than injury. The comparative move is therefore structural rather than identificatory: it maps mechanisms of reification and de-reification across domains without claiming equivalence of ends.

The translation grammar is deliberately minimal and scene-level. Disgust (D) and Humiliation (H) name two separable affective operations: boundary-work and status/face-work. Symbolic Distance (S) names how far the scene is held as role-bounded “as-if” performance rather than a literal truth-claim about who someone really is; it is the variable that predicts whether a dirtying insult remains theatrical or hardens into identity injury. Risk (R) names objective hazard independent of disgust aesthetics; it prevents the common moral confusion where “gross” becomes a proxy for “dangerous.” These variables are not offered as a psychology of the soul. They are offered as a set of handles that make philosophical vocabulary commensurable with sociological mechanism and with empirical questions about residue, leakage, and repair.

The philosophical modules are explicitly partitioned to avoid a frequent category drift. “Daoism” in popular speech often becomes a single mood—自然, flow, serenity—yet the historical field includes both philosophical Daoist texts and ritual institutions. This paper distinguishes two Daoisms. Philosophical Daoism, associated with 道德經 and 莊子, is most useful for thinking about humiliation because it treats 榮辱—honor and disgrace—as conditions that hook the person into fear and instability, a trap of valuation rather than a stable measure of worth. Religious Daoism, by contrast, is most useful for thinking about disgust and boundary governance because it operationalizes purity as technique: preparatory abstentions and purification (齋), carefully sequenced ritual worlds (醮), and restoration logics that prevent boundary disturbance from diffusing into social disorder. In the language of this paper, philosophical Daoism is an analysis of status reification; religious Daoism is an infrastructure of boundary management.

“Buddhism” is likewise treated as containing two distinct strategies for working with impurity and attachment, rather than as a single doctrine. Strategy A is aversion-training through impurity contemplation (aśubha/asubha), which deliberately engages bodily “impurity” to loosen grasping and re-categorize what the body is taken to be. Strategy B is category inversion justified as 方便 (upāya, skillful means) in certain tantric/Vajrayāna contexts, where impurity motifs may be deployed—under strict constraint and disciplined framing—to destabilize reified pure/impure binaries. The analytic point is not that BDSM reenacts tantra. The point is that Buddhist traditions contain internal resources for theorizing (a) the trainability of disgust and aversion, and (b) the necessity of exceptionally strong framing whenever impurity is used as a transformative device rather than as a merely contaminating force. That necessity closely parallels why Symbolic Distance (S) emerges in kink practice as the hinge between intense play and corrosive leakage.

The diaspora comparison—Asian Americans versus East Asians in East Asia—is not an add-on; it is the stress test that makes the mapping intellectually necessary rather than ornamental. “Dirty” is never only sensory. It is classed, moralized, and frequently racialized. In East Asian modernities, hygiene and respectability were tied to projects of statecraft, modernization, and middle-class discipline; cleanliness becomes a sign of cultivated personhood, not only a health practice. In East Asia, this produces purity economies that are dense but locally organized: “dirty” tends to be policed through family discipline, workplace respectability, and national narratives of civility, often in ways that are legible within the same cultural archive that produced them. Among Asian Americans, however, the meaning of “dirty” is forced to travel across an additional boundary: the racial–sexual regime of the United States, where “unclean” talk has historically functioned as a technology of sorting bodies, regulating intimacy, and producing deviance. For Asian Americans, then, dirtying cues can arrive with double pressure. They can activate inherited respectability scripts at home and racialized stereotype scripts outside the home, simultaneously. This is precisely the configuration in which humiliation (status devaluation) and disgust (boundary devaluation) couple most tightly, and precisely the configuration in which symbolic distance is most fragile. A phrase that remains theatrical in one context can become a social verdict in another, not because any individual speaker is secretly cruel, but because histories and audiences are silently present in the room.

That comparative framing yields a distinctive theoretical payoff. If Daoist and Buddhist analyses are, among other things, critiques of mistaking evaluative categories for ultimate truths, then the key question for eroticized disgust and humiliation becomes: under what conditions do “dirty” and “lowered” remain bounded role-tokens rather than becoming literalized as identity? In East Asia, the boundary between role and person may be stabilized or destabilized by local purity regimes; in Asian America, the same boundary is often strained by external racialization, publicness, and the anticipatory gaze of an imagined audience. The paper therefore treats Symbolic Distance (S) as the hinge concept that can speak simultaneously to comparative philosophy (de-reification), to cultural sociology (framing and performativity), and to sexuality studies (scripted scenes and stigma management). It also treats Risk (R) as a necessary orthogonal axis so that “dirty” is not allowed to smuggle moral disgust into safety claims.

The remainder of the paper develops this program in five moves. Section 2 formalizes the translation grammar (D/H/S/R) and clarifies the anti-category-error rules that keep comparative philosophy from becoming metaphor. Sections 3 and 4 lay out the two Daoisms and the two Buddhist strategies, each mapped explicitly onto D/H/S/R. Section 5 provides a micro-historiography of East Asian hygienic modernity and respectability as a substrate for contemporary purity economies, then contrasts it with the racialized sexual scripting that shapes “dirty” meanings for Asian Americans. Section 6 treats language—dirty talk, generic filth metaphors versus historically loaded dirtying tropes—as the empirical hinge where disgust becomes a performative act that can either remain within play or collapse symbolic distance. The paper closes by proposing a set of testable predictions about when eroticized disgust and humiliation function as controlled liminal play and when they become stigma amplifiers, with the Asian American versus East Asian comparison serving as the primary diagnostic contrast.

## 2\. A translation grammar: D, H, S, R and the rules of comparison

The argument of this paper depends on a deliberately minimal set of scene-level variables that can travel between sexuality studies, cultural sociology, and comparative philosophy without collapsing their differences. The goal is not to convert kink into metaphysics or to convert Daoism and Buddhism into psychology. The goal is to build a “translation grammar” that keeps three things simultaneously in view: (1) what a scene does affectively, (2) how it is held as meaningful or not meaningful beyond the scene, and (3) what hazards it carries independent of meaning. The grammar begins with two axes—Disgust (D) and Humiliation (H)—and adds two orthogonal extensions—Symbolic Distance (S) and Risk (R). Each variable is defined at the level of the scene-as-event, not at the level of stable personality, because erotic disgust and humiliation are best understood as scripted achievements rather than as fixed traits.

Disgust (D) names the salience of contamination and abjection cues within the scene: cues that mark contact, proximity, or substance as boundary-threatening, “out of place,” or difficult to metabolize. In ordinary life, these cues activate recoil and avoidance. In BDSM contexts, they can be converted into intensity when the scene provides a credible frame of safety, play, and repair. D therefore indexes boundary-work: the scene’s recruitment of the felt distinction between clean/unclean, inside/outside, self/other, person/thing. The crucial point is that D is not identical with danger. Disgust can be triggered by purely symbolic cues, and low-disgust scenes can involve high hazard. D is the phenomenological and semiotic charge of “dirty,” not a proxy for biomedical risk.

Humiliation (H) names the salience of status and face cues within the scene: cues that lower, expose, discipline, ridicule, or otherwise threaten the social self. Humiliation is structurally tied to the gaze of others, even when no one else is present, because the social self is built out of imagined audiences. H therefore indexes status-work: the scene’s recruitment of hierarchy, rank, submission, “being made less,” and the risky pleasures of exposure. In Chinese terms, humiliation touches the ecology of 面子 (face) and its management; it is not reducible to disgust, even though “dirtying” language often smuggles devaluation through contamination metaphors. A scene can be high D and low H when it plays on contamination cues while preserving affirmation and dignity. A scene can be high H and low D when it plays on rank, discipline, or embarrassment without invoking filth or impurity. Treating D and H as separable prevents a common analytic error: collapsing all “dirty” kink into degradation, and collapsing all degradation into disgust.

The third variable, Symbolic Distance (S), captures how the scene’s signs are held as meaningful. S indexes the degree to which dirtying cues and degrading speech acts are processed as role-bounded “as-if” performance rather than as literal truth-claims about the person. The distinction matters because disgust and humiliation are volatile precisely when they generalize. Disgust can leave contamination residue: lingering “unclean” feelings, avoidance, rumination, or moral self-disgust. Humiliation can leave shame residue: feelings of being diminished, mistrust, or a damaged sense of worth. Symbolic distance is the insulation that prevents generalization. It is produced through sexual scripts and interactional framing: explicit role-bounding (“this is play”), stylized genre cues (protocol, ritual language), tonal markers (exaggeration, incantatory cadence), privacy infrastructures, and closure practices that reintegrate the ordinary person. It can also collapse, especially when words or acts align with historically loaded hierarchies—racialized or classed “dirtying” tropes, identity-proximal insults, or publicness that activates a hostile imagined audience. S therefore translates a comparative-philosophical concern—whether an evaluative category is taken as ultimately true—into a scene-level variable that can be described without claiming spiritual attainment. In the Daoist module, this will resonate with not being captured by 榮辱 (honor/disgrace). In the Buddhist module, it will resonate with not mistaking 淨/穢 (pure/impure) for ultimate reality. But S remains a sociological construct: it is about framing and meaning-containment, not enlightenment.

The fourth variable, Risk (R), captures objective hazard independent of symbolic meaning. R refers to the probability and severity of physical or biomedical harm given the techniques and constraints of the scene. This variable is necessary because “dirty” is an unreliable moral instrument: people often invoke disgust to police danger, and invoke danger to moralize disgust. Separating R from D and H allows analysis to remain ethically concrete without turning into condemnation. A scene can be high D and low R, where “dirty” is largely aesthetic or symbolic. A scene can be low D and high R, where nothing feels particularly filthy yet hazards exist. R is thus the practice-facing extension of the grammar, while S is the theory-facing extension. Together they prevent the two most common errors in writing about kink: collapsing safety into respectability, and collapsing meaning into hazard.

These variables do not exist in isolation; they interconvert through a bridging construct that the paper will return to repeatedly: residue and leakage. Residue refers to what remains after the scene—contamination feelings, shame, relational mistrust, or intensified bonding. Leakage refers to the extension of scene meanings beyond the scene—when “dirty” becomes an enduring self-concept, when humiliation becomes contempt, or when a private script becomes legible as stigma under a broader social gaze. The grammar predicts that leakage is most likely when humiliation is high and symbolic distance collapses, or when disgust is high and closure fails to restore ordinary boundaries. It also predicts that diaspora contexts, especially Asian American contexts, will show higher structural fragility of S for certain lexicons because “dirty” and “unclean” are not merely private metaphors there; they are socially policed predicates that can align with racialized stereotypes and respectability regimes.

Because the paper uses Daoist and Buddhist resources, it must also specify rules of comparison to avoid category errors. The first rule is telos non-equivalence: philosophical and religious practices are oriented toward liberation, order, or cosmological alignment, while BDSM scenes are oriented toward erotic play, intimacy, and negotiated power. Structural analogy does not imply shared ends. The second rule is institutional non-equivalence: 方便 (upāya) in tantric contexts is embedded in lineage constraints and vows; it cannot be treated as a general license for transgression. Analogies to framing and constraint can be drawn, but claims of equivalence should be refused. The third rule is semantic non-equivalence: “non-reification” is not the same as dissociation, and symbolic distance is not the same as transcendence. S refers to interpretive containment within a social event, not a metaphysical insight. The fourth rule is historical charge awareness: purity regimes and dirtying tropes carry classed and racialized histories that change how signs function. A term that operates as generic filth in one setting can operate as dehumanization in another; the grammar is designed to make that difference describable rather than moralistically asserted.

Finally, the grammar makes clear why the paper must separate two Daoisms and two Buddhist strategies. Philosophical 道家 texts will be read as diagnostic theories of how attachment to 榮辱 and valuation captures the self—hence their relevance to H and S. Religious 道教 will be read as boundary technologies—hence their relevance to D, R, and ritual closure. Buddhist asubha practice will be read as aversion-training through impurity re-categorization—hence its relevance to D plasticity and S reappraisal. Buddhist 方便 will be read as a disciplined logic for category inversion under constraint—hence its relevance to the strongest possible framing demands on S, and to the insistence that R cannot be wished away by symbolism. With this grammar in place, the subsequent sections can map concepts across domains without treating any domain as a mere metaphor for another, and without losing the central object: how eroticized disgust and humiliation are made, held, and repaired in scenes whose meanings are always already entangled with histories of purity and stigma.

## 3\. Two Daoisms: valuation critique and purity governance

### 3.1 Philosophical 道家: 榮辱, 面子, and the reification of rank

Philosophical 道家 is most useful to this project not because it offers a ready-made ethic for sexuality, but because it offers a diagnosis of how the self becomes capturable by social valuation. In the paper’s grammar, humiliation (H) is the scene’s activation of status-work: lowering, exposure, discipline, ridicule, and the felt presence of an audience—real or imagined. A Daoist lens treats such status-work as a problem of reification: the moment when “I am lowered” is not merely a scene token but becomes an ontological claim about who I am. The point is not to deny that rank has real social consequences, but to notice how rank becomes internalized as the self’s measure and thus becomes a lever for fear.

This is why the thematic complex 榮辱 matters. The oscillation between honor and disgrace is not, in philosophical 道家, a neutral spectrum of social outcomes; it is a machine for producing disturbance. When favor and disgrace become the axis of self-regard, the self is compelled to chase what is unstable and to fear its reversal. The resulting vulnerability is precisely the affective profile of humiliation when symbolic distance collapses: the scene stops being a bounded performance and begins to feel like an exposure of truth. In the model’s terms, the Daoist critique targets the low-S version of humiliation—the version in which the demeaning sign is not held as “as-if,” but as a verdict.

面子 sharpens this analysis by specifying the social technology through which humiliation binds. Face is not merely pride; it is a relational object produced and maintained through audience, recognition, and social legibility. Humiliation play reliably intensifies the imagined audience because it mobilizes what is socially legible: who is above, who is below, who commands, who obeys, who is seen, who is hidden. A Daoist reading does not treat this as “bad,” but it does treat it as dangerous to the extent that the subject clings to face as the ground of selfhood. The provocative comparative claim is that symbolic distance (S) in erotic humiliation is, functionally, a containment practice against face reification. High S allows the participant to undergo status inversion without converting it into a stable identity statement. Low S allows the scene to colonize the self: humiliation becomes shame residue, and the relational field becomes contaminated by a sense that contempt is “really there.”

A second Daoist resource, especially visible in 莊子, is the suspicion of rigid categories and the cultivation of play in distinctions. Where humiliation is status-work, the Daoist question is whether the scene’s hierarchy is being treated as metaphysically thick or as instrumentally thin. Instrumental thinness does not mean emotionally shallow. It means that the participant can feel the full intensity of being lowered while still holding the role as a token within a larger process—an event rather than a definition. The “thinness” here corresponds closely to S: the capacity of the scene to remain a scene. This also makes clear why Daoist diagnosis does not imply Daoist endorsement. A scene can mobilize humiliation in ways that loosen fixation on rank, or it can mobilize humiliation in ways that deepen fixation through degradation that becomes literalized. The analytic point is that the difference is not in the surface content alone; it is in whether valuation is being treated as ultimate.

### 3.2 Religious 道教: 齋/醮 as boundary technologies and the governance of pollution

Religious 道教 enters the paper through a different door. Where philosophical 道家 is an analysis of valuation capture, religious 道教 is an infrastructure of boundary governance. Its relevance lies in how it operationalizes purity not as mere moralism but as a technology for making boundary-crossing containable. In the model’s grammar, disgust (D) is the salience of contamination and abjection cues—what feels “unclean,” “out of place,” or boundary-threatening. Risk (R) is objective hazard independent of aesthetic disgust. Religious Daoist ritual systems are not “kink-like,” but they do illuminate how cultures build practices that approach potentially polluting forces under constraints that prevent diffusion: preparation, sequence, protected space, authorized roles, and restoration. Those are exactly the structural problems that any high-D scene must solve if it is to remain intense without producing lasting contamination residue.

齋 (zhāi) can be treated as a preparatory regime that reorganizes the practitioner’s body, attention, and social conduct so that a ritual world can be entered without uncontrolled leakage. Whatever the cosmological metaphysics, the practical form is recognizable: abstentions, restrictions, and purification operate as boundary-setting devices. They do not merely declare “cleanliness”; they manufacture a temporary ecology in which contact, speech, and presence are governed. 醮 (jiào), as a communal ritual complex, intensifies this ecological dimension: it creates a bounded field with roles, sequences, and transformations, often oriented toward restoring order and negotiating with forces that are not casually approached. The analytic payoff is not that Daoist ritual purity “explains” BDSM. It is that religious Daoism makes purity legible as governance rather than as superstition, and thereby helps us think about how disgust can be approached without becoming sticky.

This matters for the paper’s central comparative contrast between Asian Americans and East Asians. In East Asian contexts, purity regimes—whether inherited, modernized, or hybrid—often remain locally intelligible: the grammar of “clean/proper” is embedded in family discipline, institutional respectability, and everyday etiquette. In Asian American contexts, that grammar is forced to negotiate a second regime: the racialized public sphere in which “unclean” talk has historically functioned as a sorting device. When erotic disgust is staged, the scene may therefore be traversing not one purity ecology but two. The religious Daoist emphasis on preparation and restoration provides an unexpectedly concrete language for why closure practices matter sociologically: aftercare, cleanup, re-naming, and debrief are not only comforting; they are mechanisms that prevent pollution from becoming an identity predicate. In the model, they are part of what stabilizes S and reduces residue at high D, and they are also part of what keeps “dirty” from being recruited as a weapon of valuation at high H.

Religious 道教 also highlights an often-missed feature of boundary work: purity is not only about substances; it is about authorized relations. Who is permitted to touch what, say what, enter where, and under which conditions is a social ordering problem disguised as hygiene. That observation loops back to the philosophical Daoist critique of valuation. Scenes that eroticize disgust and humiliation often braid boundary-work and status-work by turning “dirtying” into lowering. The Daoist split therefore helps keep the braid analyzable. Philosophical 道家 clarifies the danger of taking lowering as ultimate truth; religious 道教 clarifies the necessity of building boundary governance when playing near pollution cues. Together they justify the paper’s insistence that intense erotic boundary play is never only sensation: it is a managed ecology of valuation and purity, with framing and restoration as the hinge between generative transgression and corrosive leakage.

## 4\. Two Buddhist strategies: re-categorization and inversion as rival technologies of impurity

Where the Daoist split clarified two different orientations toward valuation and purity—philosophical critique of 榮辱 and ritual governance of boundary ecologies—Buddhist materials sharpen a different tension. Buddhism does not merely denounce impurity or celebrate transgression; it offers multiple strategies for working with the very mechanisms by which the mind turns bodies and categories into sources of grasping, aversion, and suffering. For the purposes of this paper, two strategies are especially instructive because they pull in opposite directions while targeting the same problem: how “pure/impure” becomes a sticky ontology. Strategy A, commonly associated with aśubha/asubha practice, works by re-categorization: it reshapes perception so that the attractive object is seen differently, thereby loosening attachment and re-training aversion. Strategy B, often framed under 方便 (upāya, skillful means) in Vajrayāna/tantric registers, works by inversion: it uses impurity motifs to destabilize the binary itself, attempting to short-circuit the compulsion to sort experience into clean/dirty in the first place. Their tension is not a scholastic curiosity. It is a conceptual engine for thinking about eroticized disgust because it clarifies two distinct ways that “impurity” can be engaged: one that cools the erotic charge by changing what the body is taken to be, and one that intensifies charge under strict framing to dissolve the authority of the category.

Strategy A begins from a sober phenomenology: the mind is prone to project solidity and desirability onto bodies, then to defend those projections through craving and aversion. Asubha practice intervenes by systematically re-describing the body—not as an object of glamour, but as a composite, impermanent, and often unlovely process. The method is not primarily moral; it is cognitive-affective. The practitioner repeatedly attends to aspects of embodiment that ordinary erotic imagination edits out, thereby weakening the felt inevitability of attraction. This is re-categorization in a strong sense: the same body is still there, but its meaning has been rebuilt. What matters for this paper is that asubha demonstrates a disciplined claim about disgust: disgust is not merely a reflex; it can be cultivated, sustained, and related to differently. The affective alarm can be turned into an instrument of insight because its object is reconstituted as “not worth clinging to,” and because the practitioner learns to hold the cue without spiraling into panic or self-hatred. In the translation grammar, this is a technology for changing the coupling between disgust cues and identity: boundary signals are engaged, but the goal is to dissolve the spell of the object rather than to eroticize the alarm.

Eroticized disgust, by contrast, often does the opposite: it intensifies the boundary alarm and converts it into arousal. That might seem to place it outside the Buddhist frame. Yet the deeper structural resonance is this: both asubha and BDSM disgust play are experiments in how cues acquire meaning. The difference is telos and directionality. Asubha aims to reduce grasping by cooling desire; BDSM disgust play often aims to heighten intensity by heating the boundary. The tension is productive because it forces an analytic question that sexuality studies sometimes evades: does repeated engagement with impurity cues loosen fixation on purity, or does it merely rehearse it in erotic form? Asubha insists that the answer depends on framing and relation: without a container that prevents disgust from hardening into moral condemnation, impurity contemplation can slide into aversion; without a container that prevents erotic disgust from hardening into self-disgust, kink can slide into residue. The strategies differ, but both are sensitive to how the mind literalizes categories.

Strategy B presses on the category itself. In certain tantric/Vajrayāna idioms, impurity motifs—charnel imagery, boundary-crossing substances, taboo inversions—appear not as descriptions of the world but as instruments meant to undermine reification. Here impurity is not primarily used to teach “this body is not desirable.” Instead, it is used to puncture the very confidence with which the mind insists on pure/impure as ultimate. This is inversion: the sign that ordinarily triggers avoidance is brought into the center of practice under a frame that claims it is medicinal, not contaminating. 方便 names the justificatory logic: the practice is not an endorsement of impurity as such, but a method tailored to conditions, capable of using what ordinarily binds the mind in order to free it. The price of this approach is that it requires extraordinary containment. Inversion without containment does not dissolve binaries; it often amplifies them. If “impurity” is approached without stable framing, it becomes merely scandal, indulgence, or harm; if approached with rigid pride, it becomes another badge of purity-by-transgression. The strategy’s central vulnerability is therefore not impurity but misrecognition: mistaking inversion for liberation.

For this paper’s purposes, Strategy B offers two analytic gifts. First, it clarifies why symbolic framing is not decorative. If impurity is used as a method rather than an accident, the practice must stabilize the “as-if” status of the sign—its non-literalness at the level of selfhood. Second, it clarifies why risk cannot be spiritualized away. Even in religious contexts, 方便 is not a license to ignore constraints; it is a reason to take constraints seriously because the method is potent and easily misapplied. This resonates sharply with the kink model’s insistence on separating symbolic “dirty” from objective hazard. Inversion can intensify disgust cues without increasing risk, but it can also tempt the practitioner—or the scene—into confusing intensity with safety or meaning with permission. The very logic of “using impurity” makes error more likely when the frame is weak.

The tension between re-categorization and inversion therefore maps onto two ways of handling the stickiness of “dirty.” Re-categorization changes the object: it says, in effect, “what you thought was pure/desirable is composite and impermanent,” and it works by cooling attachment. Inversion changes the category: it says, in effect, “pure/impure is not the kind of distinction you think it is,” and it works by bringing the taboo into proximity under stringent framing. In diaspora contexts—especially among Asian Americans—this tension becomes more than an intramural Buddhist debate because the category “dirty” is not merely internal. It is socially weaponized through racialization, respectability politics, and the imagined public gaze. Re-categorization offers a way to understand why certain inherited purity scripts can be softened through cognitive-affective training; inversion offers a way to understand why playing with impurity can feel liberating yet be structurally fragile when the social world insists on reading impurity literally. A key implication, carried forward to the mapping table and the historiography, is that “impurity” is never just a cue. It is a social predicate with histories. Buddhist strategies, read as technologies of category management, help explain why eroticized disgust can either loosen purity fixation or become a rehearsal of stigma, depending on how the scene frames the sign, repairs the self, and negotiates the outside world that is always already prepared to interpret “dirty” as truth.

## 5\. Hygienic modernity and the afterlives of purity: East Asia, then Asian America

To understand why “dirty” can function as an erotic intensifier and a stigma weapon at the same time, it helps to treat purity not as a timeless moral instinct but as a historically engineered regime. In East Asia, “cleanliness” and “hygiene” became densely institutionalized across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries through projects of modernization that tied bodily discipline to national strength, civic order, and middle-class respectability. Hygiene was never only a medical discourse. It was also an aesthetic and moral pedagogy—training bodies to become legible as modern, cultivated, and governable. In such settings, the boundary between the sanitary and the respectable often thinned: what counted as “clean” increasingly indexed self-control, education, and proper comportment. This is one reason “dirty” carries such reliable force as a social predicate. It is not merely an assessment of germs; it is a verdict about the person’s capacity for discipline.

East Asian purity economies are not uniform, but they share a family resemblance: cleanliness becomes a sign of cultivated personhood, and cultivated personhood becomes a classed horizon. A modern urban middle-class ideal—quiet control of bodily outputs, careful regulation of smell and visibility, appropriate clothing, proper speech—congeals around the promise that refinement protects one from stigma. Disgust, in this ecology, is not only a sensory recoil but a cultural reflex trained to recognize “out of place” matter and “out of order” persons. That training has intimate consequences. Families become hygiene institutions. Schools become laboratories of comportment. Gender norms frequently intensify the demand for bodily propriety, especially where female virtue becomes a proxy for family honor. Even when people reject explicit moralism, they often inherit its micro-techniques: what should not be said aloud, what should not be shown, what must be cleaned before one is “presentable.” These are not abstract structures; they are lived scripts that organize what can be erotic without becoming shame.

When kink scenes mobilize “dirty” aesthetics within East Asian contexts, they therefore collide with a thick inheritance: the learned equivalence between cleanliness and worth. This does not mean that East Asians cannot eroticize disgust; it means that the eroticization is often negotiated against a strong background of propriety. A scene’s intensity can come partly from violating that background, and the post-scene residue can come partly from the same source. The key variable is whether the scene is able to mark the violation as bounded and reversible, or whether the violation is experienced as a revelation of “what I really am.” In other words, the local thickness of purity regimes changes the stability of symbolic distance: the more a culture binds “clean” to “good person,” the more easily “dirty” collapses from playful sign into self-judgment.

The Asian American case adds a second layer that is not simply “more of the same.” Asian Americans inherit, variably and unevenly, East Asian respectability scripts shaped by hygienic modernity. But they also live inside a US racial order in which “unclean” talk has historically functioned as a sorting technology: a way to police boundaries of intimacy, space, and citizenship. “Dirty” in the US has long been available as a racial predicate, a language of contagion that can attach to bodies and neighborhoods and then masquerade as common sense. For Asian Americans, this produces a distinctive double bind. On the one hand, respectability politics encourages an investment in cleanliness, propriety, and achievement as shields against stigma. On the other hand, racialization can make those shields feel fragile: no amount of cultivated cleanliness guarantees immunity from being read through stereotypes of foreignness, impurity, or threat. The result is an especially charged relationship to “dirty” language. It can feel like a violation of family discipline and like an invitation to external racial contempt at once.

This double bind matters for erotic disgust and humiliation because it changes how the speech act “dirty” lands. In many BDSM scenes, dirtying language works as a controllable intensifier precisely because it can be held as theatrical: a role token that heightens boundary and status play without asserting a lasting truth about the person. For Asian Americans, some dirtying lexicons are structurally less theatrical because they arrive already saturated by public history. The words can recruit an imagined hostile audience more quickly, and humiliation can therefore become more literalized: “I am dirty” begins to sound like “I am the kind of person the world already wants to treat as dirty.” When that happens, the scene’s hierarchy ceases to be a local arrangement and becomes a rehearsal of social sorting. The erotic charge may spike, but so can the risk of leakage—shame residue, mistrust, or the feeling that the scene has made the stereotype real.

Class intensifies this dynamic. Asian American respectability is not only about race; it is also about class mobility and its performance. Cleanliness becomes a visible credential of belonging: the tidy home, the controlled body, the correct speech, the “proper” relationship. In such a regime, dirty play can be thrilling because it suspends the performance of being acceptable. But it can also be terrifying because it threatens to undo the performance on which safety is thought to depend. This is where humiliation (status-work) and disgust (boundary-work) braid most tightly. “Dirty” does not merely mean contaminated; it can mean low, vulgar, disreputable—precisely the traits respectability seeks to avoid. The erotic scene becomes a small theater where the subject tries, briefly, to stop auditioning for acceptability. The outcome depends on whether the scene can credibly promise a return: not a return to repression, but a return to personhood without stigma.

This micro-historiography therefore reframes the Asian Americans versus East Asians comparison. In East Asia, “dirty” often draws its force from dense local purity economies and from the moral-aesthetic pedagogy of hygienic modernity. In Asian America, “dirty” is more likely to be doubled: it is bound to inherited propriety scripts and to a racialized public discourse that can interpret impurity as foreignness or threat. The practical consequence for kink cultures is not a simple increase in shame or a simple decrease in transgression. It is a change in the conditions under which transgression can remain play. Symbolic distance becomes more fragile for certain lexicons and scenarios, and closure practices must do more work to restore the self as socially intact. This is one reason that discourse about consent, safety, and aftercare can function as more than individual ethics in diaspora communities: it becomes counter-stigma infrastructure, a way to insist that boundary-play is not a confession of worthlessness and that humiliation is not a license for dehumanization.

The next section turns from history to language as the hinge where these dynamics become immediate. If “dirty” is a historically engineered predicate, then dirty talk is not merely descriptive spice. It is a performative act that can either preserve theatrical distance or collapse it, and in Asian American contexts the collapse is more likely when the lexicon aligns with ambient racial-sexual scripts. The question is not whether one can eroticize disgust under such conditions—one can—but what kinds of frames, metaphors, and repairs are required to prevent history from silently becoming the third participant in the scene.

## 6\. Dirty talk as speech act: stickiness, trope selection, and bilingual code-switching (髒／污／穢)

The preceding sections argued that “dirty” is never only sensory and never only private: it is a historically loaded predicate that can be eroticized precisely because it already does social work. Language is where that work becomes immediate. Dirty talk is not merely a commentary on arousal; it is a technology for producing persons and relations in real time. When someone is named as dirty, unclean, filthy, or polluted, the utterance does more than describe a state. It positions the target within a moral-aesthetic order, recruits an audience (often imagined), and sets up a hierarchy of who may touch and who must be cleaned. In kink scenes, this performativity is harnessed as craft. The words are chosen to intensify boundary cues (disgust) and status cues (humiliation), and then—if the scene is well-contained—to be dissolved back into ordinary personhood. The key question is therefore not “what words are used,” but “how do words stick”: how do they attach to identity, migrate across contexts, and become hard to shake off after the scene ends?

One way to clarify this is to distinguish generic filth metaphors from historically loaded dirtying tropes. Generic metaphors—filthy, nasty, disgusting, dirty—often operate as portable intensifiers. They can be exaggerated, stylized, and nested inside an explicit role frame (“this is play”), allowing the utterance to function like ritual language: it heightens the scene’s edge while remaining comparatively easy to “close” afterward. Historically loaded tropes, by contrast, arrive with a prior audience built in. They import an archive of social sorting—class contempt, racial dehumanization, sexual shaming—and therefore reduce the plausibility that the utterance is purely local. The scene has to do more framing work to keep these tropes from becoming literal. The risk is not only offense in the moment; it is the collapse of symbolic distance, where the listener’s mind treats the trope as an externally validated judgment rather than as a negotiated token. When that collapse happens, disgust and humiliation braid tightly: “dirty” becomes not only boundary threat but personhood devaluation.

This distinction is especially sharp in Asian American contexts because “dirty” has often been used against Asians as a boundary predicate—foreignness, contagion, unclean food, unclean bodies—while Asian American respectability politics has often demanded intensified cleanliness as a form of protection. In such a setting, even generic filth metaphors can become less portable if they align too neatly with ambient racial scripts; the words can summon the public gaze faster. Conversely, some participants may find that eroticizing “dirty” precisely in this context produces an intense thrill of defiance—an interval in which the respectability performance is suspended. Both outcomes are possible because dirty talk is not a chemical stimulus; it is an indexical act whose meaning depends on which social worlds it points to. When the index points outward to a hostile history, stickiness increases.

Bilingual code-switching makes this indexicality even more complex, and it cannot be treated as mere translation. In Asian American kink scenes, switching between English and Chinese (or other heritage languages) can change dirty talk’s force in at least three ways: semantic shading, social audience, and moral register. Consider the Chinese cluster 髒／污／穢. On the surface, all can be rendered as “dirty” or “impure,” but they do not land identically. 髒 tends to be concrete and everyday, closely tied to physical dirtiness and mess. 污 often carries a stronger moral taint—污穢, 污辱, 污名—suggesting contamination that stains reputation as much as body. 穢, especially in compounds like 穢物 and 污穢, can carry a more formal or classical register, evoking impurity that is not merely messy but defiling. When an English-speaking scene slips into 髒, it may feel like a tactile, almost childish dirtiness, intensifying boundary play while staying relatively domestic. When it slips into 污 or 穢, it may thicken into reputational defilement, inviting humiliation to ride on top of disgust. The stickiness of the utterance changes because the moral register changes.

Code-switching also changes the implied audience. English dirty talk in US kink spaces often points to a subcultural genre—pornographic scripts, leather scenes, dungeon protocols—where filth words are recognizable as play. Switching into Chinese can suddenly point elsewhere: to family discipline, to intergenerational propriety, to schoolroom scolding, to the voice of an aunt or parent whose disgust is not theatrical. That shift can increase intensity by importing taboo from the domestic sphere, but it can also increase residue because the scene is now borrowing from a disciplinary archive that was designed to shape the child into a respectable person. In this sense, bilingual dirty talk can function as a deliberate manipulation of symbolic distance: English may preserve “as-if” theatricality, while Chinese can collapse it into “this is the voice of real judgment.” Some couples may use that collapse intentionally, then repair it explicitly in closure; others may stumble into it and find that the words keep echoing after the scene.

A third effect is re-racialization. In some contexts, switching into Chinese may feel like reclaiming an intimate idiom, making the scene less legible to the dominant gaze and therefore safer. In other contexts, it can heighten racial legibility by activating stereotyped fantasies of “Asian” sexuality—especially if the code-switching is performed for an audience or aligned with orientalist tropes. The same linguistic move can therefore either buffer stickiness (by privatizing meaning within a shared heritage frame) or amplify it (by aligning the scene with externally imposed scripts). The difference depends on who the scene is for, whether there is an audience, and whether the words are treated as genre play or as identity truths. Code-switching is not a neutral stylistic flourish; it is a lever that shifts the scene’s semiotic environment.

These dynamics complicate any simple ethics of “avoid slurs” versus “anything consensual goes.” The more precise claim is that different lexicons have different default stickiness, and bilingual lexicons can change stickiness abruptly by changing register, audience, and moral freight. Generic filth metaphors tend to be easier to metabolize because they are more easily bracketed as genre tokens. Historically racialized/classed tropes, and heritage-language terms that activate domestic discipline or reputational taint, require more explicit framing if they are to remain bounded. Closure then becomes more than comfort: it is a linguistic counter-spell. Re-naming, praise, and ordinary speech reassert the person outside the predicate. In Asian American contexts, this may include deliberately switching back to English (or deliberately staying in Chinese) as a way of choosing which audience is being invoked in the return phase.

The upshot is that “dirty talk” is best treated as a micro-ritual of naming. It produces a temporary ontology—who is clean, who is dirty, who commands, who is lowered—and then it must be dissolved if the scene is to end cleanly. Bilingual code-switching sharpens both the power and the danger of this ritual because it can summon different moral worlds with a single syllable. That is why the terms 髒／污／穢 are not interchangeable decorations: they are switches that route the scene through different histories of impurity, respectability, and stigma. For a paper that compares Asian Americans with East Asians, language provides the most concrete point of contact between macro-history and micro-phenomenology: it is where hygienic modernity, racialization, and intimacy collide, and where “dirty” becomes either an erotic edge or a sticky social verdict.

## 7\. Discussion: non-reification as containment, and the politics of “dirty” as a portable predicate

The preceding sections have built an argument that tries to be simultaneously modest and ambitious. Modest, because it refuses equivalence: Daoist and Buddhist traditions are not recoded as kink manuals, and kink is not recoded as disguised religion. Ambitious, because it claims that a shared structural problem—how evaluative categories become sticky truths—can be analyzed across these domains using a common translation grammar. The payoff is a way to theorize eroticized disgust and humiliation that does not collapse into either privatized psychology (“taste”) or moralized sociology (“deviance”). Instead, it treats scenes as micro-rituals where boundary-work and status-work are intensified under frames that either succeed or fail at containing meaning.

At the center of that containment problem sits Symbolic Distance (S). In the paper’s sociological idiom, S names the durability of the “as-if” bracket: whether a dirtying sign or degrading speech act remains a negotiated role token or becomes a literal claim about who the person is. In the paper’s comparative-philosophical idiom, S is a proxy for non-reification: the capacity to not take 榮辱 or 淨/穢 as ultimate truths of the self. The temptation is to treat this as merely a psychological skill, but the analysis suggests it is better understood as a social achievement supported by infrastructures—scripts, privacy, community norms, closure rituals, and lexicons that can be metabolized without importing hostile publics. In other words, S is not a private attitude floating free of history; it is a fragile relational property of scenes embedded in unequal social worlds.

The Daoist modules clarify why humiliation is so volatile as erotic material. Philosophical 道家 reveals humiliation’s core mechanism as valuation capture: the self becomes hostage to rank and fear because it takes external valuation as a stable measure. Humiliation play can therefore function in two very different registers that look similar from the outside. In one register, hierarchy is instrumentally thin: it is intense, even overwhelming, but it is held as contingent. The scene becomes a controlled encounter with the contingency of face, and the return from the scene reasserts personhood without residue. In the other register, hierarchy is metaphysically thick: it is not merely enacted but believed. Here humiliation is not a role token but a truth claim, and the scene risks producing shame residue and relational contamination—less as a moral punishment than as an effect of taking valuation seriously as ontology. Religious 道教 complements this by clarifying how disgust is governed when boundary-crossing is treated as real and consequential. Even if one rejects the cosmology, the form remains sociologically instructive: preparation, sequence, authorized roles, and restoration are technologies for keeping pollution from diffusing. In kink terms, this is a reminder that the management of “dirty” is never only about what happens in the moment; it is about how the scene is entered and how it is exited, how the ecology of boundaries is restored so that “dirty” does not become a permanent descriptor of the person.

The Buddhist module sharpens the internal tension in impurity work. Strategy A (asubha) is re-categorization: it modifies perception so that the object loses its power to command attachment and aversion. It makes disgust legible as trainable and as meaningful only within a particular interpretive regime. Strategy B (方便/upāya) is inversion: it uses impurity motifs to destabilize the binary itself, insisting that the category “impure” can be instrumentalized toward liberation under strict constraint. What matters here is not to import tantric rhetoric into kink discourse, but to treat these strategies as two different logics of how impurity can be handled. Re-categorization tends to cool by changing what bodies are taken to be; inversion tends to heat by bringing taboo into proximity under strong framing. The danger that both strategies illuminate is not impurity per se but misrecognition: when the mind treats the category as ultimate, either by hardening aversion (re-categorization without compassion) or by hardening pride (inversion as identity). Translating this into the scene grammar, misrecognition is the collapse of S: the “dirty” sign stops being an instrument and becomes a verdict.

The Asian American versus East Asian contrast then functions as the stress test that forces this abstract hinge into concrete sociology. In East Asian contexts shaped by hygienic modernity and respectability scripts, purity economies are thick but locally organized; they discipline bodies as cultivated personhood and tie cleanliness to classed self-control. In Asian American contexts, those inherited scripts collide with a US racial order in which “unclean” talk has operated as boundary policing at the level of the public. The result is a distinctive overdetermination of “dirty” as a predicate: it can simultaneously invoke domestic discipline and external racialization, simultaneously threaten face and trigger contamination alarm. This double audience changes the stability of symbolic distance. The scene is not only negotiating between two partners; it is negotiating with the imagined publics that words summon. That is why the paper treats language as the hinge where macro-history becomes micro-affect.

Dirty talk, in this view, is a speech-act ritual: it produces a temporary ontology of clean/dirty and high/low, then demands dissolution if the scene is to end cleanly. The central theoretical contribution of the bilingual module is that code-switching is not translation; it is routing. Switching between English and terms like 髒／污／穢 changes the implied audience, the moral register, and the direction of the predicate’s force. 髒 can land as tactile mess; 污 and 穢 can thicken into defilement that stains reputation, making humiliation ride on top of disgust. In Asian American contexts, heritage-language terms can pull the scene toward domestic moral worlds—family discipline, propriety, the voice of “real judgment”—and therefore alter the default stickiness of the utterance. Code-switching can buffer stigma by privatizing meaning within an in-group idiom, or it can amplify stigma by aligning the scene with orientalist fantasies or by importing disciplinary archives designed to produce respectability. The same linguistic move can thus either stabilize S (as a private genre token) or collapse S (as a literalized verdict), depending on which audience it calls into being.

This reframes a debate that often appears in kink theory as a moral standoff between “anything consensual” and “some words are always harmful.” The framework proposed here suggests a third position: predicates differ in default stickiness because they differ in historical charge and in the audiences they recruit. Consent is necessary, but it is not sufficient to guarantee non-sticky meaning, because consent does not control the social archive that a word carries. The analytic task is therefore not to ban impurity language, but to theorize how impurity language becomes either a bounded intensifier or a stigma amplifier. In the grammar of this paper, the question is how D and H are coupled by speech acts, how S is stabilized or eroded by trope selection and audience, and how R remains orthogonal even when symbolism is powerful. This is also where the two Daoisms and two Buddhist strategies converge: they supply different vocabularies for the same hinge problem—how to encounter potent predicates without taking them as ultimate truths.

The final implication is methodological. Eroticized disgust and humiliation should be treated as sites where social theory can be tested at high resolution. Purity/pollution frameworks become visible not in abstract moral discourse but in breath-level speech. Status and face become visible not in institutions alone but in the tremor of exposure. Diaspora histories become visible not in census tables but in which word—dirty, 髒, 污, 穢—feels “too real” to say. A hybrid venue paper can therefore claim a genuine contribution here: it shows how comparative philosophy can function not as cultural ornament but as a theory of category stickiness, and how sexuality studies can be enriched by treating scenes as micro-rituals where reification and containment are enacted, contested, and repaired. The question is not whether disgust and humiliation can be erotic. The question is when the eroticization loosens the grip of purity and rank, and when it merely rehearses them with the lights turned low.

---

This paper’s translation thesis is that eroticized disgust and humiliation become analytically commensurable across sexuality studies, cultural sociology, and 道家／佛教 thought when we treat scenes as micro-rituals that couple boundary-work and status-work (D/H) while negotiating the social conditions of non-reification—symbolic distance (S)—against the irreducible orthogonality of objective hazard (R). In that frame, “dirty” is neither a private kink flavor nor a timeless moral reflex but a portable, historically charged predicate whose stickiness is governed by audience, code-switching (髒／污／穢), and the success or failure of containment and return, so that what looks like transgression can either loosen the grip of purity and rank or quietly rehearse their verdicts in the most intimate register.
