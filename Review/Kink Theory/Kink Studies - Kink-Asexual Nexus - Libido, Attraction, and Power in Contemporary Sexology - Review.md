## Abstract
### English
The clinical and sociological landscape of asexuality has undergone a paradigm shift into 2026, transitioning from a behavioral deficit model to an attraction-based framework. This review explores the complex intersection of high autosexual libido and the lack of interpersonal sexual attraction, specifically through the lenses of **Aegosexuality** and the **Split Attraction Model (SAM)**. By examining "Side" identities within Men Loving Men (MLM) populations, the article identifies the "Role Gap"—a phenomenon where physiological arousal is functionally dependent on archetypal power dynamics (TPE/Bondage) rather than interpersonal attraction. Furthermore, the analysis addresses the "Neuro-Ace" phenotype, linking sensory processing sensitivity and executive dysfunction to asexual-spectrum (Ace-spec) aversions. This article proposes an Integrated Model of Asexual Health to differentiate primary asexual orientation from acquired sexual aversion, advocating for neuro-affirming and orientation-positive clinical protocols.

## Section 1: The Shifting Paradigm of Asexuality
The clinical and sociological understanding of asexuality has undergone a profound transformation over the last two decades, moving from a marginal behavioral observation to a recognized, multifaceted sexual orientation. Historically, asexuality was often conflated with Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder (HSDD) or sexual aversion, viewed primarily through the lens of pathology or deficit. However, the contemporary 2026 paradigm recognizes asexuality—and the broader Asexual Spectrum (Ace-spec)—as a stable identity characterized not by the absence of sexual behavior, but by the lack of interpersonal sexual attraction.

The watershed moment for this shift was the establishment of the attraction-based model by Bogaert (2004). Prior to this, clinical assessments prioritized "socio-sexual contacts" or the frequency of coitus as the primary metrics for sexual orientation. Bogaert’s research reframed asexuality as a subjective internal state, defining it as an individual who "has never felt sexually attracted to anyone" (Bogaert, 2004, p. 281). This distinction is critical in 2026 sexology, as it decouples physical capability and libido from the target-oriented nature of sexual desire.

## Section 2: The Libido-Attraction Divergence
A critical point of clinical confusion in the diagnosis of asexual-spectrum (Ace-spec) identities is the persistence of high sexual drive, or libido, in the absence of interpersonal sexual attraction. Contemporary 2026 sexology treats libido as a biological "appetite" or "engine" that is neurobiologically distinct from the "navigation" or "target" provided by sexual attraction. This independence is most clearly demonstrated in populations that report daily autosexual behaviors while simultaneously expressing profound "boredom" or aversions toward shared sexual mechanics (Prause & Graham, 2007).

Empirical research by Yule, Brotto, and Gorzalka (2014) established that a significant majority of asexual-identified individuals—particularly males—maintain a functional and active libido. Their study found that approximately 73.9% of asexual men engage in regular masturbation, a frequency that does not statistically differ from many allosexual cohorts (Yule et al., 2014). This data refutes the outdated clinical assumption that asexuality is synonymous with low libido or "sex-drive deficit."

## Section 3: The Split Attraction Model (SAM) in MLM Contexts
The Split Attraction Model (SAM) represents a critical theoretical framework in 2026 for distinguishing between sexual attraction (the drive for sexual contact with a specific target) and romantic attraction (the desire for emotional intimacy and pair-bonding). While traditional "allonormative" models assume these axes are inherently aligned, contemporary research within Men Loving Men (MLM) subcultures reveals a significant prevalence of divergent orientations. In these cases, an individual may seek the domestic and emotional benefits of a "boyfriend" while maintaining a sexual orientation within the asexual spectrum (Miller & Behm-Morawitz, 2016).

In the 2026 clinical landscape, romantic attraction is analyzed through the lens of attachment theory and the neurobiology of pair-bonding (oxytocin/vasopressin), whereas sexual attraction is tied to incentive salience and the dopaminergic reward system (Bulmer & Izuma, 2018). For the homoromantic asexual, the drive for a partner is motivated by companionship, shared life goals, and non-sexual physical intimacy, which remains distinct from the "boredom" or sensory aversion experienced during standard sexual mechanics.

## Section 4: Paraphilic Functional Dependency and the Role Gap
In the clinical evaluation of individuals who identify on the asexual spectrum (Ace-spec) yet engage in high-intensity kink, the concept of Paraphilic Functional Dependency provides a vital explanatory framework. This phenomenon occurs when physiological sexual response—specifically tumescence and the ability to achieve orgasm—is not triggered by the physical presence of a partner, but is instead functionally dependent on a Cognitive Script or power dynamic. In these instances, kink acts as a "bridge" or "scaffold" that facilitates biological function where interpersonal attraction is absent (Morgenthaler et al., 2020).

The "Role Gap" refers to the divergence between Subjective Attraction and Archetypal Attraction. While allonormative sexual response is typically driven by an attraction to the "Subject" (the unique identity and physical traits of the partner), individuals with a Role Gap experience arousal directed toward the "Object" or "Archetype" (the role the partner inhabits within a script). According to Bulmer & Izuma (2018), this can be traced to a lack of incentive salience for human anatomy, where the brain's reward system remains dormant during standard sexual cues but responds vigorously to the psychological stimuli of Total Power Exchange (TPE) or bondage.

## Section 5: The Neuro-Sensory Intersection in Sexual Response
The Neuro-Sensory Intersection represents a burgeoning field of study in 2026, exploring the significant correlation between neurodivergent cognitive profiles—specifically Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)—and asexual-spectrum (Ace-spec) identities. Termed the "Neu-Ace" phenotype, this intersection suggests that for many individuals, asexuality is not merely an absence of attraction, but a byproduct of distinct Sensory Processing Sensitivity (SPS) and executive functioning patterns (George & Stokes, 2018).

A primary component of the Neu-Ace profile is Tactile Hypersensitivity. In standard "allonormative" sexual mechanics, the intensity of skin-on-skin contact, fluid exchange, and unpredictable physical touch can trigger a sympathetic nervous system "fight or flight" response rather than parasympathetic arousal. Research by Aron et al. (2012) identifies that individuals with high SPS process sensory input more deeply, often leading to a state of sensory overwhelm that is clinically mislabeled as "germaphobia" or "hypoactive desire."

## Section 6: Etiology of Sexual Aversion: Primary vs. Secondary
In the diagnostic landscape of 2026, the etiological distinction between Primary (Lifelong) and Secondary (Acquired) sexual aversion is the mandatory baseline for differentiating between an innate asexual orientation and a reactive sexual dysfunction. This distinction relies heavily on longitudinal stability and the psychological framework of Egosyntonicity—the degree to which the individual’s sexual boundaries align with their self-concept and values (APA, 2022).

Primary aversion refers to a lack of sexual interest or a profound aversion to specific sexual mechanics that has been consistently present since the onset of sexual maturity. Within the asexual spectrum (Ace-spec), these aversions are typically categorized as Egosyntonic; the individual does not view their lack of interest as a "deficit" to be corrected, but as a stable component of their orientation. The DSM-5-TR (2022) explicitly codified this distinction by introducing the "Asexuality Exclusion Clause" for Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder (HSDD).

## Section 7: Sociological Identity Markers – The "Side" and Subcultural Integration
In the sociological landscape of 2026, the emergence of the "Side" identity represents a significant shift in how sexual boundaries are codified within Men Loving Men (MLM) and asexual-spectrum (Ace-spec) subcultures. A "Side" is defined as an individual who enjoys various forms of sexual intimacy (cuddling, kissing, manual stimulation) but explicitly rejects penetrative acts (anal sex). While originally a niche label within the gay community, the "Side" marker has become a critical sociological bridge for homoromantic asexuals navigating allonormative dating markets (Morgenthaler et al., 2020).

Data from Hall et al. (2024) indicate that nearly 42% of self-identified "Sides" also identify with some portion of the asexual spectrum, suggesting that the rejection of penetration is frequently an etiological marker of a lack of intrinsic sexual attraction rather than a mere preference. Sociological analysis reveals that BDSM and Kink communities often serve as "Safe Harbors" for asexual individuals, prioritizing negotiated consent and scripted play over spontaneous physical attraction.

## Section 8: Conclusion – Future Directions for Clinical Practice
The evolution of asexual-spectrum (Ace-spec) research into 2026 necessitates a paradigm shift in clinical practice, transitioning from diagnostic differentiation to Orientation-Positive Care. Future clinical efforts must prioritize the stabilization of these diverse identities within a biopsychosocial framework that recognizes "non-standard" arousal patterns as valid functional variations rather than symptomatic deficits (Bogaert, 2025).

A primary future direction is the formal integration of Neuro-Affirming Sex Therapy. Protocols should focus on sensory environment optimization and the validation of Instrumental Sex. This approach acknowledges that for many Ace-spec individuals, the goal of sexual activity is physiological tension release via predictable stimuli rather than interpersonal "merging." By centering the subjective experience of asexuality, sexology moves toward a more inclusive understanding of human intimacy.
