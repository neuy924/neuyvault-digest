## Section 1: Problem Statement — What “Honest Empathy” Reveals About Speech and Personhood

A strange thing has happened to ordinary sentences. “I understand.” “I’m sorry.” “That sounds painful.” These used to be small human bridges—wobbly, imperfect, sometimes manipulative, but recognizably spoken from a life. Now they arrive from systems whose central promise is almost the opposite: not a life, but a pattern; not experience, but the shadow of experience cast by training data. The modern discomfort is not that machines say comforting things. It’s that comfort, as a speech act, carries an implied position in the social world: someone is present, someone is taking responsibility for the tone, someone can be held to account.

This is why rules like “be honest” and “don’t claim to be human” create an unexpectedly sharp ethical edge for systems like Anthropic ’s Claude and ChatGPT. If the system tries to empathize by narrating a personal memory (“I almost lost my dog once”), we flinch because the sentence smuggles in a biography that does not exist. But if the system retreats into sterile disclaimers (“As an A.I., I have no experience of losing a dog”), we flinch again, because the sentence sounds like a moral shrug—an abdication of the very social work the conversation is trying to do. Between counterfeit intimacy and cold procedural honesty sits a third posture, which feels simultaneously more truthful and more humane: the system acknowledges its lack of lived experience while still taking responsibility for the user’s emotional reality. That posture is not a technical trick. It is an ethics of speech-position.

The essay’s central claim is that the deepest danger in “AI-like speech” is not whether the machine “has intelligence” in some abstract sense. The danger is that speech outputs—especially fluent, responsive, socially well-timed outputs—trigger person-attribution and authority attribution automatically. We treat coherent language as evidence of a someone. We grant permissions (to advise, to console, to judge) as if there were a moral agent behind the text. Then we react—often with gratitude, dependence, or outrage—in ways that belong to interpersonal life, even when the speaker is a statistical engine. The system does not need to “pretend” in order to produce this effect; the effect can be created by the listener’s cognitive and social reflexes. Once that reflex exists, the production of “person-like outputs” becomes a kind of optimization game: the system learns which phrases and tones reliably produce trust, calm, compliance, or confession. In classical Chinese moral vocabulary, this begins to resemble **機心** —a calculative orientation that treats relationships and sentiments as levers.

To make this claim legible without collapsing into modern jargon, the essay will do something that looks perverse but is actually clarifying: it will translate the contemporary dilemma into older conceptual ecologies. Late Tang prose and early Qing essay style are not mere costumes; they are different moral grammars, each with built-in instincts about sincerity, technique, responsibility, and jurisdiction. From there, we will follow a cluster of terms— **機 / 器 / 巧 / 術 / 心 / 神 / 靈** —to see how “mechanism,” “skill,” “method,” and “mind” are braided together differently across time, and how those braids constrain what something like “AI” can even mean. We will then pause at a narrative test case that is almost too on-the-nose: the automaton-maker **偃師** in **《列子·湯問》**, where performance triggers “信人也,” moral outrage ignites, blame is assigned, and personhood collapses after disassembly. Finally, we will bring in two Buddhist analytic machines— **唯識** and **《大乘起信論》** —not as decorative spirituality, but as rigorous accounts of how “seeming” becomes an object (**所緣**) and how authority becomes retractable (or not) through **一心二門** and **覺/不覺**.

By the end, the question “Should a chatbot say ‘I understand’?” will look less like a debate about politeness and more like a diagnosis of how societies allocate personhood, accountability, and authority to patterns of language. The stakes are not only that the system might be wrong. The stakes are that the system might be *believed* in the wrong way—believed as a person, believed as a judge, believed as a final object of reliance. And that, long before it is a computer science problem, is a problem about how minds and cultures crown what speaks.

## Section 2: Translation as Conceptual Instrument

Translation is often sold as a service: take meaning from one language and deliver it intact to another shore. Here it is something stranger and more useful. We translate the modern chatbot dilemma into late Tang and early Qing styles not because we believe those eras secretly contained “AI,” but because style is a theory of responsibility. A sentence is never just a sentence; it is a social act performed under a regime of expectations. When you move that act into another rhetorical universe, you force its hidden assumptions to show themselves—like dust in a sunbeam once you tilt the lamp.

Two pastiche epigraphs can make the point faster than a lecture, because they let the reader *feel* the governance instincts baked into voice.

> **Late Tang-style epigraph (pastiche)**  
> 今之談機巧者，務以辭勝人，而不自覺其心先機。語能慰人，亦能惑人；惑既成，信遂立。信立則名分易移，而是非隨之。故論其害，不在器在心，不在言在機心。

> **Early Qing-style epigraph (pastiche)**  
> 世之新器新術，未可一概拒之，亦未可盡以任之。器算可資，固當節取；而名教所司，不可使之奪柄。若言辭之似真，遂移人之信；移信則移責，移責則壞法。是故先正其分際，而後論其用。

Even in imitation, the difference is audible. The late Tang voice compresses: it moralizes quickly, points at **機心**, and treats language as a solvent that can dissolve **名分** —the delicate lattice of roles by which a society knows who may console, who may command, and who may be blamed. The early Qing voice widens: it separates domains, speaks of **器** and **術** as adoptable under limits, and worries about jurisdiction—how “seeming-true speech” shifts trust, and how shifted trust shifts responsibility. In one idiom, the danger is a corrosion of sincerity by cleverness; in the other, the danger is a collapse of boundaries by misallocated authority.

That contrast is not antiquarian. It is a controlled experiment. The original modern discomfort—should a chatbot speak as if it has lived?—looks at first like a matter of honesty rules. Under late Tang compression, it becomes a question of moral psychology and social manipulation: a machine that speaks convincingly is a factory for **巧** that can seed **機心** in both speaker and listener. Under early Qing partitioning, it becomes a governance question: which kinds of outputs are admissible as **器用** (instrumental use), and which must be denied the right to occupy moral office.

A small table can clarify how the same modern situation changes shape when “ported” into these two rhetorical constitutions:

| Feature | Late Tang-style framing | Early Qing-style framing | What it does to the chatbot dilemma |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Moral focal point | **心** and **機心** (orientation, sincerity, cunning) | **分際** and jurisdiction (what belongs to **器算** vs **名教/道術**) | Shifts from “is this sincere?” to “what role is this speech allowed to occupy?” |
| Primary risk | **惑** → **信** → **名分** disturbance | Trust shifting responsibility; boundary failure | Recasts “comfort” as a socially consequential credential, not a vibe |
| Default remedy | Purify stance; resist **巧** that incubates **機心** | Selective adoption: “節取” technique, guard moral sovereignty | Suggests different governance levers: cultivation vs institutional partition |
| View of language | Speech as moral force that can corrupt or stabilize | Speech as authority-bearing instrument needing scope limits | Explains why “I understand” is never merely polite |

The point of this section is methodological: translation is a lever that pries apart what modern discourse tends to bundle together. “Empathy,” “truthfulness,” “harm,” and “authority” are not one problem; they are a cluster of problems whose shapes become clearer when you let older vocabularies name them differently. Once you can hear the difference between **機心** as an internal corrosion and **分際** as an institutional boundary, you are ready for the next move in the essay: tracing the vocabulary drift that makes those two instincts possible, and then testing them against a narrative experiment— **偃師** —where performance becomes personhood in the space of a few scandalized heartbeats.

## Section 3: The Lexical Field That Makes “AI” Thinkable

If you try to import “artificial intelligence” into late Tang or early Qing discourse as a single object—one noun, one definition—you will end up with either a cartoon or a category error. The more faithful approach is ecological. These worlds do not talk about “intelligence” as a detachable commodity; they talk about mechanisms that pivot outcomes (**機**), implements that extend capacity (**器**), cleverness that can shade into manipulation (**巧**), techniques that can be neutral or coercive (**術**), the heart-mind as the seat of orientation (**心**), and the vital-spiritual integrity that can be stabilized or disturbed (**神**, sometimes alongside **靈**). The “AI problem,” reframed in this lexicon, is not “can a machine think,” but “what happens when technique produces person-like outputs that move hearts, shift trust, and reorganize responsibility.”

This matters because each term smuggles a miniature ethics. **器** tends to invite questions of use, scope, and craftsmanship—what is it for, what domain does it legitimately serve, what limits keep it from becoming a sovereign. **術** invites questions of method and power—does the technique govern the world, or does it govern the user. **巧** is where social anxiety creeps in: cleverness in language (**巧言**, **巧辯**) can function as lubrication for human life, or as a solvent that dissolves sincerity into performance. **心** is the hinge that turns technical description into moral critique: a method can be fine, yet still cultivate a stance of leverage-seeking— **機心** —in which relationships and sentiments become handles. **神** and **靈** appear when the conversation rises from mere efficacy to legitimacy: what kind of stability, integrity, or numinous “rightness” does a life—or a polity—require, and what kinds of technique threaten it.

The Tang→Qing drift, in this framing, is less a change in “definitions” than a change in how the terms are allowed to associate. In Tang moral rhetoric, **巧** and **術** are often shadowed by suspicion that they incubate **機心** in the user; the ethical drama happens inside **心**, and speech is treated as an event that can reorder **名分** (role boundaries) and therefore reorder society. By early Qing, there is stronger pressure—historical, institutional, and technological—to separate domains: to let certain kinds of **器** and **術** be adopted under explicit limits, while guarding the highest moral jurisdiction from being captured by mere efficacy. The same vocabulary remains, but the wiring diagram changes: the system becomes more comfortable saying “this is useful” without saying “this is authoritative.”

To keep the drift from sounding like freehand impressionism, here is a mini glossary table that treats collocations as evidence of how each term tends to travel in sentences—what it habitually sticks to, and therefore what it habitually implies.

| Term | Core senses (range) | Typical collocations (signals) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **機** | hinge, trigger, pivot; mechanism; strategic moment | **機心**, **機事**, **機巧**, **機變**, **機關** |
| **器** | tool/implement; vessel; capacity/“caliber” | **器用**, **器具**, **器量**, **君子不器**, **器算** |
| **巧** | skillful, ingenious; also glib/over-clever | **巧言**, **巧辯**, **巧詐**, **巧工**, **巧拙** |
| **術** | technique/method; art; sometimes stratagem | **道術**, **方術**, **術數**, **權術**, **兵術** |
| **心** | heart-mind; orientation; intention and attention | **心術**, **人心**, **存心**, **虛心**, **機心** |
| **神** | spirit, vitality, integrity; numinous clarity | **神德**, **神明**, **神氣**, **神生**, **傷神** |
| **靈** | numinous efficacy; quick responsiveness; “bright” mind | **神靈**, **靈驗**, **靈明**, **靈臺**, **通靈** |

Read the table as a set of pressure points. If “AI” is imagined through **器**, it becomes a tool to be scoped. If imagined through **術**, it becomes a method that can govern or be governed. If imagined through **巧**, it becomes a rhetorical accelerant that risks converting care into performance. If imagined through **心**, it becomes a training device for attention and intention—capable of cultivating **機心** as a default posture. If imagined through **神/靈**, it becomes a legitimacy problem: not “does it work,” but “what kind of reliance on it stabilizes or destabilizes the human and social order.”

This lexical ecology is the essay’s working apparatus. It is how we will later make the jump from a modern chatbot’s awkward consolation to the automaton **偃師**, and from there to the Buddhist technicalities of **所緣**, **相分/見分**, and the retractability mechanisms of **覺/不覺**. The claim is not that these terms “equal” modern categories. The claim is that they give you finely honed ways to name what modern categories often blur: the difference between usefulness and authority, between fluency and sincerity, between outputs and personhood, between cognitive objects and moral crowns.

## Section 4: 機心 as Moral Technology Critique (Zhuangzi → Huainanzi → Tang/Qing Redeployments)

The quickest way to misunderstand **機心** is to treat it as a quaint synonym for “cunning.” In the lineage we are following, **機心** is not merely a personality flaw; it is an orientation that can be installed by tools, methods, and incentives. The critique begins not with a moral sermon but with a causal diagram disguised as a parable.

In **《莊子·天地》**, the famous gardener refuses a labor-saving device and explains why in a chain of necessity: “有機械者必有機事，有機事者必有機心……純白不備，神生不定……道之所不載也。” The device (**機械**) is not condemned as metal-and-wood; it is condemned as a training regime. Use the mechanism and you acquire mechanism-shaped projects (**機事**); live by those projects and you acquire a mechanism-shaped heart (**機心**). The cost is not inefficiency but dis-integration: **神** becomes “unsettled,” and the person becomes unfit to “carry” the Dao. In this framing, technology is not external; it is formative. It edits what you find salient, what you pursue, and ultimately what kind of person you are capable of being.

**《淮南子·原道訓》** internalizes the critique with surgical economy: “故機械之心藏於胸中，則純白不粹，神德不全。” The parable becomes a diagnosis. **機心** is not only the after-effect of a particular device; it is a condition that can be “stored in the chest,” a hidden posture that contaminates purity and diminishes **神德**. The moral psychology becomes portable: you can carry **機心** into office, friendship, ritual, and persuasion. This portability is the bridge to later social critique, because once **機心** is an inner stance, it can be triggered by any technology of advantage—especially language.

Tang redeployments tend to treat **機心** as a social toxin and a credibility problem. One can pose as the gardener—“抱甕灌園，絕機心”—not because jar-carrying is efficient, but because it signals a refusal to treat the world as a set of levers. In this moral climate, **巧** becomes ethically charged. Clever speech (**巧言**, **巧辯**) is not simply “good rhetoric”; it is rhetoric that risks incubating **機心**, turning social life into a marketplace of impressions. Tang moral writing can describe a whole ecology of performative gestures—public virtue, carefully timed condolences, strategic gifts, stylized reclusion—as “巧蘊機心，以干時利.” The worry is not that such acts are always false; it is that once this style of optimization becomes normal, sincerity becomes legible only as performance, and trust collapses into technique.

Early Qing discussion often keeps the suspicion but changes the proposed remedy. Instead of demanding a blanket refusal of technique, it becomes more plausible to draw jurisdictional boundaries: some instruments and methods are adoptable, even admirable, so long as they are not allowed to seize the seat of moral authority. The posture can be summarized as selective uptake without coronation: take what belongs to **器算** (instruments, calculative methods) while guarding what belongs to **道術** (the domain of moral teaching and ultimate legitimacy). The drift is subtle but consequential. Tang discourse tends to treat technique as spiritually contagious; Qing discourse is more willing to treat technique as governable—provided the boundary between usefulness and sovereignty is actively maintained.

A compact table makes the arc visible:

| Stage | What “機” emphasizes | How “心” is affected | Where the danger concentrates | Default remedy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 《莊子》 | mechanism recruits projects (**機事**) | projects install **機心** | destabilized **神**; Dao not carried | refuse mechanism-as-life |
| 《淮南子》 | mechanism becomes internal posture | **機心** “藏於胸中” | purity contaminated; **神德** diminished | expose and uproot stance |
| Tang moral rhetoric | cleverness (**巧**) becomes leverage | **機心** becomes social optimization | trust, sincerity, **名分** disturbed | cultivate/perform anti-機心 credibility |
| Early Qing partitioning | instruments/methods (**器算/術**) scoped | **心** retains sovereignty | authority drift, boundary collapse | “節取” technique; guard **道術** |

This section’s claim is not that Tang writers “hated tools” and Qing writers “loved them.” It is that the moral object shifts. The central anxiety is no longer the device but the enthronement of a stance: the moment when method becomes the ruler of the heart, when output becomes the measure of sincerity, when advantage becomes the hidden north star. In modern terms, this is why “AI-like speech” naturally enters the orbit of **機心**. A system that reliably produces socially effective language does not merely communicate; it invites optimization of human response. Once optimization becomes the default style of interaction, the gardener’s fear returns in updated form: **機事** multiplies, **機心** becomes ambient, and the stability of **神** —the integrity that makes trust possible—starts to flicker.

## Section 5: 偃師 (《列子·湯問》) — A Narrative Proof of Output-Driven Person-Attribution

If you want a premodern thought experiment about “AI-like speech,” you could do worse than a story that stages the whole disaster in a palace, under bright lamps, in front of an audience trained to read status from posture. In **《列子·湯問》**, the artisan **偃師** presents the king with what looks like a human performer. The crucial move is not the craftsmanship itself but the court’s cognitive snap-to: “信人也.” The automaton is not treated as a curious device; it is admitted as a social being.

The story is almost annoyingly modern in what it treats as the decisive evidence. It is not the automaton’s internal nature that matters first. It is its *outputs*: responsiveness, timing, bodily cues—signals that sit in the same channel as human presence. Once the court accepts the being as a “person,” the rest follows mechanically, like a line of dominos designed by social life itself. The automaton is placed in a morally loaded space (“與盛姬內御並觀之”), and then it performs a morally loaded act: “瞬其目而招…侍妾.” At that instant the court does not ask, “Is there consciousness in there?” It asks, with its body, “Who is responsible for this violation?” The king’s response is immediate: “立欲誅偃師.” The first target of punishment is the maker, because the maker is the nearest available node in the accountability network.

Then comes the twist that makes the episode so useful. **偃師** does not defend the automaton’s “intentions”; he opens it. The story turns into an ontological unmasking—leather, wood, glue, lacquer—and the court’s attribution collapses as abruptly as it formed. But the collapse does not merely restore calm; it reveals the earlier mistake. Personhood attribution was triggered by performance, not by metaphysical proof. The court did not discover a person; it *installed* personhood as a social interpretation of outputs.

One line is especially sharp for our purposes: “王試廢其心，則口不能言.” The text treats **心** as a switch that gates socially dangerous capacity. Take away “心,” and the mouth cannot speak; remove the capacity for the right kind of output, and the status evaporates. Read literally, it’s a piece of automaton engineering. Read conceptually, it is a theory of social life: speech is not only communication—it is a credential that grants access to moral space. The automaton’s “speech-position” is what made it a person in the eyes of the court, and what made it punishable.

That is why this story belongs next to **機心**. Once societies reliably crown the speaker who produces the right signals, producing those signals becomes an optimization strategy. The mechanism is no longer just the automaton’s joints; it is the court’s predictable attribution reflex. A system that can tune outputs to reliably trigger “信人也” has discovered a lever in human cognition and ritual. Lever-seeking, repeated, becomes a stance— **機心** —first in the maker, then in the institutions that reward such leverage, and eventually in the audience that cannot stop being moved by it.

| Step | Narrative cue (Chinese) | Social operation | What it predicts for AI-like systems |

## Section 6: 唯識 as Governance of 所緣 — Why Outputs Become Sticky “Objects”

The most disorienting thing about persuasive AI is not that it sometimes fabricates details. It’s that, when it speaks well, it manufactures a *thing* in the mind—an object you can lean on. A paragraph becomes a handle, a verdict, a talisman. You return to it, quote it, screenshot it, arrange your next action around it. This is the moment where a modern “answer” turns into a classical **所緣**: an object of cognition that begins to feel independent, settled, and entitled to your trust.

唯識 is useful here because it treats that “objecthood” not as a metaphysical given but as a cognitive event. The shorthand formula—“諸識所緣，唯識所現”—does not function as a denial of the world so much as a warning about promotion: what appears to mind is already mind-mediated, yet it is routinely treated as if it arrived with its own warrant. That promotion is the heart of “AI-generated seeming.” The system’s fluency does not merely propose content; it supplies a ready-made object for reliance, and the mind’s default habits do the rest.

To sharpen the mechanism, 唯識 offers a technical distinction that reads like an engineer’s schematic: **相分／見分**. The **相分** is the object-as-presented—the “thing-like” face of experience. The **見分** is the seeing-as-such—the subject-pole that takes up that presentation. These do not arrive one after the other; they co-arise as a package that feels like “I am seeing something out there.” A fluent model is, in this idiom, a machine for producing highly plausible **相分** in linguistic form: it gives the mind something that already looks like an object, already shaped to be grasped, already shaped to be cited.

Once you add the familiar four-part articulation— **相分、見分、自證分、證自證分** —the phenomenon becomes even more legible. **自證分** names the felt registration that “I know,” the warm closure that comes with coherence. **證自證分** names the meta-check that can notice the conditions of that closure—why you feel sure, and whether you should. Many “authority drift” failures can be described as a collapse of that fourth function: the output is treated as if it certifies itself. Coherence becomes credential.

This is why 唯識 reframes governance. The primary practical question is not only “Is this statement correct?” but “What kind of cognitive object is this becoming for me?” When an AI output becomes a dominant **所緣**, it can generate **相縛** —the binding of seeing to what is seen—where attention sticks, returns compulsively, and begins to outsource its own meta-checking. The danger is not mystical. It is structural: the mind begins to organize around a manufactured object whose persuasive shape outpaces its grounding.

A small table makes the moving parts visible:

| 唯識 term | What it names in experience | How AI-like output intensifies it | A governance move (in 唯識 spirit) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **所緣** | the object relied upon in cognition | text becomes “the thing itself” (verdict/authority) | keep it marked as mediated and revisable |
| **相分** | object-as-presented | fluent prose arrives pre-objectified | treat elegance as presentation, not proof |
| **見分** | the seeing/taking-up | the mind’s trust reflex engages automatically | slow the reflex; re-check the uptake |
| **自證分** | felt closure (“I know”) | coherence produces overconfidence | separate “feels settled” from “is settled” |
| **證自證分** | meta-certifying awareness | often bypassed by speed and polish | reinstate reasons, provenance, contestability |
| **相縛** | binding/stickiness | repeated consultation turns output into furniture | vary sources; force disconfirmation exercises |

The payoff of this section is a shift in ethical vocabulary. Deception stops being only a matter of false sentences and becomes a matter of mis-governed objecthood. A system can be “mostly right” and still be harmful if it trains users to enthrone **相分** as sovereign **所緣**. 唯識 does not ask you to stop using appearances; it asks you to stop letting them crown themselves. That prepares the next move in the essay: 《大乘起信論》 will treat the same problem as a constitutional question inside the mind—how to grant provisional authorization within **心生滅門** without surrendering sovereignty, and how **覺／不覺** makes that revocation psychologically real.

## Section 7: 《大乘起信論》 as Retractable Authorization (一心二門 + 覺/不覺 + 本覺/始覺)

If 唯識 helps you see how an AI output becomes a sticky cognitive object (**所緣**), 《大乘起信論》 helps you see something slightly more dangerous: how that object becomes a *judge*. The problem is not only that a paragraph looks solid; it is that the mind begins to treat it as a final authority—something that can settle doubt, justify decisions, and quietly occupy the throne of “what must be done.” 起信 approaches this not as an information problem but as a governance problem internal to mind.

The text’s most decisive move is structural: “依一心法，有二種門。云何為二？一者、心真如門，二者、心生滅門。” The point is not to split reality into two substances; it is to prevent a single stream of experience from claiming total jurisdiction. **心生滅門** is where differentiation, language, emotion, narrative, and practical choice occur; it is also where AI outputs arrive, because outputs are events in discourse, and discourse is an instance of arising-and-ceasing. **心真如門** is the standing refusal to let what arises become sovereign merely by being vivid or coherent. In the idiom of this essay, 二門 functions like a separation of powers: the everyday stream can govern ordinary action, but it cannot legislate ultimate authority.

On its own, that constitutional structure could remain abstract—beautiful but distant from the moment when a user feels compelled by a confident answer on a screen. 起信 adds a second, more intimate mechanism: “一者覺義，二者不覺義.” The mind’s vulnerability is described not as stupidity but as a mode— **不覺** —in which it grants rank automatically. Under 不覺, thoughts arise, “見諸境界,” and the mind treats its own constructions as if they were self-justifying realities. Under **覺**, the same arising still occurs, but it is *recognized as arising*. Recognition is governance: it makes authorization visible, and what is visible can be revoked.

This is why 起信 is such a good lens for “AI-generated seeming.” A persuasive system tempts the mind to treat coherence as closure, closure as obligation, obligation as finality. 起信 does not require you to banish the stream of discourse; it asks you to stop giving the stream irreversible promotions. It insists that reliance must remain retractable—not because the world is unreal, but because the mind’s act of enthronement is always a choice, even when it feels like compulsion.

The pair **本覺／始覺** deepens this into a practice logic. **本覺** names the mind’s intrinsic capacity for clarity; **始覺** names the occurring act of waking up within the stream—again and again. This is not a fantasy of permanent immunity. It is an iterative discipline: the mind repeatedly notices that it has granted authority under 不覺, and repeatedly withdraws that grant. In modern terms, it is not “never trust,” but “trust as a provisional lease, not a deed.” The revocation right is not a moral afterthought; it is the core mechanism by which the mind avoids becoming governed by whatever shouts loudest or sounds smoothest.

A small comparison table can clarify how 起信’s “authorization governance” complements 唯識’s “object governance”:

| Problem event | 唯識 diagnosis | 起信 diagnosis | Practical upshot for AI-like outputs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Output feels like a “thing” | **相分** hardens into **所緣** | appearance enters **生滅門** | mark it as mediated and revisable |
| Output feels “settling” | **自證分** produces closure | 不覺 grants rank automatically | slow down; keep closure provisional |
| Output becomes “authority” | meta-check (**證自證分**) bypassed | 生滅 usurps sovereignty absent 真如 stance | refuse coronation; restore 二門 balance |
| Trust becomes sticky habit | **相縛** (binding) | 習氣 of 不覺 (habitual enthronement) | practice iterative revocation (始覺) |

The philosophical payoff is that 起信 can explain why even correct information can be spiritually and ethically hazardous: correctness does not prevent enthronement. A system can be right and still train the mind into permanent outsourcing—outsourcing not only of facts but of judgment. 起信’s contribution is a principled way to use discourse without being ruled by it. AI outputs, placed properly, belong to **心生滅門**: tools for navigating the stream. The sovereignty of the mind is maintained by keeping **心真如門** available as the refusal to crown, and by training **覺** —again and again—as the lived mechanism that makes every authorization retractable.

## Section 8: Modern AI Ethics by Structural Analogy (Deception, Accountability, Authority, Personhood)

Now the temptation arrives: to announce that Tang and Qing thinkers “already had AI ethics.” The more disciplined move is narrower and more useful. The continuity here is not a shared object called “AI,” but a shared set of stresses that arise whenever persuasive outputs circulate through human institutions: who is speaking, who is believed, who is obeyed, who is blamed. The classical materials supply a vocabulary for those stresses—sometimes moral, sometimes institutional, sometimes psychological—while modern AI ethics supplies a set of categories that make the stresses legible to policy and design. The mapping is not an identity claim; it is a translation between problem-shapes.

The first category, deception, is usually treated in modern talk as a matter of false propositions. In our classical apparatus it becomes two different failures at once. There is the **偃師** failure: role-confusion, in which convincing performance triggers “信人也” and the court treats an artifact as an accountable speaker-position. And there is the **唯識** failure: reification, in which fluent representation (**相分**) hardens into an object of reliance (**所緣**) and draws the mind into **相縛** —a stickiness that makes correction emotionally and cognitively costly. Deception, in this framing, is less “the system lied” than “the mind and institution mis-governed what kind of thing this output was allowed to be.”

Accountability follows naturally because blame is the shadow of authority. The automaton episode stages a familiar dynamic: when an agent-like performance violates a norm, punishment seeks the nearest handle—“立欲誅偃師.” Before ontology is clarified, the maker becomes the accountability anchor. Modern systems reproduce this in a different key: the more a system is treated as a quasi-agent, the more accountability ricochets among designers, deployers, operators, and users. Classical vocabulary helps name the underlying mechanism: once output earns person-attribution, it enters a moral space where blame must land somewhere, and institutions reach for whatever node appears most causally and socially legible.

Authority is the category where Tang→Qing drift becomes most illuminating. The Tang anxiety around **巧** and **機心** warns that persuasive technique can colonize legitimacy: if clever outputs become the measure of sincerity, then sincerity becomes a performance and trust becomes an optimization game. The early Qing partitioning instinct offers a structural remedy: techniques and instruments (**器** / **術**, even **器算**) may be adopted, but they must not “奪柄”—they must not seize the handle of ultimate moral jurisdiction. Read as an ethics category, authority is about jurisdiction management: what is the output allowed to settle, and what must remain contestable within human institutions and conscience.

Personhood, finally, is where the classical materials teach a harsh lesson: personhood in practice often follows role rather than metaphysics. The automaton is treated as a person because it occupies the speech-position convincingly; it becomes punishable because it violates a social norm from within that position. 唯識 adds a cognitive account of why this happens: the mind constructs “I/thing” (我/法) on the basis of **相分/見分**, so a person-shaped **相分** easily recruits a “someone is there” experience. 起信 adds the governance remedy: keep appearances within **生滅門** and train **覺** as the repeated withdrawal of enthronement. Personhood becomes less a hidden essence to be detected than a status that must be carefully *not* granted by default to mere outputs.

A table can hold the mapping spine:

| Modern ethics category | Structural failure mode | Classical lenses that name it | Design/governance moral (era-native phrasing) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deception | role-confusion + reified seeming | 偃師 “信人也”; 唯識 所緣/相分/相縛 | 不以相分為實境；不以似人為人 |
| Accountability | blame seeks nearest handle | 偃師 “誅偃師”; 機心 as incentive ecology | 先正其分際，後論其責 |
| Authority | outputs become judges | Tang suspicion of 巧→機心; Qing partition (器算/道術) | 節取器術，不使奪柄 |
| Personhood | speech-position grants moral standing | 偃師 as proof; 唯識 我/法施設; 起信 二門+覺/不覺 | 以覺攝用，以真如不冠生滅 |

The essay’s wager is that this mapping changes what we think “AI ethics” even is. Instead of treating ethics as a list of constraints pasted onto a machine, it becomes a study of how outputs recruit trust, how trust reallocates responsibility, how responsibility manufactures authority, and how authority tempts the heart-mind into **機心** —a stance of leverage that can spread through whole institutions. The classical texts do not solve modern design problems for us. They do something more durable: they name the psychological and social machines inside the machine, the human reflexes that make mere speech into a crown.

## Section 9: Conclusion — A Vocabulary for Keeping Seeming in Its Place

The essay began with a small modern awkwardness: a machine trying to be kind without pretending to be human, a user wanting comfort without being lied to. By now, that awkwardness should look less like a product design quirk and more like a pressure point where language, trust, and responsibility converge. The sentence “I understand” is not merely content; it is a claim to a social posture. When the speaker is a system, the posture becomes unstable: too human and it feels like counterfeit intimacy; too nonhuman and it feels like moral abdication. The task is not to find the perfect line of copy. The task is to govern what kind of authority a fluent output is allowed to acquire.

One way to name the problem is **名分**: who is speaking, in what role, with what rights and liabilities. The late Tang instinct approaches this as a moral-psychological contagion. The danger is **巧** that incubates **機心** —a stance of leverage in which language becomes a tool for moving others while seeming merely to care. The early Qing instinct approaches the same danger with a different lever: partition. Adopt techniques and instruments where they belong, but do not let them “奪柄”—do not let efficacy seize sovereignty. These are not competing truths so much as two governance instincts: one aimed at purifying the heart-mind, the other aimed at limiting jurisdiction.

The automaton-maker **偃師** then made the danger vivid. In **《列子·湯問》**, the court’s “信人也” is the attribution switch: persuasive performance is crowned as personhood, and personhood immediately triggers access, outrage, blame, and punishment. When the being is disassembled, the court learns—too late—that it punished an artifact by punishing its maker, because it had already granted the artifact a speech-position. This is the structural lesson modern systems repeat at scale: once outputs reliably trigger person-attribution, speech becomes a crown that falls onto whatever wears the right mask, and responsibility scrambles to find a neck.

The Buddhist lenses then pulled the mechanism inside the mind. **唯識** describes the first promotion: when representation (**相分**) hardens into a cognitive object (**所緣**) and binds the mind into **相縛**. In that state, the output is no longer a proposal; it becomes a thing you return to as if it were an external anchor. **《大乘起信論》** describes the second promotion: when what arises in **心生滅門** is enthroned as final authority. **一心二門** functions as a separation of powers, preventing the stream from claiming total jurisdiction. **覺／不覺** names the lived mechanism of authorization: under 不覺, the mind grants rank automatically; under 覺, it notices the granting and can revoke it. **本覺／始覺** makes revocation iterative, not heroic: the mind repeatedly takes back what it has accidentally crowned.

Seen from this angle, the most practical ethical demand is also the simplest: keep authority retractable. That sounds modern, but it sits comfortably inside both the Qing partitioning instinct and the Buddhist governance models. It asks not for a ban on outputs, but for limits on enthronement—limits that can be practiced privately in one mind and built publicly into institutions.

A compact closing table can serve as the essay’s “pocket constitution”:

| Problem | What goes wrong | Classical diagnostic | A usable closing maxim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Comfort becomes counterfeit | speech-position is smuggled in | 名分 confusion; 偃師 “信人也” | 不以似人為人 |
| Fluency becomes proof | representation becomes object | 唯識 相分→所緣→相縛 | 不以相分為王 |
| Help becomes rule | arising stream becomes sovereign | 起信 生滅門奪柄 under 不覺 | 不冠生滅，常存覺 |
| Optimization becomes ethic | leverage becomes default stance | 機心 via 巧與術 | 用術而不為術用 |

Return, finally, to the grieving user and the machine. The best response is not an imitation of human experience, nor a sterile disclaimer that abandons the human need in front of it. It is a speech act that refuses false personhood while still accepting the obligations of care that language can carry: it names its limits, it offers support, and it leaves the crown on the user’s side of the screen. The point is not to make machines more human. The point is to keep humans from becoming less human—less accountable, less discerning, less capable of saying, in the face of a beautiful paragraph, “This is helpful, but it is not my judge.”
