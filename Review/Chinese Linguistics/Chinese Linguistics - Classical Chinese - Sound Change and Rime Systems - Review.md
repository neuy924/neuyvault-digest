## Abstract — English

Classical Chinese poetry is often read today through modern pronunciations that no longer preserve the phonological categories assumed by the poems themselves. This review examines the technical apparatus required to interpret those categories historically: the Qieyun tradition, Guangyun, Pingshui Yun, rime tables, Middle Chinese tone classes, modern dialect reflexes, and Sino-Xenic readings. It argues that classical poetic reading must distinguish three interacting but independent tracks: script history, literary register, and spoken phonology. The central diagnostic category is 入聲, the entering or checked tone, historically associated with final stop codas such as -p, -t, -k. Modern Standard Mandarin generally loses these codas and redistributes former entering-tone words across its modern tones, while Cantonese, Minnan, and many Wu varieties preserve checkedness in different ways. Cantonese retains -p, -t, -k robustly; Minnan preserves checked tone categories but complicates them through tone sandhi and literary-colloquial strata; Wu often preserves checkedness through glottal or stop-like closure. Sino-Japanese, Sino-Korean, and Sino-Vietnamese readings provide external comparative evidence for historical finals, especially in words such as 目, whose Mandarin form obscures an older checked final preserved in Cantonese, Hokkien, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese reflexes. The review applies this framework to 王之渙's 《登鸛雀樓》, showing how 流 and 樓 rhyme under 平水韻 下平十一尤, while 目 illustrates the importance of 入聲 for 平仄 analysis. The article concludes that classical Chinese poetry cannot be interpreted adequately through unannotated modern pronunciation. It requires a layered method: written text, poetic form, rime-book category, rime-table metadata, modern lect reflexes, and Sino-Xenic triangulation.

## 1. Introduction: The Problem of Reading Historical Chinese Through Modern Pronunciation

Classical Chinese poetry is often encountered through modern pronunciations that no longer preserve the sound categories assumed by the poems themselves. A Tang regulated poem may be written in graphically stable Chinese characters, transmitted through a classical literary register, classified by a historical rhyme system, and yet read aloud today in Modern Standard Mandarin, Cantonese, Minnan, Wu, Japanese 音読み(おんよみ), Korean 한자음, or Vietnamese Hán-Việt. These readings are not interchangeable. They preserve, merge, or erase different parts of the older phonological system.

The technical problem is therefore not simply "how did ancient Chinese sound?" It is more precise: what phonological categories did classical literary culture assume, and how do those categories map unevenly onto modern readings? The answer requires distinguishing at least six layers: written form, literary register, rime-dictionary category, rime-table structure, modern lect reflex, and comparative Sino-Xenic evidence.

The central case is the mismatch between traditional poetic categories and modern pronunciation. Classical regulated verse depends on inherited tone classes and rhyme groups. The four Middle Chinese tone classes—平, 上, 去, 入—are reduced in poetic scansion to the binary 平 / 仄, where 平 corresponds to level tone and 仄 includes the non-level categories 上, 去, 入. This division is basic to tone-pattern analysis in regulated verse. But Modern Standard Mandarin no longer preserves 入聲 as a distinct audible category, because the final stop codas associated with many entering-tone syllables have disappeared. Southern lects such as Cantonese and Minnan, by contrast, often preserve checked syllables with final stops or stop-like reflexes, making some older categories more audible.

This article reviews that problem technically. It moves from historical layers of Chinese writing and reading to the phonological machinery of the Qieyun tradition, then to modern dialect reflexes, rime-table metadata, and Sino-Xenic comparison. The purpose is not to reconstruct one definitive pronunciation for Tang poetry. It is to show why serious literary reading requires a layered apparatus rather than reliance on any single modern pronunciation.

### 1.1 The Central Mismatch: Written Continuity, Phonological Discontinuity

Chinese literary culture gives a strong impression of continuity because the written characters remain visually recognizable across long periods. This visual continuity can mislead readers into assuming phonological continuity. In reality, Chinese characters preserve a written lineage more effectively than they preserve sound. A character such as 目 remains graphically stable enough to be identified across textual traditions, but its modern readings differ sharply: Mandarin mù, Cantonese muk6, Hokkien ba̍k / bo̍k, Sino-Japanese モク, Sino-Korean 목, and Sino-Vietnamese mục. The graphic form is continuous; the phonological reflexes are not.

The literary tradition managed this instability through reference systems. The 《切韻》 Qieyun, published in 601, was a rime dictionary organized by rhyme groups and fanqie spelling. It is widely treated as a foundational source for reconstructing Middle Chinese phonology, but it should not be confused with a direct recording of one ordinary spoken dialect. Its system functioned as a literary-phonological norm for reading and classification. Later rime dictionaries, especially 《廣韻》 Guangyun, expanded the same tradition.

The mismatch becomes acute in poetry because poetry makes sound structurally important. Rhyme words must belong to appropriate rhyme groups; tone positions must satisfy 平仄 expectations; and entering-tone words may matter metrically even when they no longer sound checked in Mandarin. A poem that appears imperfect by modern sound may be fully regular by historical category.

**Table 1.1. The mismatch between written form and reading sound**

| Layer | What remains relatively stable | What changes substantially | Consequence for poetry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Script | Character identity, textual transmission | Graphic style, standard forms, simplification/traditional variants | The text can be read across centuries even when pronunciation changes |
| Literary register | Classical syntax and genre conventions | Degree of vernacularization, punctuation, prose norms | 文言 reading requires historical grammar as well as sound knowledge |
| Rime category | 韻部, tone class, fanqie tradition | Later compression or reclassification in systems such as 平水韻 | Rhyming must be judged by category, not only by modern ear |
| Modern pronunciation | Local reading traditions | Finals, tones, codas, initials | Different lects preserve different "fossils" of older categories |
| Poetry scansion | 平/仄 binary | Modern tone values and tone numbers | Mandarin tone numbers cannot be used mechanically as 平仄 proxies |

The point is methodological. Modern pronunciation is evidence, not authority. Mandarin, Cantonese, Minnan, Wu, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese readings may all illuminate historical categories, but none of them is identical to Middle Chinese.

### 1.2 Research Questions

This review is organized around five technical questions.

| Research question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How should script history, literary register, and spoken phonology be separated? | Prevents the common error of treating "Chinese" as one continuous sound system simply because the writing system is continuous |
| What does the Qieyun tradition encode? | Establishes the rime-dictionary basis for Middle Chinese initials, finals, tones, and rhyme groups |
| How do 平上去入 and 陰陽 map onto modern lects? | Explains why Cantonese, Minnan, Wu, and Mandarin preserve different tonal reflexes |
| Why is 入聲 the crucial diagnostic category? | It exposes the contrast between Mandarin loss of checked codas and southern/Sino-Xenic preservation |
| How should rime books and rime tables be used when reading poetry? | Provides a workflow for interpreting rhyme, 平仄, medials, grade, and historical finals |

The most important of these is the fourth. 入聲 is the category that most clearly exposes the limitations of reading classical poetry through Modern Standard Mandarin alone. In Middle Chinese classification, 入 belongs to the non-level, or 仄, side of the poetic tone system. It is also associated with checked syllables, historically involving final stops such as -p, -t, -k. Modern Mandarin generally lacks these final stop codas; Cantonese preserves them robustly; Minnan preserves checked categories; Wu often preserves checkedness through glottal or stop-like reflexes. This one category therefore links historical phonology, dialect comparison, and poetic scansion.

A second major question concerns rhyme granularity. 平水韻 Pingshui Yun became one of the most important rhyme systems for later Chinese poetry and is commonly described as a 106-rhyme system used after the Tang and as an official standard in later dynasties. But Pingshui is not identical to the finer categories of Qieyun or Guangyun. It is a practical poetic system, not a transparent phonetic transcription. This distinction matters when a word pair such as 流 / 樓 rhymes in a Pingshui group even though rime-table metadata may distinguish their finer historical finals.

### 1.3 Argument of the Review

The article advances a layered model of classical Chinese reading:

*Classical Chinese poetry should be read through historical sound categories, not through unannotated modern pronunciation.*

This does not mean that modern recitation is illegitimate. It means that modern recitation must be interpreted against older categories. The visible text alone does not tell us the rhyme class. Mandarin pinyin alone does not tell us whether a character was 入聲. Cantonese, Minnan, Wu, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese readings can preserve older segmental clues, but each is filtered through its own later phonological history.

The review's argument has four parts.

First, the history of Chinese must be divided into script, register, and speech. Script evolves from oracle-bone and bronze forms through seal, clerical, regular, print, and digital standards. Literary register evolves from early classical prose through Han fu, parallel prose, regulated verse, ci, qu, vernacular fiction, and modern written vernacular. Spoken phonology follows a different path: Old Chinese, Middle Chinese, Old Mandarin, modern Mandarin, and non-Mandarin Sinitic lects. These tracks intersect but do not coincide.

Second, the Qieyun-Guangyun tradition provides a categorical framework for Middle Chinese. It organizes characters by rhyme group, tone, and fanqie spelling, and later rime tables add analytical features such as 開/合 and 等. These features are not decorative. They encode distinctions relevant to medials, rounding, palatality, finals, and poetic rhyme behavior.

Third, modern lects should be treated as comparative witnesses. Mandarin is indispensable but not sufficient. Cantonese and Minnan are especially valuable for demonstrating the survival of checked syllables; Wu shows a different path through glottalized checkedness; Sino-Xenic readings provide external evidence for historical finals and entering-tone behavior.

Fourth, literary interpretation benefits from a reproducible workflow: identify the poem's rhyme words, look up their 平水韻 category, classify 平/仄, check whether line-final or metrically relevant characters are 入聲, then compare modern dialect and Sino-Xenic reflexes. This method does not replace literary interpretation; it prevents phonological misreadings from distorting it.

### 1.4 Scope and Limits

This review is technical but category-based. It does not attempt to establish one complete phonetic reconstruction of Middle Chinese. That would require adopting a specific reconstruction system, such as Baxter, Pulleyblank, Wang Li, or another scholarly model, and then applying it consistently to all examples. Instead, the review focuses on the more stable apparatus shared by the rime tradition: initial categories, final categories, tone classes, rhyme groups, entering codas, 開/合, 等, and dialect reflexes.

The review also avoids the claim that any modern lect "is Tang Chinese." Cantonese and Minnan preserve important features, especially checked codas, but they also underwent their own innovations. Mandarin lost many final stop distinctions but preserves other historically important developments. Sino-Xenic readings are powerful evidence, but they are mediated by borrowing periods, local phonologies, and multiple reading strata.

**Table 1.2. What this article treats as established, interpretive, and uncertain**

## 2. Three Independent but Interacting Tracks: Script, Register, and Speech

The history of Chinese cannot be represented adequately as a single line from "ancient Chinese" to "modern Chinese." That model collapses three different histories: the history of graphic form, the history of literary register, and the history of spoken phonology. These histories overlap, but they do not move at the same speed or respond to the same pressures. Script changes through material media, scribal practice, bureaucracy, standardization, printing, and modern state policy. Literary register changes through genre, education, ideology, elite taste, print markets, and modern reform. Spoken phonology changes through sound change, regional divergence, contact, and standardization.

This distinction is essential for reading classical poetry. A Tang poem may survive in recognizable Chinese characters; its syntax may remain classical; its rhymes may be classified through 平水韻; its deeper phonological coordinates may be recoverable through 切韻, 廣韻, and 韻圖; and its modern oral realization may differ radically depending on whether it is read in Mandarin, Cantonese, Minnan, Wu, Japanese 音読み(おんよみ), Korean 한자음, or Vietnamese Hán-Việt. No single modern pronunciation captures all historical layers.

### 2.1 Script Evolution: From 甲骨文 to Digital Standards

The script track concerns the visible form of written characters: how graphs were shaped, standardized, transmitted, and reproduced. It is the most visually continuous of the three tracks, but this continuity is partly deceptive. The recognizable survival of characters does not mean the survival of their historical pronunciation.

The earliest securely attested Chinese writing is oracle-bone script 甲骨文, associated with late Shang divinatory inscriptions. Oracle-bone inscriptions were carved on turtle plastrons and animal scapulae, and they record royal divination topics such as warfare, sacrifice, agriculture, illness, weather, and dynastic concerns. They are usually dated to the late second millennium BCE and constitute the earliest substantial corpus of Chinese writing.

Bronze inscriptions 金文 followed in ritual and commemorative contexts, especially during the Zhou. Qin standardization promoted small seal script 小篆, while the late Warring States, Qin, and Han periods saw the emergence and consolidation of clerical script 隸書, a major structural turning point in the shape of Chinese characters. By the medieval period, regular script 楷書 became the dominant legible model for manuscript and later print culture. The later history of script is not one of complete replacement but of layered coexistence: seal and clerical forms survive in calligraphy, epigraphy, seals, and antiquarian scholarship; regular script dominates ordinary legibility; running and cursive styles preserve scribal and aesthetic practice; print and digital fonts standardize everyday visual norms.

**Table 2.1. Script track: graphic evolution and institutional function**

| Period | Script form | Main medium / setting | Defining feature | Relevance to literary reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Late Shang | 甲骨文 | Turtle plastrons, ox scapulae | Incised divinatory inscriptions | Earliest large corpus of Chinese writing, but not a guide to later poetic pronunciation |
| Western/Eastern Zhou | 金文 | Ritual bronzes | Commemorative, ritual, lineage-focused inscriptions | Shows early writing beyond divinatory formulae |
| Qin | 小篆 | Imperial standardization, inscriptions | Graphic standardization across empire | Demonstrates state control over script independent of speech |
| Qin-Han | 隸書 | Administration, bamboo/wood slips, stone | Flattened, clericalized forms; faster bureaucratic writing | Major structural transformation toward later character forms |
| Wei-Jin to Tang | 楷書 | Manuscripts, stelae, calligraphy | Stable regular forms | Main basis for later legible manuscript and printed character forms |
| Song-Ming-Qing | Printed regular forms + calligraphic traditions | Woodblock print, movable type, manuscripts | Print stabilizes book-form characters | Widens circulation of classical texts and rhyme books |
| Modern | Simplified/traditional digital standards | Schools, publishing, computing | State and platform standardization | Fixes visual forms while pronunciation remains regionally divergent |

The crucial point is that script history is not phonological history. A graph can remain stable while its pronunciation changes drastically. 目, for example, can be visually continuous while its readings diverge as Mandarin mù, Cantonese muk6, Hokkien ba̍k, Japanese モク, Korean 목, and Vietnamese mục. The written character alone does not tell the reader whether the word belonged to 入聲 or how it behaved metrically in regulated verse.

### 2.2 Literary Register Evolution: From 文言 to 白話

The second track is not graphic but stylistic: the history of written register. Classical Chinese 文言 is not simply "old Chinese written down." It is a literary register that became increasingly detached from everyday speech while remaining central to elite education, administration, historiography, philosophy, poetry, and examinations.

Early classical prose developed through transmitted texts associated with the Warring States and early imperial periods: argument, aphorism, dialogue, narrative, memorial, and historiography. Han writing expanded both historical prose and ornate 賦 fu. The Six Dynasties elevated 駢文 parallel prose, emphasizing syntactic symmetry and rhetorical balance. Tang culture canonized regulated verse 近體詩, while the 古文 movement advocated a return to clearer classical prose models against excessive parallelism. Song writers developed essayistic prose and 詞 ci lyric. Yuan drama and 曲 qu brought performance-linked, more colloquial energies into elite textual memory. Ming-Qing commercial print culture supported large-scale 白話小說 vernacular fiction, even as classical prose remained central to official and scholarly life.

Modern 白話文 written vernacular became dominant only after intellectual and institutional struggle. The early twentieth-century vernacular movement, intensified around the May Fourth period, promoted vernacular writing as part of a broader program of literacy, education, modernization, and national reform. Following the May Fourth movement, written vernacular Chinese became the normal written form in modern Chinese education and publication.

**Table 2.2. Register track: major literary styles and their functions**

| Period | Dominant or emblematic register/style | Representative genres | Defining feature | Relationship to speech |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-Qin | 先秦文言 | Philosophy, speeches, aphorisms, narrative prose | Compact syntax, particles, parataxis, argument | Related to earlier speech but already literarily conventional |
| Han | 史傳文, 漢賦 | Historiography, biographies, rhapsody | Historical narration and ornate display | Elite written register; not ordinary speech transcription |
| Six Dynasties | 駢文 | Court prose, letters, prefaces | Parallelism, balance, allusion | Highly artificial elite style |
| Tang | 唐詩, 古文 | Regulated verse, essays | Tonal patterning in poetry; prose revival | Poetry depends on inherited phonological categories |
| Song | 宋文, 宋詞 | Essays, lyrics, commentarial prose | Analytic prose and lyric refinement | Some lyric forms preserve musical-performance traces |
| Yuan | 元曲 | Drama, arias | Dialogue and performance-driven idiom | Stronger colloquial pull |
| Ming-Qing | 白話小說, elite 古文 | Fiction, essays, examination prose | Vernacular narrative expands; classical prose persists | Diglossic coexistence |
| Modern | 白話文 | Essays, journalism, textbooks, fiction | Modern punctuation, syntax, translation-influenced prose | Written norm more closely aligned with modern standard speech, though not identical |

For this review, the most important implication is that classical poetry is not just old speech preserved in old characters. It is a literary technology governed by specialized conventions: rhyme categories, tonal patterns, line lengths, parallelism, allusion, and genre-specific expectations. A reader who approaches Tang poetry using only modern Mandarin pronunciation is therefore using one modern reflex to read a text whose formal apparatus belongs to a different phonological regime.

### 2.3 Spoken Phonology: From Old Chinese to Middle Chinese and Modern Lects

The third track is the history of sound systems. It is the least directly visible because ancient speech is not recorded acoustically. It must be reconstructed from evidence: rhymes in early texts, phonetic series in characters, rime dictionaries, rime tables, modern dialects, transcriptional evidence, and Sino-Xenic readings.

A conventional high-level sequence distinguishes Old Chinese 上古漢語, Middle Chinese 中古漢語, Old or Early Mandarin 近古官話, and modern Sinitic lects. The most important anchor for this review is Middle Chinese, not because it was a single spoken language of all China, but because the Qieyun tradition provides the technical categories used in later historical phonology and literary rhyme analysis. The Qieyun, published in 601, was a rime dictionary using fanqie to indicate character pronunciation and became a core documentary source for reconstructing historical Chinese phonology.

The spoken track matters because modern lects do not descend from Middle Chinese by identical paths. Mandarin, Cantonese, Minnan, and Wu preserve and innovate differently. Mandarin loses final stop codas and thus loses 入聲 as a separate audible category. Cantonese preserves -p, -t, -k and therefore preserves checked syllables vividly. Minnan preserves checked categories but complicates them through tone sandhi and multiple reading strata. Wu often preserves checkedness through glottal stop or related closure rather than maintaining all older stop-coda distinctions.

**Table 2.3. Spoken track: major stages and evidence**

| Stage | Approximate historical anchor | Main evidence | Technical relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old Chinese 上古漢語 | Shang/Zhou to early imperial period | Early rhymes, phonetic series, comparative reconstruction | Background to later tone development and syllable structure |
| Middle Chinese 中古漢語 | Sui-Tang, especially Qieyun tradition | Qieyun, Guangyun, rime tables, rhyme categories | Main coordinate system for this article |
| Late Middle / early Mandarin transitions | Song-Yuan-Ming | Rime books, northern rhyme traditions, changed finals | Shows movement toward Mandarin-type systems |
| Modern Mandarin | Qing-modern standardization; twentieth-century national standards | Standard descriptions, education, pinyin | Loses final stops; modern tones do not map directly onto 平仄 |
| Modern non-Mandarin lects | Regional Sinitic continua | Cantonese, Minnan, Wu, Hakka, etc. | Preserve different historical reflexes, especially 入 and tone registers |
| Sino-Xenic readings | Borrowed Chinese reading layers in Japan, Korea, Vietnam | Japanese 音読み(おんよみ), Korean 한자음, Vietnamese Hán-Việt | External comparative evidence for historical finals and categories |

The sequence should not be read as a simple replacement chain. "Middle Chinese" in this context is a reconstruction based on a literary-phonological tradition. Modern Cantonese or Hokkien is not "unchanged Middle Chinese." Mandarin is not simply "corrupted Middle Chinese." Each modern system preserves some features and loses others.

### 2.4 Why the Three Tracks Must Not Be Collapsed

The most persistent error in discussing Chinese historical language is to treat the written text, the literary register, and the spoken reading as one object. This produces several false inferences.

First, because characters are visually continuous, readers may assume sound continuity. But the history of 目 shows the opposite: the graph remains, while the readings diverge. Second, because classical texts are transmitted in stable form, readers may assume that modern Mandarin recitation reveals the poem's original rhyme and tone structure. It does not. Third, because some southern lects preserve checked codas better than Mandarin, readers may overcorrect and claim that Cantonese or Hokkien "is Tang Chinese." They are not. They are modern lects preserving important but partial evidence.

The correct model is layered.

**Table 2.4. Three-track model and common analytical errors**

| Track | What it explains | What it does not explain by itself | Common error if collapsed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Script | Graphic continuity, textual transmission, standardization | Historical pronunciation | Assuming unchanged characters imply unchanged sound |
| Literary register | Syntax, genre, style, elite norms, vernacularization | Exact phonology | Treating 文言 as direct transcription of ancient speech |
| Spoken phonology | Sound change, tone development, dialect reflexes | Literary convention by itself | Using modern pronunciation alone to judge historical rhyme |
| Rime-book tradition | Rhyme category, tone class, fanqie evidence | One local spoken dialect | Treating Qieyun as a simple dialect dictionary |
| Rime-table tradition | Medials, grade, open/closed structure | Certainty about exact phonetic values without reconstruction choice | Treating 等 and 開合 as transparent IPA |
| Modern dialect comparison | Reflexes of older categories | Direct survival of Tang pronunciation | Claiming one modern lect is "the original" |

A more precise statement is this: classical Chinese poetry is written in characters, composed in a literary register, governed by historical rhyme categories, and read today through modern phonological systems. These layers must be separated analytically before they can be recombined interpretively.

### 2.5 The Technical Payoff for Poetry

This three-track distinction is not merely taxonomic. It directly affects how one reads poetry.

A Tang poem's written form may be transparent, but its rhyme logic may not be transparent in Modern Standard Mandarin. For example, 流 and 樓 rhyme in 平水韻 under 下平十一尤, even if their detailed rime-table histories may differ. 目, by contrast, belongs to an entering-tone category and is 仄, even though Mandarin mù gives no final stop clue. Cantonese muk6, Hokkien ba̍k, Korean 목, and Vietnamese mục preserve the old checked-final pattern more visibly.

The reader therefore needs a workflow:

| Step | Question | Tool or evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What is the written text? | Received character text and variants |
| 2 | What literary form is it? | 絕句, 律詩, 詞, 曲, prose genre |
| 3 | Which words are rhyme words? | Line position and poetic form |
| 4 | Which rhyme group do they belong to? | 平水韻, 廣韻, other rhyme systems |
| 5 | Are they 平 or 仄? | Tone class: 平 vs 上去入 |
| 6 | Are any words 入聲? | Rime dictionary and dialect/Sino-Xenic evidence |
| 7 | How do modern readings differ? | Mandarin, Cantonese, Minnan, Wu, Sino-Xenic comparison |
| 8 | What structural features explain the divergence? | 韻圖 categories: 開/合, 等, rhyme group, coda |

This workflow transforms modern pronunciation from an unreliable authority into a useful comparative witness. Mandarin, Cantonese, Minnan, Wu, and Sino-Xenic readings each become evidence for different parts of the older system.

### 2.6 Section Conclusion

Chinese historical reading requires a three-track model. Script explains how texts remain graphically transmissible. Register explains why classical literary style persists independently of speech. Spoken phonology explains why modern readings diverge from the categories assumed by poetry. The next section turns to the principal technical apparatus that made this layered system usable: the Qieyun tradition and the construction of Middle Chinese as a literary-phonological coordinate system.

## 3. The Qieyun System and the Construction of Middle Chinese

The previous section separated three tracks: script, literary register, and speech. This section turns to the technical apparatus that makes the spoken track historically recoverable: the Qieyun tradition. The key point is methodological. Middle Chinese is not simply "the Chinese spoken in the Tang." In the context of historical phonology, it is a reconstructed system grounded primarily in rime dictionaries, rime tables, poetic evidence, modern dialect comparison, and Sino-Xenic readings.

The 《切韻》 Qieyun, completed in 601, sits at the centre of this apparatus. It classified characters by tone and rhyme and gave pronunciations through 反切 fanqie, a spelling method that uses one character to indicate the initial and another to indicate the final plus tone. Later works, especially 《廣韻》 Guangyun, expanded and transmitted the system. Rime tables such as 《韻鏡》 Yunjing later arranged the same tradition into a more explicitly analytic grid of initials, finals, grades, and open/closed categories. Together, these sources created the technical coordinate system by which later scholars reconstruct Middle Chinese and by which readers can understand classical rhyme.

### 3.1 《切韻》 as a Literary-Phonological Coordinate System

The Qieyun was compiled by 陸法言 Lu Fayan and completed in 601, during the Sui period. Its preface frames the work as a response to divergent regional and scholarly pronunciations, with the aim of providing a corrected and harmonized reading standard. Its importance lies less in recording one local spoken dialect than in codifying a literary reading system—a norm for reading, rhyming, and classifying characters.

The structure of Qieyun is organized by:

| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| Tone class | Places characters under 平, 上, 去, 入 |
| Rhyme group | Groups characters with similar finals for literary and phonological purposes |
| Fanqie spelling 反切 | Gives pronunciation using two characters: one for initial, one for final plus tone |
| Character entries | Provide lexical items sorted into tone-rhyme categories |
| Normative orientation | Offers a cultivated reading standard rather than a plain vernacular transcript |

The crucial implication is that Qieyun is not equivalent to a modern pronunciation dictionary. A modern dictionary generally tells a reader how a word is pronounced in a standard language. Qieyun, by contrast, preserves a category system: a network of distinctions that can be triangulated into a reconstruction but is not itself an IPA chart.

**Table 3.1. Qieyun as a source type**

| Dimension | Qieyun system | Modern pronunciation dictionary |
|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Literary reading, rhyme classification, learned pronunciation | Standardized modern pronunciation |
| Basic unit | Character entry within tone-rhyme category | Word or character with phonetic reading |
| Spelling method | 反切 fanqie | Alphabetic, phonetic, or romanized transcription |
| Phonological status | Categorical evidence requiring reconstruction | Direct representation of a modern standard |
| Regional relation | Composite / normative literary system | Usually tied to one standard lect |
| Relevance to poetry | High: determines rhyme and tone classes | Limited: modern pronunciation may obscure historical categories |

**Fanqie as a Technical Device**

Fanqie 反切 works by splitting syllabic information between two characters. The first character supplies the initial consonant; the second supplies the final and tone.

| Target character | First fanqie character | Second fanqie character | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| X | A | B | X has the initial of A and the final/tone of B |

This is not the same as alphabetic spelling. It presupposes a shared reading tradition: the user must already know how to read the fanqie characters. Its value for reconstruction comes from the network effect. Across thousands of entries, repeated fanqie initials and finals reveal equivalence classes, allowing scholars to infer the structure of the system.

### 3.2 Tone Classes in the Qieyun Tradition: 平, 上, 去, 入

Qieyun organizes entries under the four traditional tone classes:

| Tone class | Chinese | Later poetic status | Technical note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level | 平 | 平 | Later divided into upper/lower or 陰/陽 in many lects |
| Rising | 上 | 仄 | Non-level category |
| Departing | 去 | 仄 | Non-level category |
| Entering | 入 | 仄 | Associated with checked syllables, historically stop-final codas |

For literary purposes, the later poetic binary 平/仄 compresses these four classes: 平 remains level, while 上, 去, 入 are grouped as 仄. This simplification is fundamental in regulated verse, but it should not erase the technical distinction between the three oblique categories. 入 is especially important because it preserves a segmental dimension: checked syllable closure.

The Qieyun tone classes therefore serve two roles simultaneously. They classify pronunciation historically, and they supply the basis for later literary scansion. A reader who sees only Modern Mandarin tone numbers cannot recover the old classification reliably, because Mandarin tone categories have undergone substantial reshuffling and have lost entering-tone codas.

### 3.3 Rhyme Groups and the Logic of Finals

A rime dictionary is organized not merely by tone but by rhyme groups. These groups classify characters according to their finals: the part of the syllable after the initial, including medial, main vowel, coda, and tone category in traditional analysis.

| Syllable component | Traditional category | Modern analytic equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Initial consonant | 聲母 | onset |
| Medial / glide | part of 韻母, later described via 等 and 開合 | -j-, -w-, rounding or palatal elements |
| Main vowel / nucleus | part of 韻母 | vowel nucleus |
| Coda | part of 韻母 | final consonant or glide |
| Tone | 聲調 | lexical tone category |
| Rhyme group | 韻部 / 韻目 | class of finals used for rhyme |

In the Qieyun tradition, rhyme groups are not arbitrary. They reflect systematic distinctions in finals. Some of these distinctions remain audible in modern lects; others are merged. This is why a modern reader needs the historical category. Two characters may rhyme in a traditional system even if their modern Mandarin finals differ, and two characters may sound similar in Mandarin while belonging to different historical rhyme categories.

### 3.4 《廣韻》 and the Expansion of the Qieyun Tradition

The 《廣韻》 Guangyun, completed in the Northern Song in 1008, is a major expansion and revision of the Qieyun lineage. It preserves the same basic rime-dictionary logic but includes a much larger character inventory and became one of the most important reference works for later phonological scholarship.

Guangyun matters for this review because many modern lookup systems and historical phonology datasets cite Guangyun categories rather than the fragmentary original Qieyun directly. When one identifies a character's historical rhyme group, tone, or fanqie, one is often using Guangyun as the practical source.

**Table 3.2. Qieyun and Guangyun compared**

| Feature | Qieyun 切韻 | Guangyun 廣韻 |
|---|---|---|
| Date | 601 | 1008 |
| Period | Sui | Northern Song |
| Function | Foundational rime dictionary | Expanded and revised rime dictionary |
| Scale | Smaller, earlier system | Larger character coverage |
| Status in reconstruction | Core source for Middle Chinese categories | Major transmitted source for Qieyun-lineage categories |
| Literary relevance | Establishes learned reading-rhyme framework | Provides later standard reference for rhyme and phonology |
| Practical modern use | Historically foundational but less directly accessible | Frequently used in modern databases and philological references |

The relationship between Qieyun and Guangyun is analogous to a foundational specification and a later expanded implementation. The earlier work gives the system its historical authority; the later work preserves and elaborates it in a more usable form.

### 3.5 Rime Tables: From Dictionary Lists to Analytic Grids

Rime dictionaries classify entries, but rime tables arrange the system visually and analytically. Works such as 《韻鏡》 Yunjing organize syllables by initials and finals and add metadata such as 開口 / 合口 and 等. These categories are indispensable for connecting rhyme groups to medial structure.

| Rime-table feature | Chinese | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Initial row or category | 聲母 / 字母 | Places syllables under initial consonant classes |
| Rhyme / final | 韻 | Groups finals |
| Open/closed | 開口 / 合口 | Often interpreted as absence/presence of rounding or labial medial |
| Grade | 一等, 二等, 三等, 四等 | Encodes distributional classes linked to medial/vowel structure |
| Tone | 平, 上, 去, 入 | Maintains tone-class classification |
| Division of tables | 攝, 韻, 等呼 | Organizes related finals and their variants |

The rime table turns the rime dictionary into a structural map. It helps answer questions such as:

| Question | Rime-table relevance |
|---|---|
| Why do two words belong to related rhyme families but have different modern reflexes? | They may differ in 等 or 開合 |
| Why does a modern Mandarin final show -iu while another shows -ou? | Grade and medial structure may differ |
| Why does a word preserve -k in Cantonese or Sino-Xenic but not in Mandarin? | It may belong to 入聲 with a stop coda |
| Why does Pingshui group words together that Guangyun distinguishes? | Pingshui is a poetic simplification of finer rime categories |

### 3.6 Middle Chinese as Reconstruction, Not a Single Spoken Dialect

A common error is to treat Middle Chinese as if it were one homogeneous language spoken uniformly across China during the Sui or Tang. This is not the technical meaning used in historical phonology. Middle Chinese is a reconstruction from evidence, especially the Qieyun tradition.

| Evidence type | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Qieyun / Guangyun fanqie | Defines equivalence classes for initials and finals |
| Rime categories | Establishes rhyme-group structure |
| Tone classes | Provides 平上去入 categories |
| Rime tables | Add 開合, 等, initial tables, and structural organization |
| Classical poetry | Shows rhyme practice and tone-pattern constraints |
| Modern Sinitic lects | Provide reflexes of older initials, finals, tones, and codas |
| Sino-Xenic readings | External evidence from Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese borrowing strata |
| Transcriptional evidence | Occasional evidence from foreign names, Buddhist transcriptions, and contact materials |

Middle Chinese is therefore an inferred system. Its categories are more secure than its exact phonetic values. It is usually safer to say that a character belonged to 入聲 or to a particular rhyme group than to assert a single precise phonetic value without naming the reconstruction system being used.

**Table 3.3. Category certainty vs phonetic reconstruction**

| Claim type | Example | Stability |
|---|---|---|
| Category-level | Character belongs to 入聲 | High, when supported by rime dictionary tradition |
| Category-level | Character belongs to a specific rhyme group | High to moderate, depending on source system |
| Structural | Character is 開口 or 合口 in a rime-table tradition | Moderate to high, source-dependent |
| Structural | Character is 三等 rather than 一等 | Moderate to high, source-dependent |
| Phonetic | Exact Middle Chinese vowel quality | Model-dependent |
| Phonetic | Exact value of a medial | Model-dependent |
| Phonetic | Exact initial articulation in IPA | Reconstruction-dependent |

This distinction will matter throughout the article. The review uses Middle Chinese primarily as a category system rather than as a fully specified phonetic language.

### 3.7 The Qieyun System and Literary Authority

The Qieyun tradition is not merely a linguistic artifact. It had literary authority. Poets, scholars, and exam candidates needed shared rhyme categories because local pronunciations differed. Without such a standard, rhyme would become regionally unstable. A poet from one region and a reader from another might not agree on what rhymed.

Rime dictionaries solved this by externalizing pronunciation into categories. The categories became a shared standard for literary production. Later poetic systems such as 平水韻 simplified and regularized these categories for practical composition, but they still depend on the older rime-dictionary logic.

This explains why modern pronunciation cannot be the final judge of classical rhyme. Modern Mandarin may not make a rhyme obvious; Cantonese may preserve a coda Mandarin has lost; Hokkien may preserve checkedness but apply tone sandhi; Japanese or Korean readings may preserve final stops through borrowing strata. The literary standard sits above these local readings as a historical coordinate system.

### 3.8 From Qieyun Categories to Modern Reflexes

The Qieyun system is useful because it predicts where modern divergences will occur. A few major correspondences recur:

| Qieyun / MC category | Modern reflex issue |
|---|---|
| 入聲 | Mandarin loses stop codas; Cantonese/Minnan/Sino-Xenic often preserve stop-like reflexes |
| 清 / 濁 initial classes | Later tone-register splits into 陰/陽 categories |
| 開 / 合 | Reflexes may differ by presence or absence of rounding medial |
| 三等 / 四等 | Reflexes may show palatal or i-like elements |
| Fine rhyme categories | Pingshui may merge them for poetic use |
| Fanqie initials | Later lects may merge voicing, aspiration, or place distinctions |

This is why the same poem can be read through multiple modern systems without any one of them exhausting the historical structure. The Qieyun categories form the reference layer; modern lects are reflex layers.

### 3.9 Section Conclusion

The Qieyun tradition provides the technical foundation for this review. It classifies characters by tone and rhyme, encodes pronunciation through fanqie, and forms the basis for later rime dictionaries and rime tables. Its categories allow scholars to reconstruct Middle Chinese and allow readers to understand why classical poetry rhymes and scans according to historical rules that modern pronunciation may obscure.

## 4. Middle Chinese Initials and Finals: Inventory and Structural Logic

The Qieyun tradition does not present Middle Chinese as an alphabetic phoneme chart. It gives a categorical system: initials, finals, tones, rhyme groups, and later rime-table features. Modern reconstructions translate that system into phonological inventories, but the underlying evidence remains philological. This section therefore treats Middle Chinese first as a structured category system, then compares it with Modern Standard Mandarin.

The major contrast is straightforward. Middle Chinese, as reconstructed from the Qieyun-rime-table tradition, preserves a rich set of initial categories and final distinctions, including nasal codas -m, -n, -ŋ and checked codas -p, -t, -k. Modern Standard Mandarin has a smaller coda inventory: essentially -n, -ŋ, and rhotic -r in erhua contexts; it lacks final -p, -t, -k, which is why 入聲 is no longer segmentally visible in Mandarin. Standard Mandarin also lacks the older voiced-obstruent contrast, instead using aspiration as the major contrast in stops and affricates.

### 4.1 The Thirty-Six Initials 三十六字母

The rime-table tradition organizes initials through the famous 三十六字母, conventionally called the "thirty-six initials." These are not letters in the alphabetic sense. They are named initial categories, each represented by a character such as 幫, 滂, 並, or 明. They classify syllable onsets by place and manner of articulation, including voiceless, aspirated, voiced, nasal, fricative, affricate, lateral, and laryngeal categories.

Modern descriptions often distinguish Early Middle Chinese, Late Middle Chinese, and rime-table categories. The thirty-six-initial system is especially associated with the later rime-table tradition, while Qieyun itself is reconstructed from fanqie categories. The difference matters, but for this review the thirty-six initials are useful because they show the structural richness assumed by medieval phonological analysis.

**Table 4.1. The thirty-six initials grouped by traditional place/manner**

| Broad group | Initials | Approximate structural role |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy labials 重唇音 | 幫 滂 並 明 | Labial stop/nasal series: voiceless, aspirated, voiced, nasal |
| Light labials 輕唇音 | 非 敷 奉 微 | Labiodental or "light-lip" series in later rime-table tradition |
| Dentals 舌頭音 | 端 透 定 泥 | Dental/alveolar stop/nasal series |
| Retroflex stops 舌上音 | 知 徹 澄 孃 | Retroflex or palatalized stop/nasal series |
| Dental sibilants 齒頭音 | 精 清 從 心 邪 | Affricate/fricative series |
| Retroflex sibilants 正齒音 | 照 穿 牀 審 禪 | Retroflex affricate/fricative series |
| Velars 牙音 | 見 溪 群 疑 | Velar stop/nasal series |
| Laryngeals 喉音 | 影 曉 匣 喻 | Glottal/laryngeal and approximant categories |
| Liquids / special | 來 日 | Lateral and special coronal/rhotic-like category |

A useful way to read the table is not as a fixed IPA inventory, but as a set of contrastive slots. For example, 幫 / 滂 / 並 / 明 encodes a labial series with a voiceless unaspirated member, an aspirated member, a voiced member, and a nasal. In the rime-table tradition, similar structural series appear across dentals, velars, and sibilants.

### 4.2 清濁 Categories and Their Later Importance

Traditional phonology classifies initials not only by place but also by voicing and aspiration categories. These are usually described with terms such as 清, 次清, 全濁, and 次濁.

**Table 4.2. Initial voicing classes and later tonal consequences**

| Traditional class | Approximate phonetic interpretation | Example initials | Later relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 清 | Voiceless unaspirated obstruents | 幫, 端, 見, 精 | Often feeds 陰 register |
| 次清 | Voiceless aspirated obstruents | 滂, 透, 溪, 清 | Often feeds 陰 register |
| 全濁 / 濁 | Voiced obstruents | 並, 定, 群, 從 | Often feeds 陽 register after devoicing |
| 次濁 | Sonorants: nasals, liquids, approximants | 明, 泥, 疑, 來 | Often patterns with 陽, though details vary by lect |

The importance of these classes lies in later tone history. When voiced obstruents disappeared or changed in many Sinitic lects, their voicing contrast was often transferred into tonal register. This is the mechanism behind the later 陰 / 陽 split: historically voiceless initials tend to produce 陰 tones, while historically voiced initials tend to produce 陽 tones.

This is why initial categories are not merely a matter of consonants. They explain later tonal reflexes. Cantonese, for example, lost the Middle Chinese voiced obstruents as voiced stops or fricatives, but the old voicing distinction survives indirectly in its tonal register split.

### 4.3 Modern Standard Mandarin Initials: A Compressed and Reorganized System

Modern Standard Mandarin has a much smaller and differently organized initial system. Its stops and affricates do not contrast voiced versus voiceless in the way that Middle Chinese categories imply. Instead, Mandarin primarily contrasts unaspirated and aspirated voiceless obstruents.

**Table 4.3. Mandarin initials by pinyin category and phonetic contrast**

| Pinyin series | Approximate IPA type | Contrastive basis |
|---|---|---|
| b / p | [p] / [pʰ] | unaspirated vs aspirated |
| d / t | [t] / [tʰ] | unaspirated vs aspirated |
| g / k | [k] / [kʰ] | unaspirated vs aspirated |
| z / c | [ts] / [tsʰ] | unaspirated vs aspirated |
| zh / ch | [ʈʂ] / [ʈʂʰ] | unaspirated vs aspirated |
| j / q | [tɕ] / [tɕʰ] | unaspirated vs aspirated |
| s, sh, x, h, f | fricatives | place contrasts |
| m, n, l, r | sonorant / liquid / rhotic categories | voiced sonorants or approximant-like categories |

The important comparison is this: Middle Chinese categories encode older voiced obstruents, while Mandarin does not preserve voiced obstruents as a major contrast. This is one reason modern Mandarin tone categories cannot be mechanically equated with Middle Chinese tone classes.

**Table 4.4. Middle Chinese initials versus Mandarin initials**

| Feature | Middle Chinese / rime-table tradition | Modern Standard Mandarin |
|---|---|---|
| Voiced obstruents | Present as a major category: 並, 定, 群, 從, etc. | Not preserved as voiced stops/affricates in the standard |
| Aspiration | Contrastive in series such as 幫/滂, 端/透 | Major contrast in stops and affricates |
| Sibilant complexity | Multiple sibilant and retroflex series | Alveolar z/c/s, retroflex zh/ch/sh/r, alveolo-palatal j/q/x |
| Labiodental series | Nontrivial later category: 非敷奉微 | Mandarin has f but lacks a native /v/ phoneme in the standard |
| Sonorants | Nasal, lateral, approximant categories | m, n, l, r plus glides y/w in pinyin analysis |
| Historical function | Conditions later tone-register splits | Modern surface system; older conditioning often opaque |

### 4.4 Middle Chinese Finals: Medial, Nucleus, Coda, Tone

A Chinese syllable in traditional analysis consists of an initial 聲母 and a final 韻母, with tone. The final can include a medial, nucleus, coda, and tone category. Rime dictionaries group characters by finals, while rime tables further classify finals by features such as 開口 / 合口 and 等.

Middle Chinese finals are structurally rich. They include:

| Final component | Examples or categories | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Medial | palatal or labial elements inferred through 等 and 開合 | Explains reflexes such as Mandarin -i-, -u-, -ü-, -iu-, -uo- |
| Main vowel | reconstructed differently by scholars | Core of rhyme-group classification |
| Nasal coda | -m, -n, -ŋ | Preserved differently across modern lects |
| Stop coda | -p, -t, -k | Defines many 入聲 syllables |
| Tone class | 平, 上, 去, 入 | Determines rhyme-book and poetic classification |

The stop codas are the most important for this review. A checked syllable ending in -p, -t, or -k belongs to 入聲 in the Middle Chinese tradition. The checked-tone category is therefore not simply a pitch contour; it is a syllable type involving closure.

### 4.5 Nasal and Stop Codas: The Core Final Contrast

Middle Chinese final codas can be grouped into open/glide, nasal, and stop-final categories. The nasal-stop pairing is especially important:

| Non-entering coda family | Entering counterpart | Traditional implication |
|---|---|---|
| -m | -p | Labial nasal and labial stop families are related in rhyme classification |
| -n | -t | Dental nasal and dental stop families are related |
| -ŋ | -k | Velar nasal and velar stop families are related |

This pairing explains why entering tones were historically classified separately. The final stop syllables did not fit comfortably into the same pitch categories as open or nasal-final syllables.

**Table 4.5. Coda inventory: Middle Chinese versus Mandarin**

| Coda type | Middle Chinese / Qieyun-rime tradition | Modern Standard Mandarin |
|---|---|---|
| Open vowel | yes | yes |
| Glide-like endings | yes, reconstruction-dependent | yes, as part of finals |
| Final -m | yes | no independent final -m |
| Final -n | yes | yes |
| Final -ŋ | yes | yes |
| Final -p | yes, entering tone | no |
| Final -t | yes, entering tone | no |
| Final -k | yes, entering tone | no |
| Final glottal stop | not the classic MC stop system, but appears in some later reflexes | no standard coda |
| Rhotic -r | not central to Qieyun coda classification | yes in erhua and some independent syllables |

This single difference drives many later interpretive problems. In Mandarin, 目 is mù, 國 is guó, 北 is běi, and 八 is bā. None preserves a final stop. In Cantonese, by contrast, these appear as checked forms such as muk6, gwok3, bak1, and baat3. In Hokkien, forms such as ba̍k, kok, pak, and pat preserve checked codas or checked categories.

### 4.6 The Entering Tone 入聲 as a Final-Coda Category

The entering tone must be treated as a final-coda category before it is treated as a tonal category. In classical terminology, 入 is one of the four tones. In phonological terms, however, it reflects checked syllables. This dual identity is why it matters so much for poetry.

**Table 4.6. 入聲 from three perspectives**

| Perspective | What 入 means | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional tone classification | One of 平上去入 | Belongs to 仄 in poetic scansion |
| Segmental phonology | Syllable ending in stop coda, typically -p/-t/-k | Preserved or lost differently across lects |
| Literary reading | A category relevant to rhyme and tone patterning | Cannot be inferred reliably from Mandarin pronunciation |

**Table 4.7. Selected 入聲 characters across systems**

| Character | Meaning | Mandarin | Cantonese | Hokkien / Minnan | Historical point |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 目 | eye | mù | muk6 | ba̍k / bo̍k | Old -k reflex visible outside Mandarin |
| 國 | country | guó | gwok3 | kok | Old -k reflex visible |
| 北 | north | běi | bak1 | pak / pok | Mandarin has lost final -k |
| 八 | eight | bā | baat3 | pat | Old -t reflex visible |
| 十 | ten | shí | sap6 | cha̍p / tsa̍p | Old -p reflex visible |
| 法 | law, method | fǎ | faat3 | hoat / huat | Old checked category visible in non-Mandarin reflexes |

The table should not be read as a direct Middle Chinese reconstruction. It is a reflex table. It shows that modern systems preserve different evidence for older categories.

### 4.7 Middle Chinese Finals and Mandarin Final Compression

Modern Mandarin did not simply delete finals randomly. It reorganized the entire final system. Several broad processes are relevant:

| Historical distinction | Mandarin outcome | Literary consequence |
|---|---|---|
| -p, -t, -k checked codas | Lost | 入 cannot be heard directly |
| -m coda | Merged or transformed; no independent Mandarin final -m | Some rhyme distinctions collapse |
| Complex medial distinctions | Reorganized into Mandarin glides and palatal series | 等 distinctions become indirect |
| Fine rhyme categories | Many merge in modern finals | Traditional rhyme must be looked up |
| Voicing-conditioned tone splits | Reflected only indirectly | Mandarin tone number is not a safe proxy for Middle Chinese category |

The compression is visible in syllable counts as well. Standard Mandarin has a relatively restricted syllable inventory when tone is ignored, and many morphemes become homophonous. This high degree of merger is one reason rhyme and tone categories cannot be reconstructed from Mandarin alone.

### 4.8 Initial-Final Interaction and the Emergence of Mandarin Palatals

One important modern Mandarin innovation is the alveolo-palatal series written in pinyin as j, q, x. These do not correspond simply to one Middle Chinese initial series. They largely arise through later interactions between older velar or dental/sibilant initials and high front or palatal medials.

This matters because rime-table 等 categories are often connected to medial structure. Grade III and Grade IV finals are frequently associated with palatal elements, while Grade I finals tend to lack them. When modern Mandarin shows forms such as liú for 流 but lóu for 樓, the difference partly reflects historical final structure: 流 belongs to a Grade III-type final in the rime-table tradition, while 樓 belongs to a plainer Grade I-type final in many classifications. Both may rhyme in Pingshui under 十一尤, but their deeper rime-table metadata differs.

**Table 4.8. Example of final structure and modern reflex**

| Character | Poetic grouping | Rime-table-level distinction | Mandarin reflex | Interpretive point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 流 | 平水韻 十一尤 | 尤-type, often treated as 開口三等 | liú | Palatal/i-like coloring visible |
| 樓 | 平水韻 十一尤 | 侯-type, often treated as 開口一等 | lóu | Plainer -ou-type reflex |
| 目 | 入聲一屋 | 屋-type, 開口三等入 | mù | Mandarin loses -k; non-Mandarin/Sino-Xenic readings preserve checkedness |

The broader point is that poetic rhyme systems and rime-table systems operate at different levels of granularity. Pingshui may group words for practical poetic rhyme; rime tables preserve finer structural differences.

### 4.9 Why Mandarin Alone Cannot Reconstruct the Older System

**Table 4.9. What Mandarin preserves, obscures, and erases**

| Category | Mandarin status | Example consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Tone as lexical contrast | Preserved | Mandarin has four main lexical tones plus neutral tone |
| Middle Chinese 平/上/去/入 categories | Obscured | Modern tone number does not directly equal old tone class |
| 入聲 codas | Erased segmentally | 目 mù gives no audible -k |
| Voiced obstruents | Erased as voiced obstruents | Old voicing survives only indirectly through tone history |
| Nasal codas -n/-ŋ | Partly preserved | Some older nasal distinctions remain audible |
| Final -m | Lost as independent coda | Some rhyme distinctions collapse |
| Medial distinctions | Reorganized | Some Grade III/IV effects survive indirectly through palatalization or vowel reflexes |

This is why a technical reading of classical poetry must compare Mandarin with other evidence. Cantonese, Minnan, Wu, Sino-Japanese, Sino-Korean, and Sino-Vietnamese readings are not optional curiosities. They are comparative controls against Mandarin merger.

### 4.10 Section Conclusion

Middle Chinese, as inferred from the Qieyun and rime-table traditions, had a richer initial and final system than Modern Standard Mandarin. Its initial categories preserve distinctions of voicing, aspiration, place, and manner that later shaped tonal register splits. Its final categories include nasal and stop codas, with -p, -t, -k forming the segmental basis of 入聲. Modern Mandarin compresses this system, losing final stops and voiced obstruents and reorganizing finals and medials.

The implication for classical poetry is direct. A Mandarin reading may preserve the text and some rhyme effects, but it cannot by itself reveal the full historical structure. To recover rhyme, tone class, checkedness, and medial structure, the reader must use rime dictionaries, rime tables, dialect comparison, and Sino-Xenic evidence.

## 5. Tone Systems: 平上去入, 陰陽, and 平仄

The preceding section treated initials and finals as structural categories in the Middle Chinese system. This section turns to tone, but "tone" here must be handled carefully. The historical categories 平, 上, 去, 入 are not identical with modern Mandarin tone numbers, Cantonese tone numbers, or Minnan tone numbers. They are inherited tone classes used in rime dictionaries, dialectology, and poetic scansion. Later systems split, merge, and redistribute these categories in different ways.

Three related systems must be distinguished:

| System | Chinese terms | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Middle Chinese tone classes | 平 上 去 入 | Historical classification of syllables in rime dictionaries |
| Tonal register split | 陰 / 陽 | Later split often conditioned by earlier initial voicing |
| Poetic binary | 平 / 仄 | Regulated-verse simplification: level vs oblique |

The conceptual error to avoid is equating any one modern tone inventory with Middle Chinese tone classes. Modern Standard Mandarin has four main lexical tones, but these are not simply the four Middle Chinese tones. Cantonese has a richer tone inventory and preserves checked syllables more transparently, but it too is a modern system with its own splits and innovations. Minnan preserves entering categories and has strong tone sandhi, but its surface tones cannot be read directly as static Middle Chinese categories.

### 5.1 平上去入 as Historical Tone Classes

**Table 5.1. The four Middle Chinese tone classes**

| Tone class | Chinese | Conventional English label | Later poetic status | Technical note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 平 | píng | level / even | 平 | Level-tone category; later often split into 陰平 and 陽平 |
| 上 | shǎng | rising | 仄 | Non-level category |
| 去 | qù | departing / going | 仄 | Non-level category |
| 入 | rù | entering / checked | 仄 | Checked syllable category, historically associated with final stops |

The four tone classes should be treated as lexical categories, not as simple pitch contours recoverable from modern Mandarin. Even in Middle Chinese analysis, the relation between tone class and phonetic realization is not straightforward: checked syllables behave differently from open or nasal-final syllables.

### 5.2 入聲 as a Checked-Syllable Category

Among the four tone classes, 入聲 is the most diagnostically important. It is often translated as "entering tone," but this translation can mislead. In phonetic terms, 入 is not merely a pitch contour. It is a category of checked syllables, historically associated with final stops such as -p, -t, -k.

**Table 5.2. Why 入 is not just another pitch tone**

| Aspect | Open / nasal-final tones 平上去 | Entering tone 入 |
|---|---|---|
| Typical syllable shape | vowel-final, glide-final, or nasal-final | stop-final or checked |
| Historical codas | open, -m, -n, -ŋ, etc. | -p, -t, -k |
| Poetic classification | 平 for 平; 仄 for 上/去 | 仄 |
| Modern Mandarin reflex | preserved only indirectly through redistributed tones | stop coda lost; category no longer audible |
| Cantonese reflex | tone categories split and preserved | final -p/-t/-k retained in checked syllables |
| Minnan reflex | tone categories and sandhi interact | entering categories usually preserved as tones 4 and 8 |

This explains why 入聲 repeatedly emerges as the stress test for historical reading. A Mandarin speaker cannot reliably hear that 目 mù, 國 guó, 北 běi, 八 bā, or 十 shí were historically checked. Cantonese muk6, gwok3, bak1, baat3, and sap6 preserve the segmental evidence much more directly. Minnan similarly preserves checked forms such as ba̍k, kok, pak, pat, and cha̍p / tsa̍p, depending on variety and lexical reading.

The implication for poetry is direct: because 入 belongs to 仄, Mandarin pronunciation can obscure the tone-pattern status of a character. A character may sound like a modern Mandarin level or rising tone but still belong historically to 入 and therefore function as 仄 in classical scansion.

### 5.3 From Four Tones to Eight Registers: 陰陽 Splits

The four Middle Chinese tone classes later underwent register splits in many Sinitic lects. These are conventionally labelled 陰 yīn and 陽 yáng, also described in English as "dark/upper" and "light/lower" registers. The conditioning factor was often the voicing of the Middle Chinese initial consonant: syllables with historically voiceless initials tended to develop 陰 reflexes, while those with historically voiced initials tended to develop 陽 reflexes.

**Table 5.3. General route from initial voicing to 陰陽 register**

| Middle Chinese initial type | Traditional label | Typical later register | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voiceless unaspirated | 清 | 陰 | Higher or "dark" register in many systems |
| Voiceless aspirated | 次清 | 陰 | Also normally feeds 陰 |
| Voiced obstruent | 全濁 / 濁 | 陽 | Voicing lost or weakened, leaving tonal register contrast |
| Sonorant | 次濁 | Often 陽 | Frequently patterns with voiced initials, though lect-specific |

If all four tone classes split into two registers, the result is an eight-category system:

| Four-tone base | 陰 reflex | 陽 reflex |
|---|---|---|
| 平 | 陰平 | 陽平 |
| 上 | 陰上 | 陽上 |
| 去 | 陰去 | 陽去 |
| 入 | 陰入 | 陽入 |

This eightfold frame is especially useful for understanding Cantonese and Minnan. Cantonese preserves a highly transparent relationship between older tone classes, register splitting, and checked syllables.

### 5.4 Cantonese as a Clear Example of Tone Class plus Register

Cantonese is pedagogically useful because its traditional tone labels map relatively cleanly onto modern tone numbers. The usual six-tone analysis treats tones 1–6 as the main non-checked tones; traditional descriptions may additionally count entering tones as 7, 8, and 9, corresponding to checked syllables with final -p, -t, -k.

**Table 5.4. Cantonese mapping of historical tone categories**

| Historical category | Cantonese label | Common tone number | Coda type |
|---|---|---|---|
| 平 + 陰 | 陰平 | 1 | open or nasal-final |
| 上 + 陰 | 陰上 | 2 | open or nasal-final |
| 去 + 陰 | 陰去 | 3 | open or nasal-final |
| 平 + 陽 | 陽平 | 4 | open or nasal-final |
| 上 + 陽 | 陽上 | 5 | open or nasal-final |
| 去 + 陽 | 陽去 | 6 | open or nasal-final |
| 入, high / dark | 陰入 | 7 or tone-1 checked | -p, -t, -k |
| 入, mid | 中入 | 8 or tone-3 checked | -p, -t, -k |
| 入, low / light | 陽入 | 9 or tone-6 checked | -p, -t, -k |

The familiar Cantonese mnemonic 詩 史 試 時 市 事 色 錫 食 gives a compact demonstration.

**Table 5.5. Cantonese tone mnemonic and historical mapping**

| Character | Jyutping | Tone number | Historical / traditional category |
|---|---|---|---|
| 詩 | si1 | 1 | 陰平 |
| 史 | si2 | 2 | 陰上 |
| 試 | si3 | 3 | 陰去 |
| 時 | si4 | 4 | 陽平 |
| 市 | si5 | 5 | 陽上 |
| 事 | si6 | 6 | 陽去 |
| 色 | sik1 | 7 / checked 1 | 入, high checked |
| 錫 | sek3 | 8 / checked 3 | 入, mid checked |
| 食 | sik6 | 9 / checked 6 | 入, low checked |

The mnemonic makes visible two principles at once. First, the historical classes have not disappeared: 平, 上, 去, and 入 remain structurally meaningful. Second, entering syllables are distinguished not only by pitch but also by checked coda shape. The final -k in 色 sik1, 錫 sek3, and 食 sik6 makes 入 audible in a way that Mandarin cannot.

### 5.5 Minnan as an Example of Tone Class, Register, and Sandhi

Minnan, especially Taiwanese Hokkien and Xiamen/Amoy Hokkien, also preserves an eight-category traditional tone framework.

**Table 5.6. Traditional Minnan tone categories**

| Tone number | Traditional category | General type |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 陰平 | non-checked |
| 2 | 陰上 | non-checked |
| 3 | 陰去 | non-checked |
| 4 | 陰入 | checked |
| 5 | 陽平 | non-checked |
| 6 | 陽上 | often merged in many modern varieties |
| 7 | 陽去 | non-checked |
| 8 | 陽入 | checked |

Minnan differs from Cantonese in one crucial respect: tone sandhi is central. A syllable's citation tone may change when it appears before another syllable. Therefore, Minnan tone categories are historically and lexically important, but the surface tone heard in connected speech may not be the citation tone.

**Table 5.7. Minnan tone examples in POJ-style notation**

| Tone | Category | Example set | Checked? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 陰平 | 詩 si, 君 kun | no |
| 2 | 陰上 | 死 sí, 滾 kún | no |
| 3 | 陰去 | 四 sì, 棍 kùn | no |
| 4 | 陰入 | 骨 kut, 答 tap | yes |
| 5 | 陽平 | 時 sî, 群 kûn | no |
| 6 | 陽上 | 是 sǐ, 動 tǒng | usually merged in many varieties |
| 7 | 陽去 | 示 sī, 洞 tōng | no |
| 8 | 陽入 | 獨 to̍k, 踏 ta̍p | yes |

The Minnan case prevents a simplistic equation of preservation with transparency. Minnan often preserves checked categories better than Mandarin, but its tone sandhi system means that citation categories and surface pitch are not identical.

### 5.6 Mandarin: Why Modern Tone Numbers Do Not Equal 平上去入

Modern Standard Mandarin has four main lexical tones plus a neutral tone. It is tempting to map them directly onto 平, 上, 去, 入, but this is wrong. Mandarin tone 1 is not simply Middle Chinese 平; Mandarin tone 2 is not simply 上; Mandarin tone 3 is not simply 去; Mandarin tone 4 is not simply 入. The most conspicuous disruption is the loss of 入. Former entering-tone syllables were redistributed among the remaining Mandarin tones after final stops were lost.

**Table 5.8. Former 入 words in Mandarin**

| Character | Historical status | Mandarin | Modern Mandarin tone | Non-Mandarin checked reflex |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 八 | 入 | bā | 1 | Cantonese baat3; Hokkien pat |
| 十 | 入 | shí | 2 | Cantonese sap6; Hokkien cha̍p |
| 北 | 入 | běi | 3 | Cantonese bak1; Hokkien pak/pok |
| 國 | 入 | guó | 2 | Cantonese gwok3; Hokkien kok |
| 目 | 入 | mù | 4 | Cantonese muk6; Hokkien ba̍k |
| 法 | 入 | fǎ | 3 | Cantonese faat3; Hokkien hoat/huat |

This table shows why Mandarin alone cannot determine historical tone class. Former 入 words have no single modern Mandarin tone outcome.

### 5.7 平仄 as Poetic Compression

Classical regulated verse does not use the full complexity of historical phonology directly. It relies on the binary 平 / 仄.

| Historical tone class | Poetic class |
|---|---|
| 平 | 平 |
| 上 | 仄 |
| 去 | 仄 |
| 入 | 仄 |

The binary is simple, but its application is not. The difficulty lies in determining the historical class of each character. Modern Mandarin tone values often mislead. Rime books remain necessary because poetic 平仄 is a historical-literary classification, not a direct reading of modern surface pitch.

**Table 5.9. Why modern tone numbers are unsafe proxies for 平仄**

| Modern lect | Temptation | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Mandarin | Use tones 1–2 as 平 and 3–4 as 仄 | Former 入 words are redistributed; historical 平 may split; modern tone class does not equal MC class |
| Cantonese | Use tone numbers directly | Closer for many cases, but still requires historical category; entering-tone numbering conventions vary |
| Minnan | Use citation tone numbers | Tone sandhi and mergers complicate surface realization |
| Wu | Use surface tone contour | Tone sandhi and phonation/register interactions complicate category mapping |

The correct procedure is not "listen and classify." It is: identify the character's historical tone class through rhyme-book evidence, then interpret how a modern reading realizes or obscures it.

### 5.8 Tone Categories and Rhyme Categories Interact

Tone class and rhyme group are separate but interdependent in rime dictionaries. A rhyme group is not simply a vowel; it is indexed by tone category. In entering-tone categories, stop codas often correspond structurally to nasal codas in non-entering categories.

**Table 5.10. Structural relation between nasal and entering codas**

| Non-entering family | Entering counterpart | Example historical logic |
|---|---|---|
| -m | -p | labial nasal vs labial stop |
| -n | -t | dental nasal vs dental stop |
| -ŋ | -k | velar nasal vs velar stop |

This relation helps explain why 入 is grouped separately while also remaining connected to other final families. Rime tables and reconstructions often treat stop-final and nasal-final series as structurally related. This structure is still visible in many modern southern lects and in Sino-Xenic readings, where -k, -t, and -p reflexes persist.

### 5.9 Implications for Classical Reading

A technically sound reading of classical poetry requires the following distinctions:

| Analytical question | Required category |
|---|---|
| Does this character rhyme with another? | 韻部 / 韻目 |
| Is this character level or oblique in meter? | 平 / 仄 |
| Is this character historically checked? | 入聲 |
| Does a modern reading preserve checkedness? | Dialect reflex, e.g. Cantonese/Minnan/Wu |
| Why does a modern reading differ from another? | Tone split, coda loss, medial structure, sandhi |
| Does external evidence support a final stop? | Sino-Xenic readings |

The practical consequence is that one should not scan a Tang poem by Mandarin tone numbers. Nor should one assume that Cantonese or Hokkien automatically gives the "original" reading. A more disciplined method begins with rime-book classification, then uses modern lects and Sino-Xenic evidence to interpret reflexes.

### 5.10 Section Conclusion

The historical tone system has three layers: 平上去入 as rime-dictionary tone classes, 陰陽 as later register splits conditioned largely by earlier initial voicing, and 平仄 as the poetic binary used in regulated verse. The most important diagnostic category is 入聲, because it ties tone classification to syllable closure and exposes the divergence between Mandarin, southern lects, and Sino-Xenic readings.

Modern Mandarin preserves tone as a lexical system but does not preserve the Middle Chinese four-tone system transparently. Cantonese and Minnan preserve more of the older checked-tone structure, but each does so through its own modern phonology. Classical reading therefore requires historical categories first, modern reflexes second.

## 6. Modern Reflexes: Mandarin, Cantonese, Minnan, and Wu

The historical categories described in Sections 4 and 5 do not survive evenly across modern Sinitic lects. Modern Standard Mandarin, Cantonese, Minnan, and Wu each preserve a different subset of the Middle Chinese system. The most important contrast concerns 入聲: Mandarin generally loses final stop codas and redistributes former entering-tone syllables into its modern tone system, while Cantonese and Minnan preserve checked syllables more visibly, and many Wu varieties preserve checkedness through glottal or stop-like closure.

This section treats modern lects as reflex systems, not as direct survivals of Middle Chinese. A reflex is a modern outcome of an older category. Reflexes can preserve, merge, shift, or obscure the older category. The analytical task is therefore not to ask which modern lect is "closest" to Tang Chinese, but to ask which older distinctions each lect preserves.

### 6.1 The Comparative Frame: Reflex, Not Survival

A modern reading is evidence for historical phonology, but it is never identical with the historical system.

| Step | Question | Evidence type |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What is the historical category? | Qieyun / Guangyun / Pingshui / rime-table data |
| 2 | What is the modern reflex? | Mandarin, Cantonese, Minnan, Wu pronunciation |
| 3 | Is the old contrast preserved, merged, or transformed? | Cross-lect comparison |
| 4 | Does Sino-Xenic evidence support the older final or tone class? | Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese readings |
| 5 | Does the modern reflex help poetic reading? | Rhyme audibility, 入 audibility, 平仄 classification |

This method prevents two common errors. The first is treating Modern Standard Mandarin as the default historical pronunciation. The second is treating Cantonese, Minnan, or Wu as unchanged Middle Chinese. Both are wrong. Mandarin preserves some historical information but loses final stops. Cantonese preserves checked codas but has its own tonal restructuring. Minnan preserves checked categories but complicates them through tone sandhi and reading strata. Wu preserves checkedness in many varieties but often neutralizes older stop-coda distinctions into glottal closure.

### 6.2 Mandarin: Loss of 入 as an Audible Category

Modern Standard Mandarin is phonologically distant from the Middle Chinese checked-tone system in one decisive respect: it lacks final -p, -t, -k. Former entering-tone words survive as lexical items, but they no longer form a segmentally distinct syllable type. They are redistributed across modern Mandarin tones.

**Table 6.1. Former 入聲 words redistributed in Mandarin**

| Character | Historical category | Mandarin | Modern Mandarin tone | Cantonese | Minnan / Hokkien | What Mandarin obscures |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 八 | 入 | bā | 1 | baat3 | pat | final -t |
| 十 | 入 | shí | 2 | sap6 | cha̍p / tsa̍p | final -p |
| 北 | 入 | běi | 3 | bak1 | pak / pok | final -k |
| 國 | 入 | guó | 2 | gwok3 | kok | final -k |
| 目 | 入 | mù | 4 | muk6 | ba̍k / bo̍k | final -k |
| 法 | 入 | fǎ | 3 | faat3 | hoat / huat | checked final |

Modern Mandarin tones are therefore unsafe proxies for classical 平仄. A former 入 word may now have any of the modern Mandarin tones. Since 入 belongs historically to 仄, Mandarin can mislead the reader if tone numbers are used mechanically.

| Mandarin reading strategy | Problem |
|---|---|
| Treat tone 1 and 2 as 平, tone 3 and 4 as 仄 | Incorrect for many historical categories |
| Classify 入 words by modern Mandarin tone | Fails because 入 is redistributed |
| Judge rhyme by Mandarin finals alone | Misses historical rhyme groups and Pingshui categories |
| Use Mandarin as one comparative reflex | Valid, if checked against rime-book categories |

### 6.3 Cantonese: Preservation of Checked Codas

Cantonese is the clearest modern lect discussed here for demonstrating the survival of checked syllables. Standard Cantonese preserves final -p, -t, -k, and these finals correspond closely to the old entering-tone domain.

**Table 6.2. Cantonese checked reflexes**

| Character | Mandarin | Cantonese | Final coda | Historical category |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 十 | shí | sap6 | -p | 入 |
| 八 | bā | baat3 | -t | 入 |
| 北 | běi | bak1 | -k | 入 |
| 國 | guó | gwok3 | -k | 入 |
| 目 | mù | muk6 | -k | 入 |
| 法 | fǎ | faat3 | -t | 入 |

Cantonese also makes the 陰 / 陽 register split highly visible:

| Historical class | Cantonese label | Common tone number |
|---|---|---|
| 平 + 陰 | 陰平 | 1 |
| 上 + 陰 | 陰上 | 2 |
| 去 + 陰 | 陰去 | 3 |
| 平 + 陽 | 陽平 | 4 |
| 上 + 陽 | 陽上 | 5 |
| 去 + 陽 | 陽去 | 6 |
| 入, high / dark | 陰入 | 7 or checked 1 |
| 入, mid | 中入 | 8 or checked 3 |
| 入, low / light | 陽入 | 9 or checked 6 |

The Cantonese system shows why the phrase "preserving Middle Chinese" is tempting but imprecise. Cantonese preserves final stops and many tone-category distinctions better than Mandarin, but its tone values and distributions are modern Cantonese outcomes. It is evidence, not the original system.

### 6.4 Minnan / Hokkien: Checked Categories, Tone Sandhi, and Reading Strata

Minnan is especially useful because it preserves checked categories while also showing why preservation does not equal simplicity.

| Tone number | Traditional category | Type |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 陰平 | non-checked |
| 2 | 陰上 | non-checked |
| 3 | 陰去 | non-checked |
| 4 | 陰入 | checked |
| 5 | 陽平 | non-checked |
| 6 | 陽上 | often merged |
| 7 | 陽去 | non-checked |
| 8 | 陽入 | checked |

The checked tones are 4 and 8. Minnan adds two complications. First, tone sandhi is pervasive: a syllable's citation tone changes depending on its position in a phrase. Second, Minnan has multiple reading layers, often described as literary 文讀 and colloquial 白讀 strata.

**Table 6.3. Minnan checked examples**

| Character | Mandarin | Cantonese | Minnan / Hokkien | Checked coda | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 十 | shí | sap6 | cha̍p / tsa̍p | -p | common checked reflex |
| 八 | bā | baat3 | pat | -t | clear entering reflex |
| 北 | běi | bak1 | pak / pok | -k | old -k preserved |
| 國 | guó | gwok3 | kok | -k | old -k preserved |
| 目 | mù | muk6 | ba̍k / bo̍k | -k | strong 入 example |
| 獨 | dú | duk6 | to̍k | -k | 陽入 example |

Taipei Taiwanese Hokkien and Xiamen/Amoy Hokkien share the traditional tone-category apparatus, but their phonetic realization and mergers differ:

| Feature | Taipei Taiwanese Hokkien | Xiamen / Amoy Hokkien |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional tone numbering | 1–8 framework used | 1–8 framework used |
| Checked tones | 4 and 8 | 4 and 8 |
| Tone 6 | often merged | often merged |
| Tone sandhi | central to phrase phonology | central to phrase phonology |
| Literary/colloquial strata | important | important |

Minnan therefore preserves 入 more visibly than Mandarin but requires more caution than Cantonese because surface pitch is strongly affected by sandhi.

### 6.5 Wu: Checkedness Through Glottal or Stop-Like Closure

Wu lects, including Shanghainese and related Lower Yangtze varieties, occupy a different position in the comparison. Many Wu varieties preserve checked syllables, but often not by maintaining all three Middle Chinese stop codas -p, -t, -k as separate final consonants. Instead, older checked codas may be reflected as a glottal stop or glottalized syllable closure.

**Table 6.4. Wu-type checked reflex pattern**

| Historical category | Cantonese-type outcome | Wu-type outcome | Mandarin outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| -p entering | -p | glottal / checked closure in many varieties | lost |
| -t entering | -t | glottal / checked closure in many varieties | lost |
| -k entering | -k | glottal / checked closure in many varieties | lost |
| 入 as category | preserved with stop codas | often preserved as checkedness | lost as distinct category |

Wu also has strong interactions among voicing, phonation, tone, and tone sandhi. In some descriptions, the 陰 / 陽 split remains connected to voiceless versus voiced or breathy-initial categories more transparently than in Mandarin.

### 6.6 North–South Split: A Useful but Imperfect Heuristic

**Table 6.5. North–south heuristic for 入聲**

| Macro-pattern | Typical lects | 入聲 outcome | Coda outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Northern / Mandarin-type | Standard Mandarin, many Mandarin dialects | 入 lost as separate category | final stops lost |
| Lower Yangtze / Wu-type | Wu varieties | checkedness often preserved | glottal or stop-like closure |
| Far southern / Yue-type | Cantonese | 入 robustly preserved | -p, -t, -k preserved |
| Southeastern / Min-type | Hokkien, Taiwanese, Amoy | 入 preserved | -p, -t, -k and/or glottal reflexes, depending on variety |

The safer statement is: modern Sinitic lects form a spectrum of reflexes. Mandarin generally erases the segmental basis of 入; Cantonese and Minnan often preserve it; Wu frequently transforms it into glottalized checkedness.

### 6.8 Implications for Reading Classical Poetry

Modern lect reflexes affect literary reading in three main ways. First, rhyme audibility differs. A pair of words that rhyme historically may or may not sound close in a modern lect. Second, 入 audibility differs. Mandarin usually cannot signal entering-tone status by sound. Cantonese and Minnan frequently can. Third, 平仄 classification cannot be derived mechanically from modern tone values.

**Table 6.7. Modern lect usefulness for classical reading**

| Lect / system | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Mandarin | widely standardized; useful for modern access | loses 入 codas; modern tones mislead for 平仄 |
| Cantonese | preserves -p/-t/-k; clear 入 audibility | still modern; tone numbering conventions vary |
| Minnan | preserves checked categories and older strata | tone sandhi and multiple readings complicate analysis |
| Wu | preserves checkedness and register/phonation clues | strong sandhi; coda place often neutralized |
| Sino-Xenic | external evidence for historical finals | borrowing strata and language adaptation complicate direct use |

The practical conclusion is that modern lects should be used comparatively. The historical category remains primary.

### 6.9 Section Conclusion

Modern reflexes reveal why historical phonology matters for literary reading. Mandarin has lost the segmental basis of 入聲, making many entering-tone words opaque by ear. Cantonese preserves final -p, -t, -k and therefore makes checked syllables highly audible. Minnan preserves entering categories but complicates them through tone sandhi and reading strata. Wu frequently preserves checkedness through glottal or stop-like closure. None of these systems is identical to Middle Chinese; each preserves and transforms different parts of the older structure.
