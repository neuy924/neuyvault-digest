The discourse mapped these terms across a timeline spanning from the Pre-Qín era to the late Qīng Dynasty, utilizing a multi-lens matrix to deconstruct how language codifies civilizational boundaries, imperial management, and the transition into the modern nation-state era.

### Key Topics and Taxonomic Evolution

A primary focus was the character **番**, which originated as a pictograph of a beast’s footprint on a field (**采** over **田**). Its semantic trajectory shifted from agricultural periodicity and "taking turns" to a taxonomic marker for the "Outer Other." In the Míng and Qīng dynasties, this prefix was applied to both indigenous peoples (the "Raw/Cooked" **生/熟** distinction) and New World botanical resources (**番茄**, **番薯**). In vernacular contexts like Hokkien and Cantonese, **番** remains active as a linguistic fossil, preserving colonial-era hierarchies and ethnic slurs like **番仔 (huan-á)** and **番鬼 (fan-gwai)**.

The character **夷** was identified as the ontological counterweight to **华 (Huá)**. Structurally a "man with a bow," it transitioned from an ethnographic descriptor of Eastern tribes to a universal label for "Barbarians." The 1858 **Treaty of Tientsin** served as a critical catalyst for its demise; Article 51 explicitly forbade the use of **夷** in official British documents, forcing a shift toward **洋 (Yáng)** —a term that spatialized the "Other" as an oceanic, rather than animalistic, power.

The discussion of **胡** and **回** highlighted a shift from biological to ideological categorization. **胡** (originally signifying a "dewlap") was a phenotypical marker for bearded Central Asians. As Islam became the defining trait of these groups, the ethnonym for the Uyghurs (**回鶻**) was repurposed into **回**, moving the "Other" into a religious category.

### Major Themes

**1\. Spatial and Cosmological Mapping:**The **Sìyí (四夷)** system illustrated how the Sinitic center used cardinal directions (**东夷, 西戎, 北狄, 南蛮**) to create a "Moral Geography." This quadrant-based exclusion ensured that humanity was defined by its proximity to the ritualized center.

**2\. Imperial Pragmatism and the "Buffer" Class:**Under the **Yuán Dynasty**, the Mongols inverted traditional hierarchies by installing the **Sèmù (色目)** —including figures like Marco Polo—as an administrative buffer between the Mongol elite and the Han majority. The subsequent **Míng** restoration attempted to dismantle this via forced Sinicization, while the **Qīng** later re-emphasized ethnic difference to maintain a "Mosaic Empire."

**3\. Transcultural Hybridization:**The analysis of the **Peranakan** (Baba Nyonya) community and the **Ryūkyū Kingdom** demonstrated "liminal taxonomies." These groups functioned as middle-men, where the **番** label was either reclaimed as a hybrid identity or utilized performatively to navigate the competing demands of the Sinitic and Japanese world orders.

## I. Introduction: The Semiotics of the "Middle Kingdom"

The conceptualization of identity within the Sinitic world-view has historically been predicated not upon immutable racial categories, but upon a sophisticated, diachronic system of **Taxonomic Alterity**. At the core of this system lies the **Huá-Yí Zhī Biàn** (华夷之辨), or the "Distinction between the Chinese and the Barbarians." This was not a mere binary of "Self" versus "Other," but a comprehensive semiotic framework that organized the known world into a moral and ritual hierarchy. To understand the evolution of terms such as **番** (*fān*), **夷** (*yí*), and **胡** (*hú*), one must first recognize the underlying logic of the **Middle Kingdom** (中国 - *Zhōngguó*) as a civilization defined by its adherence to **Lǐ** (礼 - Ritual/Propriety).

### 1.1 Defining the Huá-Yí (华夷) Distinction

The **Huá-Yí** distinction served as the foundational pillar of Sinitic political and cosmological thought. The term **Huá** (华), often paired with **Xià** (夏), denotes a "flowering" or "radiant" civilization, symbolizing the peak of human ethical cultivation. Conversely, **Yí** (夷) represented the "uncooked" or "unrefined" periphery. Unlike the Western Westphalian concept of nation-states defined by borders, the Sinitic world defined itself through **concentricity**.

In this system, "Chineseness" was a performative attribute rather than a biological one. A group could "enter" the realm of the **Huá** by adopting Confucian rituals, the Chinese script, and filial structures—a process known as **Xiànghuà** (向化 - "turning toward transformation"). This created a permeable membrane between the center and the periphery, where the linguistic labels used for "Others" acted as progress reports on their level of "civilization." The primary mechanism of exclusion was not a lack of shared blood, but a lack of shared **Lǐ**.

### 1.2 The Lexical Toolkit: Primary Graphemes

The Sinitic lexicon utilized a specialized set of graphemes to categorize external populations, each carrying a unique etymological "charge." These terms were rarely neutral; they functioned as **ontological markers** that assigned a specific nature to the group in question.

| Grapheme | Native Script | Original Etymological Root | Primary Connotation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Yí** | **夷** | Man with a bow (**大 + 弓**) | The Eastern "Other"; the archer. |
| **Fān** | **番** | Beast footprint on a field (**采 + 田**) | Periodicity; the "alternating" or "foreign" peripheral. |
| **Hú** | **胡** | Dewlap/Animal throat flesh (**肉/月**) | Hirsuteness; phenotypical anomaly (the bearded Westerner). |
| **Mán** | **蛮** | Insect/Worm radical (**虫**) | The Southern "Others"; perceived as "reptilian" or raw. |
| **Dí** | **狄** | Dog radical (**犭**) | The Northern "Others"; perceived as predatory/animalistic. |

These characters were not static. Over millennia, a term like **番** transitioned from a physical description of a track in the soil to a functional label for maritime merchants and New World flora. Similarly, **胡** moved from a specific label for the **Xiōngnú** (匈奴) to a fashionable brand for Central Asian exotics during the **Táng Dynasty** (唐朝). The "Toolkit" was adaptive, allowing the imperial bureaucracy to re-brand the "Other" as they shifted from military threats to tributary partners.

### 1.3 Scope and Methodology: Agricultural to Geopolitical Taxonomy

This review examines the shift in these taxonomies as the Sinitic world-view moved from a land-based, agricultural society to an integrated maritime power. The earliest "Othering" was deeply rooted in the **agricultural gaze** —viewing the nomad or the forest-dweller as an "animal" because they stood outside the regulated cycles of the **Tián** (田 - field). This resulted in an **Orthographic Dehumanization**, where the inclusion of animal radicals in a name was a deliberate act of civilizational ranking.

However, as the **Silk Road** and maritime trade routes expanded, this "Animal Taxonomy" proved insufficient. The presence of sophisticated "Others"—such as the **Sèmù** (色目) administrators of the **Yuán Dynasty** (元朝) or the oceanic powers of the late **Qīng** (清)—forced a **Geopolitical Re-orientation**. The taxonomy shifted from the biological (**番/夷**) to the spatial (**洋** - *yáng* / "Oceanic"). By deconstructing these shifts, we can map the transition of the Sinitic state from a universal empire into a modern participant in a global, albeit Westphalian, system. The following sections will detail how these specific graphemes mediated the empire's encounter with the "Other" across its long and turbulent history.

## II. Foundations of Exclusion: The Quadrant System (Sìyí)

The structural organization of alterity in early Sinitic thought was not an amorphous prejudice but a geometric mandate. Central to the state’s ontological security was the **Sìyí** (四夷) system, a taxonomic quadrant that partitioned the known world into four distinct categories of "Otherness" based on cardinal orientation. This system functioned as a cartographic extension of the **Huá-Yí** distinction, ensuring that every non-Sinitic group was assigned a fixed location within a cosmic hierarchy. This section deconstructs the spatial logic of these quadrants and the orthographic mechanisms used to codify the perceived sub-human status of those residing within them.

### 2.1 Geometric Cartography: The Concentric Square Model

The **Sìyí** system was predicated on the **Wǔfú** (五服) or "Five Zones" of imperial influence, a model of concentric squares emanating from the **Wángjì** (王畿—the Royal Domain). In this cosmological map, the "Radiant Center" was surrounded by increasingly "diluted" zones of ritual observance. The further a population resided from the center, the more their humanity was deemed to have eroded into the natural world.

The four cardinal labels— **Dōngyí** (东夷), **Xīróng** (西戎), **Běidí** (北狄), and **Nánmán** (南蛮)—were not merely geographical markers; they were "states of being." The Sinitic center functioned as the gravitational pivot, and the **Sìyí** functioned as the "Wilderness" (**Huāng** 荒) that bounded it. This spatiality created a "Moral Compass" where the quality of a person was determined by the coordinates of their birth relative to the Emperor’s throne.

### 2.2 Orthographic Dehumanization: Zoomorphic Radicals

A defining feature of the **Sìyí** system was its use of **Orthographic Dehumanization**. Sinitic scribes and philologists utilized specific radicals to embed the perceived nature of the "Other" into the very structure of their names. This was a form of "Linguistic Enclosure," where the script itself performed the act of civilizational ranking.

| Quadrant | Native Script | Primary Radical | Taxonomic Implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Běidí** (North) | **北狄** | **犭** (Dog) | Predatory, nomadic, pack-oriented; lacking filial piety. |
| **Nánmán** (South) | **南蛮** | **虫** (Insect/Snake) | Reptilian, unrefined, associated with "poisonous" environments. |
| **Xīróng** (West) | **西戎** | **戈** (Halberd/Weapon) | Martial, violent, characterized by metal and warfare. |
| **Dōngyí** (East) | **东夷** | **弓** (Bow) | Technological but non-ritualistic; defined by the tool. |

The inclusion of the dog radical (**犭**) in **Dí** (狄) or the insect/worm radical (**虫**) in **Mán** (蛮) served to ontologically distance these groups from the "Human" (**Rén** 人) status of the **Huá**. This zoomorphism suggested that while these groups might possess human speech or form, their essential spirit was animalistic. This made "othering" a biological fact within the Chinese writing system, requiring no further explanation beyond the stroke of a brush.

### 2.3 The "Bow-Man" (夷): The Archetypal Eastern Other

Among the four, the **Dōngyí** (东夷) occupied a unique position in early texts such as the *Analects* (**Lúnyǔ** 论语) and the *Classic of History* (**Shàngshū** 尚书). The character **Yí** (夷) is composed of **大** (man) and **弓** (bow). Unlike the zoomorphic labels of the North and South, the **Yí** were defined by a technological attribute. Historically, this referred to the tribes of the East (modern-day Shāndōng and Jiāngsū).

Because the **Yí** were geographically and culturally closer to the center than the "Dogs" of the North or "Insects" of the South, they were often viewed as the "most civilizable" of the barbarians. Confucius famously mused about living among the **Nine Yí** (九夷), suggesting that if a man of virtue lived there, their "savagery" would be transformed. This established a normative precedent: the **Yí** were the threshold category. They represented the outer limit of the human world, standing just before the absolute dehumanization of the more distant animalistic quadrants. However, as the Sinitic state expanded, **Yí** lost its specific eastern association and became a universal, derogatory label for any defiant foreign power, a status it maintained until the linguistic purges of the late 19th century.

## III. The Agricultural Root: The Evolution of 番 (Fān)

While the **Sìyí** (四夷) system provided a static, cardinal framework for exclusion, the character **番** (*fān*) introduced a dynamic, process-oriented dimension to Sinitic alterity. Originally detached from ethnic labeling, the semantic journey of **番** tracks the transition from the physical observation of the natural world to the administrative management of the "Other." To understand the role of **番** in the imperial imagination, one must deconstruct its etymological roots, which are grounded not in the human social order, but in the tactile reality of the agricultural field.

### 3.1 Etymological Origins: From "Beast Footprint" to Periodicity

The philological foundation of **番**, as recorded in the *Shuōwén Jiézì* (说文解字), is ideographic. The character is composed of **采** (originally depicting a claw or a hand picking) over **田** (a field). In its primal state, it signified the "paw of a beast" (兽足象形). This established a cognitive association between the "Other" and the act of **tracking**. The field was the site of civilization; the footprint was the evidence of the wild's intrusion.

However, through a process of metonymic drift, the focus shifted from the footprint itself to the **repetitive nature** of the steps. In early texts such as the *Shangshu* (尚书), **番** began to signify "alternation," "turn," or "succession." This "Succession Logic" became a technical mechanism for the state to organize labor and military rotations (**番役** - *fānyì*).

| Semantic Stage | Logic | Application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Palaeographic** | Physical Mark | The beast’s track in the soil. |
| **Administrative** | Periodicity | Rotational guard duty; taking "turns." |
| **Taxonomic** | Alternation | The "Other" as a periodic, peripheral intrusion. |

### 3.2 The Shift to the Periphery: The "Alternating" Other

The transition of **番** from a verb of rotation to a noun of ethnicity occurred as the Sinitic center encountered groups that existed outside the sedentary, ritualized core. These were the "alternating" peoples—nomadic or mountain-dwelling tribes whose presence was perceived as a periodic ebb and flow against the imperial borders. Unlike **夷** (*yí*), which implied a direct ontological rival, **番** carried a sense of **jurisdictional distance**.

By the Táng (唐) and Sòng (宋) dynasties, **番** (often interchangeable with **蕃**) became a primary label for the Western and Southwestern frontiers, most notably the **Tǔfān** (吐蕃 - Tibetans). In this context, the "Fān" were those who were "outside the gate" but within the sphere of imperial tracking. They were categorized not by their specific culture, but by their position as a "rotational" force that the empire had to periodically pacified or trade with.

### 3.3 The "Raw" and the "Cooked" (生/熟): Culinary Metaphors of Integration

By the Qīng Dynasty (清朝), the character **番** reached its most sophisticated taxonomic application through the binary of **Shēng** (生 - Raw) and **Shú** (熟 - Cooked/Ripe). This culinary metaphor served as a progress report on the "transformation" (**向化** - *xiànghuà*) of indigenous and peripheral peoples, particularly in the colonization of Taiwan and the Southwestern frontiers.

- **Shēngfān (生番 - "Raw Barbarians"):** Those who remained outside imperial taxation and ritual. They were "uncooked" by Confucian culture, often residing in "wild" mountains. In the Sinitic gaze, they were closer to the original "beast footprint" etymology—unpredictable and untamed.
- **Shúfān (熟番 - "Cooked Barbarians"):** Those who had "submitted" (*guīfù* 归附), paid taxes, and adopted Sinitic agricultural practices. They were "ripened" or "processed" by the state.

This "Culinary Taxonomy" was an essential tool of **Internal Colonialism**. It allowed the state to justify different levels of violence: the "Cooked" were to be protected and managed, while the "Raw" were to be "processed" or contained. The character **番** thus functioned as a linguistic gauge for civilizational digestion, tracking the "Other" as they moved from the wild footprint in the field to a tax-paying subject within the imperial fold.

## IV. Phenotype and the Silk Road: The Hú (胡) and the West

If the character **番** (*fān*) represented a taxonomy of agricultural and jurisdictional distance, the character **胡** (*hú*) introduced a somatic dimension to Sinitic alterity. While earlier quadrants like the **Sìyí** (四夷) focused on directional and ritual exclusion, the **Hú** taxonomy was fundamentally grounded in **phenotypical anomaly**. As the Sinitic world-view expanded westward along the Silk Road, the "Other" was no longer merely a "beast footprint" in the field, but a high-nosed, deep-eyed, and profusely bearded subject whose very physicality challenged the aesthetic and ritual norms of the Central Plains (**Zhōngyuán** 中原).

### 4.1 Anatomical Taxonomy: The "Dewlap" and Hirsuteness

The philological root of **胡**, as deconstructed in the *Shuōwén Jiézì* (说文解字), is purely anatomical. The character is a phonosemantic compound comprising **古** (*gǔ* - ancient/phonetic) and **肉/月** (*ròu* - flesh/radical). Its primary meaning denotes the "dewlap"—the sagging, fleshy skin beneath the chin of an animal or an elderly person.

This primal association with "excessive flesh" or "pendulous hair" became the cognitive anchor for labeling Western nomads and Central Asians. To the relatively smooth-faced Han population, the heavy beards of the **Xiōngnú** (匈奴) and later the Sogdians were their most salient "Othering" feature. Thus, the **Hú** were not merely defined by their location in the North or West, but by a visceral, tactile "strangeness." The label was a biological marker; to be **Hú** was to possess a body that was physically "noisy" and aesthetically deviant from the Confucian ideal of refined, moderated appearance.

### 4.2 The Táng Cosmopolitanism: The "Exotic Peak" (Húfēng 胡风)

During the **Táng Dynasty** (唐朝), the **Hú** identity underwent a radical semiotic transformation. No longer just a martial threat on the frontier, the "Hú" became a brand of elite cosmopolitanism. This period represents the "Exotic Peak" of Sinitic history, where the "Other" was fetishized as a source of cultural novelty and sensory delight.

| Domain | **Hú** -Prefixed Innovation | Cultural Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Diet** | **Húbǐng** (胡饼) | Introduction of sesame seeds and Western-style oven-baked bread. |
| **Fashion** | **Húfú** (胡服) | Adoption of tight-sleeved tunics and boots, replacing bulky robes. |
| **Music** | **Húqín** (胡琴) | Evolution of Sinitic string instruments via Central Asian influence. |
| **Botany** | **Húluóbo** (胡萝卜) | The "Hú Radish" (carrot), marking the import of Western crops. |

The concept of **Húfēng** (胡风 - "Hú Style") permeated the streets of Cháng'ān. The **Hú** were the Sogdian merchants, the Persian silversmiths, and the Tocharian musicians. In this era, the taxonomy shifted from "Barbarian" to "Exotic Partner." The "hirsuteness" that was once a marker of the "dewlap" was now a symbol of the sophisticated, interconnected world of the Silk Road. The **Hú** provided the Táng elite with a "Mirror of Sophistication," allowing them to view themselves as the masters of a global, multi-ethnic civilization.

### 4.3 The An Lùshān Trauma: From Ally to Traitor

The romanticization of the **Hú** was violently terminated by the **An Lùshān Rebellion** (安史之乱) in 755 AD. **An Lùshān** (安禄山), a general of Sogdian and Turkic "Hú" descent, nearly toppled the Táng state, leading to a catastrophic normative shift in the Sinitic perception of the Western "Other."

Overnight, the "fashionable" **Hú** was re-categorized as a "dangerous parasite." The phenotypical traits that were once celebrated—the beard, the deep eyes—were now marks of inherent treachery. This trauma resulted in horrific anti-foreign purges, such as the massacre of thousands of **Hú** and **Sèmù** (色目) merchants in Yángzhōu. The taxonomy of the **Hú** moved from the bazaar to the battlefield; it was no longer a label for a "merchant with a song," but for a "wolf with a hidden blade." This defensive hardening of the **Hú** identity set the stage for the more rigid ethnic stratifications of the later dynasties, where the "West" would forever be viewed through a lens of suspicion and "barbarian" volatility.

## V. From Body to Belief: The Transition to Huí (回)

The trajectory of Sinitic alterity underwent a profound shift during the late Sòng (宋) and Yuán (元) periods, transitioning from a taxonomy of **physiology** to a taxonomy of **ideology**. While the character **胡** (*hú*) had successfully categorized the Western "Other" through the lens of phenotypical anomaly—specifically hirsuteness and "dewlap" anatomy—the arrival of Islam along the Silk Road necessitated a more sophisticated nomenclature. This era saw the emergence of the character **回** (*huí*) as the primary signifier for the Islamic world, marking the moment when the "Westerner" was redefined not by the shape of his face, but by the direction of his prayer.

### 5.1 Phonetic Displacement: From Huíhú to Huíhuí

The etymological origin of the modern term for Chinese Muslims, **Huí** (回), is rooted in a process of phonetic contraction and semantic expansion. Originally, the term was used to transcribe the ethnonym of the **Huíhú** (回鶻) or **Huíhé** (回紇)—the Uyghur Khaganate of the 8th and 9th centuries.

During the Mongol-Yuán era, as Central Asian Muslims were mobilized across the empire as part of the **Sèmù** (色目) administrative class, the term **Huíhuí** (回回) was deployed as a universal "catch-all" label. This was a **Taxonomic Displacement**: the specific Turkic ethnonym was "hollowed out" and repurposed to encompass a diverse array of Persians, Arabs, and Turks who shared a common faith. By the 13th century, the anatomical specificity of the **胡** (Hú) had been subsumed by the categorical generality of the **回** (Huí).

### 5.2 Ideological Taxonomy: The Logic of "Returning"

A disruptive analysis of the character **回** reveals why it was so successfully absorbed into the Sinitic world-view. Structurally, **回** is a pictograph of a circle within a circle, signifying "to return," "to revolve," or "to turn back."

| Concept | Native Script | Sinitic Interpretation | Islamic Contextualization |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Identity** | **回回** (Huíhuí) | The "Returning" people. | Adherents of the "Turned-West" faith. |
| **Theology** | **回真** (Huízhēn) | "Returning to the Truth." | The concept of *Tawhid* (Unity of God). |
| **Origin** | **回邦** (Huíbāng) | "The Returning Nations." | The Islamic Heartland (Central Asia/Middle East). |

Unlike the animalistic or somatic radicals used in **狄** (dog) or **胡** (flesh), **回** is an abstract, geometric character. This "neutrality" allowed Islamic scholars in China—particularly the **Hàn Kǐtāb** (汉克塔布) tradition—to re-interpret their "Otherness" as a spiritual virtue. They argued that to be **回** was to "return to the primordial nature" (*fǎn-běn-guī-yuán* 返本还原). This shifted the taxonomic focus from the **Body** (the bearded *Hú*) to the **Path** (the observant *Huí*). It was the first instance of a foreign group successfully negotiating a non-derogatory, behavior-based label within the Sinitic system.

### 5.3 The Qīngzhēn (清真) Shield: The "Pure and True" Subject

As the transition finalized during the Míng Dynasty (明朝), the **Huí** community adopted the term **Qīngzhēn** (清真 - "Pure and True") to define their ritual space and dietary laws. This was a strategic act of **Taxonomic Masking**. By using a term with deep roots in Daoist and Sinitic philosophical purity, the **Huí** transformed their "foreign" Islamic habits into a recognized form of "High Sinitic" virtue.

This "Halal Taxonomy" replaced the exoticism of the Silk Road with the discipline of the domestic subject. The **Huí** were no longer "Barbarians" (*Fān*) or "Westerners" (*Hú*); they were the "Pure and True" subjects who resided within the empire but maintained a separate ritual cycle. However, this categorization carried a dual edge: while it provided a "shield" of Sinitic legitimacy, it also "hardened" the boundary between the **Huí** and the **Hàn** (汉), creating a permanent "Internal Other" defined by what they did *not* eat and how they did *not* conform to the local ancestral cults. This tension would eventually lead to the ethnic polarizations of the late Qīng era.

## VI. Imperial Management: The Yuán Stratification

The establishment of the **Yuán Dynasty** (元朝) by the Mongols represented a seismic "Taxonomic Inversion" in the history of East Asian political thought. For the first time, the "Other"—specifically the nomadic "Barbarian" of the **Běidí** (北狄) quadrant—had seized the Sinitic center and repurposed its administrative machinery. To govern a vast, sedentary Han majority, the Mongol elite abandoned the traditional **Huá-Yí** (华夷) binary in favor of a rigid, four-tiered social hierarchy known as the **Sì-děng-rén** (四等人). This system was not merely a tool of racial segregation; it was a sophisticated mechanism of **Imperial Management** that utilized a third-party "buffer" class to insulate the Mongol rulers from their Han subjects.

### 6.1 The Sèmù (色目) Buffer: The Taxonomy of Utility

At the heart of this hierarchy was the **Sèmù** (色目人— *Sèmùrén*) class. Often translated as "Color-eyed People," the term likely referred to "Various Categories," signifying a heterogeneous group of Western and Central Asians, including Persians, Arabs, Turks, and Europeans. By elevating these "Outer Foreigners" to the second tier of the imperial hierarchy, the Mongols created a loyal middle-management class.

The **Sèmù** functioned as a **Taxonomic Buffer**. Lacking a local land-base or Confucian lineage in China, their survival was entirely dependent on the Khan's patronage. They were deployed as **Dàrwùhuāchì** (达鲁花赤—resident overseers) in local governments, ensuring that tax collection and military recruitment remained in "Foreign" hands. This effectively displaced the Han literati from their traditional role as the state's administrative backbone, transforming the "Other" into the empire's primary technical and mercantile resource.

### 6.2 The Four-Class System: Legal and Fiscal Stratification

The **Sì-děng-rén** system institutionalized inequality through a series of legal and fiscal codes that determined one’s "value" within the state. The taxonomy moved from the cosmological (where one stood in the universe) to the bureaucratic (what one's life was worth in silver).

| Rank | Native Script | Ethnic Grouping | Administrative Role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **I** | **蒙古人** (Měnggǔrén) | Mongols | Ruling elite; military command. |
| **II** | **色目人** (Sèmùrén) | Central Asians/Westerners | Middle-management; tax farming; technology. |
| **III** | **汉人** (Hànrén) | Northern Chinese/Khitan/Jurchen | Subaltern subjects; former Jin subjects. |
| **IV** | **南人** (Nánrén) | Southern Chinese (Manzi) | Lowest tier; former Song subjects; heavy taxation. |

The most visceral dehumanization was reserved for the **Nánrén** (南人—Southern People), who were frequently referred to as **Mánzi** (蛮子—Southern Barbarians). In a radical linguistic reversal, the "Civilized Center" of the former Southern Sòng was now categorized as the most primitive "Other." This served a dual purpose: it punished the most resilient Han resistance and provided a psychological "floor" for the **Sèmù** and Mongols, reinforcing the stability of the conquest through collective degradation of the majority.

### 6.3 Marco Polo and the "Feringhi" as Sèmù

A critical case study in the flexibility of this system is the categorization of **Marco Polo** and other Europeans (the "Franks" or **Feringhi**). During his seventeen-year tenure, Polo functioned as a **Sèmù** official. To the Mongol court, his European origin was less important than his status as a non-aligned "Other" with specialized observational skills.

Polo served as a **Seche** (envoy) for Khubilai Khan, a role that allowed him to bypass Han local authorities. His outsider status made him the perfect "eye" for the Khan; as a **Sèmù**, he could report on the conduct of provincial governors without the bias of local clan affiliations. This highlights the **Functional Taxonomy** of the Yuán: "Otherness" was no longer a sign of savagery to be transformed, but a specialized professional qualification. The more "Foreign" an official was, the more he was trusted to manage the "Native" population, ensuring that the levers of power remained in the hands of the **Sèmù** buffer.

## VII. The Míng-Qīng Dialectic: Sinicization vs. Pluralism

The collapse of the Yuán (元) and the subsequent rise of the Míng (明) and Qīng (清) dynasties represent a fundamental dialectic in the management of Sinitic alterity. This period saw a transition from the forced cultural homogeneity of the Míng’s **Hànhuà** (汉化—Sinicization) to the Qīng’s "Mosaic Imperialism," which re-emphasized ethnic differentiation. While the Míng sought to digest the "Other" into a singular Confucian body to secure a Han restoration, the Qīng utilized a policy of **Preserved Difference** to maintain the legitimacy of a Manchu minority ruling over a diverse, continental empire.

### 7.1 Míng Restorationism: The Liquidation of the Sèmù

The Míng founder, **Zhū Yuánzhāng** (朱元璋), viewed the Yuán’s multi-ethnic stratification as a biological and ritual "pollution" of the Central Plains. His restorationist project was predicated on **Dà Yìtǒng** (大一统—Great Uniformity), a policy that demanded the systematic dismantling of the **Sèmù** (色目) identity. This was executed through the **Dà Míng Lǜ** (大明律—Great Ming Code), which utilized legal mandates to enforce cultural assimilation.

Specifically, Article 122 of the Míng Code mandated exogamy for Mongols and **Sèmù**, requiring them to marry Han subjects under threat of punishment. By banning "barbarian" languages, clothing, and surnames, the Míng state performed a **Taxonomic Liquidation**. The privileged Central Asian administrators of the previous era were re-categorized as **Guīfù** (归附—Naturalized) or subsumed into the religious category of **Huí** (回). In this model, "Otherness" was a temporary defect to be cured through the corrective power of Confucian ritual and biological dilution.

### 7.2 Qīng Mosaic Imperialism: The Re-emphasis of Difference

The Qīng Dynasty, established by the Manchus (满洲), fundamentally inverted the Míng’s assimilationist logic. As a minority conquering elite, the Manchus recognized that a completely Sinicized empire would leave them as an exposed anomaly. Consequently, they adopted a **Multimodal Sovereignty**, where the Emperor functioned as the Son of Heaven to the Han, the Great Khan to the Mongols, and the Cakravartin (Universal Ruler) to the Tibetans.

The Qīng established the **Eight Banners** (**八旗—Bāqí**) as a hereditary military caste, strictly segregated from the Han civilian population to preserve "Manchu Purity." They famously mandated the **Queue** (**剃发令**) for Han men, a performative reversal of Míng policy where the "Center" was forced to adopt the "Other’s" aesthetic. By codifying ethnic boundaries rather than erasing them, the Qīng created a stable, pluralistic hierarchy where the "Otherness" of each group was a permanent, state-managed resource.

### 7.3 The Lifān Yuàn (理藩院): Institutionalizing the Outer Other

The most significant institutional development of this period was the creation of the **Lifān Yuàn** (理藩院—Court of Colonial Affairs). Unlike the Míng, who treated the inner frontiers as domestic provinces to be Sinicized, the Qīng governed Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang through the **Lifān Yuàn** as a distinct "Inner Asian" bloc.

This institution functioned as a **Taxonomic Gatekeeper**, managing the unique legal, religious, and military needs of the **Wàifān** (外藩—Outer Barbarians). Through the **Lifān Yuàn**, the Qīng institutionalized the "Other" as a strategic partner. They protected Tibetan Buddhism and Mongolian tribal structures from Han encroachment, ensuring that these groups remained loyal directly to the Manchu throne. This "differentiated colonialism" turned the **Sìyí** (四夷) quadrants into specific administrative zones, transforming the "Other" from a vague frontier threat into a codified, institutionalized component of the Great Qīng state.

## VIII. Maritime Re-orientation: The Rise of Yáng (洋)

The mid-19th century precipitated a foundational crisis in Sinitic taxonomy, shifting the focus of alterity from the terrestrial frontiers of Inner Asia to the maritime horizons of the coast. This "Oceanic Turn" necessitated the retirement of the traditional **Huá-Yí** (华夷) framework in favor of a new, spatialized nomenclature. As Western industrial powers breached the maritime defenses of the **Qīng** (清) state, the "Other" was no longer a "Barbarian" to be managed through ritual or tributary rotation, but a **Yáng** (洋—Oceanic/Overseas) power possessing superior technological and military parity.

### 8.1 The "Oceanic" Turn: Spatializing the Other

Historically, the Sinitic world-view was "Telluric," focusing on the land-based quadrants of the **Sìyí** (四夷). The ocean was a void, a boundary of the "Great Wash" where civilization simply ended. However, the Opium Wars forced a **Taxonomic Re-orientation**. The Westerner was re-categorized from a peripheral **Fān** (番) to a **Yángrén** (洋人—Oceanic Person). This shift was significant: while **夷** (*yí*) or **番** (*fān*) implied a deficiency in ritual or location, **洋** (*yáng*) implied a geographic origin that was physically separated from the Middle Kingdom by the vastness of the sea.

This spatialization allowed the **Qīng** state to engage with Western powers as distinct "Nations" (*Guó* 国) rather than "Tribes." The emergence of terms like **Xīyáng** (西洋—Western Ocean) and **Dōngyáng** (东洋—Eastern Ocean/Japan) transformed the "Other" into a sovereign entity existing in a parallel maritime space. The taxonomy shifted from the biological dehumanization of the "Beast" to a pragmatic recognition of "Overseas" geopolitical actors.

### 8.2 The Treaty of Tientsin (1858) and the Legal Erasure of 夷 (Yí)

The most consequential moment in the history of Sinitic "Othering" was the formal, legal prohibition of the character **夷** (*yí*). Following the Second Opium War, the British sought to dismantle the linguistic foundations of Chinese superiority. Article 51 of the **Treaty of Tientsin** (天津条约) explicitly forbade the use of **夷** in any official document pertaining to the British government or its subjects.

| Treaty Provision | Native Script | Impact on Taxonomy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Article 51** | **不准书写“夷”字** | Formal criminalization of the term "Barbarian" in diplomacy. |
| **New Descriptor** | **洋** (Yáng) | Transition to the neutral "Oceanic" or "Overseas" prefix. |
| **Institutional Shift** | **总理衙门** (Zǒnglǐ Yámén) | Establishment of the first formal Ministry of Foreign Affairs. |

This was an act of "Linguistic Colonization." By forcing the **Qīng** court to erase **夷** from its lexicon, the Western powers successfully performed a "Taxonomic Coup," elevating themselves from "Barbarians" to "Equals" through the barrel of a gun. The transition to **洋** (Yáng) marked the end of the universal empire and the beginning of the **Qīng** state's painful entry into the Westphalian system of international relations.

### 8.3 Botanical and Material Taxonomy: The Legacy of the Fān (番)

Despite the diplomatic erasure of **夷**, the older taxonomic markers survived in the material culture and botany of the period. The character **番** (*fān*), having lost its specific ethnic potency, became a prefix for foreign-origin goods and technologies. This resulted in a **Commercial Taxonomy** where the "Otherness" of a product was permanently branded by its linguistic origin.

In this context, **番** functioned as a marker of **Exotic Utility**. It stripped the "Other" of their martial threat but preserved their identity as a source of material novelty. While the officials in Beijing were learning to use the neutral **洋** (*yáng*) for diplomats, the people of the coastal provinces (particularly the **Hokkien** and **Cantonese**) continued to use **番** to categorize the deluge of Western goods and people arriving on their shores. This bifurcated taxonomy—pragmatic and oceanic in the North, vernacular and material in the South—became the linguistic landscape of late-imperial China’s encounter with modernity.

## IX. Liminal Identities: Case Studies in Hybridity

The rigid quadrants of the **Sìyí** (四夷) and the administrative tiers of the **Yuán** (元) and **Qīng** (清) dynasties frequently encountered "Taxonomic Friction" at the geographic and cultural margins of the empire. In these spaces, identity was not a fixed coordinate but a negotiated state. This section examines the emergence of liminal identities—communities and polities that existed in a state of ontological ambiguity, utilizing "Othering" labels as strategic tools for survival, commerce, and self-definition. Through the study of the **Peranakan** synthesis and the **Ryūkyū** (琉球) anomaly, we observe how the "Other" was not merely a passive subject of Sinitic labeling but an active participant in the reconfiguration of alterity.

### 9.1 The Peranakan Synthesis: Reclaiming the Huan (番) Root

In the maritime environment of the **Nányáng** (南洋—Southern Ocean), the character **番** (*fān*) underwent a radical transformation from a marker of exclusion to a signifier of localized hybridity. The **Peranakan** (Straits Chinese) community emerged from the union of male Hokkien immigrants and local Malay women, creating a "Third Space" that defied the binary logic of the Sinitic center.

While the newly arrived **Sīnkè** (新客—"New Guests") from the mainland utilized the term **Huan-á** (番仔—"Little Barbarian") as a derogatory marker for the indigenous population, the Peranakans internalized this "Fān" element as a foundational component of their heritage. They became the **Huan-á-sūn** (番仔孙—"Grandchildren of the Foreigners"), a label that acknowledged their maternal indigenous roots while maintaining their paternal Sinitic lineage.

In this context, **番** ceased to be a marker of "raw" savagery and became a marker of **Creolization**. The "Fān" influence provided the community with its unique linguistic patois (**Baba Malay**), its distinctive culinary synthesis (**Nyonya** cuisine), and its material culture. The taxonomy moved from the "Cooked" versus "Raw" binary to a "Hybrid" model, where the **番** prefix was reclaimed as a badge of local adaptation and superior tropical sophistication.

### 9.2 The Ryūkyū Anomaly: Performative Foreignness

The **Ryūkyū Kingdom** (琉球王国) provides the quintessential example of "Taxonomic Masking." For centuries, Ryūkyū existed in a state of **Dual Vassalage** (**Ryōzoku** —両属), serving as a formal tributary to the **Qīng** court while being simultaneously controlled by the Japanese domain of **Satsuma**. To navigate these overlapping hegemonies, the Ryūkyūans utilized **Performative Alterity**.

| Imperial Audience | Taxonomic Performance | Goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Qīng Court (Beijing)** | **Shǔguó** (属国—Vassal State) | Maintain the "Land of Propriety" (**守礼之邦**) status; secure trade. |
| **Tokugawa Bakufu (Edo)** | **I-teki** (夷狄—Barbarian) | Act as an "Exotic Tributary" to bolster the Shogun's prestige. |

The Japanese specifically ordered Ryūkyūan envoys to wear "Ming-style" robes and play "foreign" music during processions to Edo to emphasize their status as "Other." Conversely, in Beijing, the Ryūkyūans presented themselves as the most "Loyal" and "Sinicized" of the **Wàifān** (外番). They were "Barbarians" for the sake of the Japanese and "Civilized" for the sake of the Chinese. This liminality proved that the labels of the **Sìyí** were not immutable truths but a **Diplomatic Theatre** where a small kingdom could survive by reflecting back the specific taxonomic fantasies of the surrounding empires.

### 9.3 Vernacular Fossils: The Persistence of 番 in Coastal Dialects

The legacy of these liminal encounters is preserved in the "Linguistic Sedimentation" of the coastal dialects, particularly **Hokkien** (Southern Min) and **Cantonese** (Yue). In these vernaculars, **番** (*fān/huan*) remains an active prefix, functioning as a "fossilized" taxonomic marker that bypasses the modern Mandarin-centric transition to **洋** (*yáng*) or **外** (*wài*).

| **Hokkien** | **Huan-kiuh** | **番姜** | "Foreign Ginger" (Chili); material branding of the exotic. |

This vernacular persistence illustrates a "Taxonomic Ghosting" where ancient imperial categories continue to haunt modern social interactions. In the coastal imagination, the "Other" is still fundamentally a **番** —a source of both tangible material novelty and lingering social anxiety. While the official state taxonomy has been sanitized for international diplomacy, the dialectal "Fān" remains the raw, unmediated language of the street, proving that the architecture of alterity is most resilient at the linguistic margins where the empire first met the sea.

## X. Conclusion: The Persistence of the "Other"

The diachronic evolution of Sinitic "Othering" taxonomies—from the zoomorphic quadrants of the **Sìyí** (四夷) to the mercantile pragmatism of the **Yáng** (洋)—reveals a civilizational strategy defined by flexible exclusion. This trajectory suggests that the Sinitic world-view was never a static monolith of xenophobia, but a sophisticated, adaptive mechanism designed to categorize the unknown in a way that preserved the ontological security of the "Radiant Center." By deconstructing the semantic migration of characters such as **夷** (*yí*), **胡** (*hú*), and **番** (*fān*), we uncover a structural logic that has transitioned from biological dehumanization to state-managed minority identity, a legacy that continues to resonate in contemporary sociopolitical landscapes.

### 10.1 From Biological Dehumanization to Managed Identity

The primary shift in this history is the transition from **Phenotypical Taxonomy** to **Administrative Taxonomy**. In the early periods, alterity was encoded in the physical body: the "dog-like" nature of the Northern **Dí** (狄) or the "dewlap" anatomy of the Western **Hú** (胡). However, as the empire grew into a continental and maritime power, these biological markers were found insufficient for the management of sophisticated populations.

Under the **Yuán** (元) and **Qīng** (清) dynasties, "Otherness" became a legal status rather than a purely racial one. The creation of the **Sèmù** (色目) and the institutionalization of the **Lifān Yuàn** (理藩院) proved that the "Other" could be utilized as a strategic asset without being "Sinicized." This evolution demonstrates a sophisticated form of **Imperial Differentiation**, where the preservation of a group's unique "Otherness" was essential to the stability of the empire.

| Period | Logic of Alterity | Primary Mechanism | Status of the "Other" |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pre-Qin/Han** | Cosmological/Biological | **Huá-Yí** (华夷) Distinction | Animalistic/Peripheral |

| **Yuan/Qing** | Administrative/Legal | Tiered Citizenship (**四等人**) | Strategic Subaltern |
| **Modern** | Nationalistic/Geopolitical | **Mínzú** (民族) Classification | Integrated Minority |

### 10.2 The Transformation of Hierarchy: Modern Resonance

Although the 1858 **Treaty of Tientsin** (天津条约) effectively criminalized the use of **夷** (*yí*) in official diplomacy, the underlying architecture of alterity was not erased; it was merely re-coded. The 20th-century transition to the **Zhōnghuá Mínzú** (中华民族—The Chinese Nation) model attempted to synthesize the "Five Races" into a singular national identity. However, the ancient etymologies of characters like **番** (*fān*) and **回** (*huí*) persist as "Semantic Sediments."

In modern nationalistic rhetoric, the "Other" is often framed through the lens of **Wàiguó** (外国—Foreign Country), a term that inherits the "Outside-the-Wall" logic of the ancient quadrants. Furthermore, the persistent use of **番** (*fān*) in vernacular dialects to describe foreign goods and non-Han peoples demonstrates that the "Beast Footprint" etymology still informs the sub-text of local social hierarchies. The "Other" is no longer a "Barbarian" in the legal sense, but remains a "Foreigner" in the ontological sense—a distinction that continues to influence modern China's diplomatic and domestic policies.

### 10.3 Final Synthesis: Language as the Architecture of Order

Ultimately, the study of Sinitic "Othering" taxonomies proves that **language is the ultimate architecture of the world order**. The Middle Kingdom was not just a geographic territory; it was a linguistic construct. By assigning specific characters to neighboring groups, the Sinitic center performed an act of **Cosmological Ordering**. Whether through the zoomorphic radicals of the past or the neutral oceanic prefixes of the present, the state has consistently used taxonomy to mediate the tension between universalism and exclusion.

Understanding this diachronic shift is essential for analyzing the modern Sinitic encounter with globalism. The history of **番, 夷,** and **胡** reminds us that "Otherness" is a tool of statecraft—a category that can be "cooked" for integration or "raw" for exclusion, depending on the requirements of the center. As we move further into the 21st century, these ancient etymological ghosts remain active, silently shaping the boundaries of the radiant center in an increasingly interconnected world.

- **The Catalyst:** The **Táng Dynasty’s** cosmopolitanism. The "Westerner" became a mirror through which the Táng elite viewed their own global sophistication.
- **The Turning Point:** The **An Lùshān Rebellion** (755 AD) transformed the "Exotic Hú" into the "Dangerous Traitor," hardening Sinitic boundaries for centuries.

## 3\. The Imperial Inversion (c. 1271 AD – 1368 AD)

**Primary Terms:****Sèmù (色目)**, **Mánzi (蛮子)**, **Sì-děng-rén (四等人)**

- **The Logic:** Alterity was defined by **Administrative Utility**. The Mongol rulers inverted the traditional hierarchy, placing "Foreigners" (**Sèmù**) above the "Native" Han.
- **The Catalyst:** The **Yuán Dynasty** conquest. "Otherness" became a professional qualification for middle-management, used to insulate the Mongol elite from the Han majority.
- **The Shift:** Dehumanization was redirected toward the Southern Han (**Mánzi**), while Westerners like Marco Polo were integrated into the ruling tier.

## 4\. The Digestion and the Mosaic (c. 1368 AD – 1840 AD)

**Primary Terms:****Huí (回)**, **Fān (番)**, **Shéng/Shú (生/熟)**

- **The Logic:** Alterity was defined by **Sinicization vs. Pluralism**. The **Míng** attempted to "digest" the Other into a singular Han body; the **Qīng** sought to preserve ethnic differences to maintain a "Mosaic Empire."
- **The Catalyst:** The **Manchu** conquest. The Qīng needed to keep Mongols, Tibetans, and Han distinct to act as the universal arbiter between them.
- **The Marker:** The **Lifān Yuàn (理藩院)** institutionalized the "Outer Other" as a state-managed resource rather than a threat to be eradicated.

---

## 5\. The Oceanic Shock (1840 AD – 1911 AD)

**Primary Terms:****Yáng (洋)**, **Xī (西)**, **Wài (外)**

- **The Logic:** Alterity was defined by **Spatial Parity**. The terrestrial "Barbarian" was replaced by the "Oceanic Power." The "Other" was now an industrial equal arriving from the sea.
- **The Catalyst:** The **Opium Wars** and the **Treaty of Tientsin** (1858), which legally abolished the term **夷 (Yí)** in diplomacy.
- **The Legacy:****番 (Fān)** was demoted to a material prefix for foreign crops (tomato/potato), while **洋 (Yáng)** became the modern label for the Western world.
