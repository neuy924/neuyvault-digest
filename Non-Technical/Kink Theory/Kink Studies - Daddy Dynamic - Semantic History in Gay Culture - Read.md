# English Feature Article

## What We Mean When We Say “Daddy”

There are words that enter culture with a bang, and then there are words that seep into it—quietly at first, then everywhere all at once, until it becomes almost impossible to remember when they weren’t there. In contemporary gay male culture, *daddy* is one of those words.

It appears in app profiles and Instagram captions, in porn categories and dating banter, in leather spaces and mainstream queer nightlife. Sometimes it means an older man. Sometimes it means a dominant man. Sometimes it means a nurturing man, or a rugged man, or simply a man who seems composed in a world built on speed and display. Increasingly, it may not even mean age at all. A younger man with enough beard, body, confidence, and “provider energy” can be called daddy too. That is part of the point.

The word has become so common that it is easy to treat it as superficial slang—an accessory term, somewhere between flirtation and meme. But the remarkable thing about *daddy* is that it is not superficial at all. It is overloaded. It carries the residue of family language, the aura of authority, the promise of care, the texture of masculinity, and the erotic charge of asymmetry. It can suggest tenderness and control in the same breath. It can sound like a joke and a confession at once.

That density is why the term has survived. And it is why, despite all its visibility, it remains surprisingly hard to define.

For scholars of sexuality, queer culture, and BDSM, daddy is not just a sexual nickname. It is a compact social theory. It tells us something about how gay men imagine age, power, safety, masculinity, and belonging. It also tells us something about what happens when old subcultural roles get lifted out of bars, leather scenes, and private encounters and turned into searchable profile categories on apps.

To understand why *daddy* became so potent, you have to begin with a simple fact: it is not one thing. It is several things layered on top of one another.

## A Father, But Not a Father

The first temptation, especially for outsiders, is to hear the word and assume pathology. Surely this is about fathers. Surely this is a symptom, a wound, a leftover childhood conflict dressed up as erotic play.

There is a reason that interpretation has been so durable. Psychoanalytic thinking, at its most sophisticated, has long argued that adult erotic life is never fully separable from fantasy, longing, identification, and authority. The father, in this account, is not only a literal parent but a psychic figure: someone tied to law, admiration, recognition, protection, and distance. A daddy dynamic, from that point of view, may stage a fantasy of being seen, guided, approved of, corrected, wanted—or of becoming the one who can do those things for someone else (Freeman 2008; Moss 2013).

That insight should not be dismissed too quickly. One reason daddy dynamics feel emotionally thicker than many other sexual terms is precisely that they gather authority and intimacy into the same figure. A generic dominant can command. A daddy, by contrast, often implies command with attention. The erotic appeal may lie not only in power, but in power made personal.

But psychoanalysis is only part of the story, and on its own it is too narrow. The modern gay use of *daddy* is not simply a private drama of father longing. It is also a public and shared social language. People know what kind of vibe the word is trying to summon. That social intelligibility matters just as much as psychic depth.

Queer theory offers a different way to understand this. Instead of asking what hidden family drama the word reveals, it asks what kind of relationship the word helps make possible. In that view, kinship language—daddy, boy, son, sir—does not merely point backward to literal family. It gets repurposed. It becomes a way of staging alternative forms of intimacy, hierarchy, and belonging (Butler 2004; Bauer 2018). Family language gets detached from reproduction and reassigned to desire.

That helps explain why *daddy* can sound at once familial and anti-familial, warm and transgressive, stabilizing and playful. It is borrowing the emotional architecture of kinship—care, asymmetry, trust, initiation—without restoring the actual family as a social institution. In queer life, especially where many people have had to improvise new kinds of relation outside conventional family structures, that borrowing can be deeply meaningful.

Which is another way of saying: the word is not innocent, but it is not reducible either.

## Leather, Discipline, and the Inheritance of Masculinity

If you want to understand how *daddy* took on its modern shape, you have to spend time in the world that gave it one of its most durable erotic grammars: leather culture.

Mid-20th-century gay leather worlds were not just about sex acts. They were worlds of dress, bearing, hierarchy, speech, ritual, apprenticeship, and coded masculinity. Men in those spaces were building something more elaborate than a hookup scene. They were creating an aesthetic and social order organized around seriousness, command, submission, discipline, and masculine legitimacy (Mosher, Levitt, and Manley 2006).

What later became known as “old guard” leather culture was often remembered—sometimes romantically—as a world of protocol, restraint, and earned authority. A senior man in such a world might not simply be a partner. He might be a guide, mentor, disciplinarian, initiator, or keeper of knowledge. The point was not just who did what sexually. It was how masculinity itself became structured through rank, comportment, and recognition.

That history matters because it gave daddy discourse something that contemporary app culture often flattens but never fully erases: **depth**. Before *daddy* became a tag, it was embedded in traditions where authority had to be enacted, not just declared. One learned it through scenes, spaces, elders, and subcultural literacy.

Still, leather history is not a complete origin story. Daddy discourse widened beyond leather. Post-Stonewall gay culture, clone aesthetics, gym bodies, bear culture, and the broader erotic prestige of mature masculinity all helped carry the term into new settings. Then AIDS altered the emotional charge of age difference itself. Older men could become not only erotic figures, but survivors, mentors, witnesses, and stewards of memory. Age acquired historical weight. To desire the older man was not always to desire age alone; sometimes it was to desire steadiness, knowledge, endurance, and the sense of being oriented by someone who had lived through more (Silva 2023).

By the time *daddy* reached the app era, it had already absorbed several histories: leather authority, masculine performance, intergenerational transmission, and the eroticization of maturity. What digital platforms did was not invent daddy. They made him sortable.

## The App Did Not Create Daddy. It Changed Him.

One of the most consequential things apps have done to queer language is to convert historically dense roles into interface-ready metadata.

In older scene life, being a daddy might involve conduct, ritual, or recognition over time. On a platform, it can be claimed in advance. It appears in a bio, a tribe label, a tag, a filter, a search field. Grindr has literally described tags as a kind of SEO for the self. Once that happens, a role becomes a category of discoverability. It enters an attention economy.

This shift is subtle but profound. A role becomes a profile. A relation becomes a brand.

That does not make the term fake. But it does alter the kind of evidence the term requires. A daddy no longer has to be known through mentorship, protocol, or sustained interaction. He can be known first through photography, beard, body language, profile language, and the right mix of confidence and maturity on a screen. In other words, he becomes partially legible through curation.

Apps reward terms that are broad enough to attract users and vague enough to absorb multiple meanings. Daddy is perfect for that. It can mean older, dominant, nurturing, protective, masculine, stable, thick, bearded, commanding, stylish, or all of the above. Boy also travels well. Son does not. Son is more specific, more filially explicit, more niche. It tends to stay closer to scene-based kink or explicit role-play, whereas daddy can scale into mainstream desirability culture.

That asymmetry is revealing. Erotic vocabularies thrive when they allow asymmetry without forcing too much precision. Daddy and boy can do that. Son often cannot.

This is why digital platforms have not just popularized daddy discourse; they have made it more elastic. A leather daddy, a silver daddy, a younger daddy, a gym daddy, a soft daddy, a provider daddy—these are not random proliferations. They are signs of a category that has become infrastructural. It is no longer simply spoken between people. It is built into how desire is organized and displayed.

## Why the Word Feels Safe—And Why It Isn’t Neutral

Part of daddy’s modern appeal lies in its emotional economy. It promises authority without coldness. It hints at control, but not anonymous control. Even when explicit dominance is present, the term often carries an undertone of care: someone who knows, someone who steadies, someone who can hold a scene—or perhaps a life—together.

That is why daddy discourse has become so effective in a time of accelerated, profile-based intimacy. The app economy is built on quick impressions, fragmented selves, and endless comparison. Daddy offers a fantasy of coherence. The daddy figure is not just hot. He is grounded. He seems less scattered than the digital environment around him.

But that apparent safety should not be confused with innocence. Daddy is still a language of asymmetry. It organizes hierarchy, age, and authority, whether softly or sharply. And that hierarchy has never been evenly distributed.

One of the strongest criticisms emerging from recent scholarship is that daddy and leather aesthetics have often been built around a white masculine norm, even when race is not named directly (Liang 2025). The “classic” leatherman, the “old-school” masculine elder, the rugged authoritative body—these have frequently been imagined through whiteness as if whiteness were merely neutral. It is not.

That matters because who gets to look authoritative is never just a personal question. It is a social and visual one. White men can more easily appear “classic,” “traditional,” or “naturally” paternal. Black, brown, and Asian men may be desired, but often through more racialized readings: danger, excess, novelty, fetish, or stylization.

In that sense, daddy discourse is not only about intimacy. It is also about legitimacy.

## The Men Who Changed the Image

That is why race has to be part of the story—not only as exclusion, but as invention.

Black and brown queer cultures have not simply asked to be included in an already existing daddy ideal. They have remade the category. They have brought different lineages of masculinity, style, labor, neighborhood life, migration, toughness, care, and erotic authority into the field. The result is not just a more diverse daddy. It is a different grammar of what daddy can mean.

This is especially visible in Latinx and Chicano formations, where scholars such as Richard T. Rodríguez have shown how the “homeboy” aesthetic—rooted in barrio masculinity, class, and local credibility—can be queered rather than abandoned (Rodríguez 2006). The route from homeboy to daddy is not a simple, documented straight line. But the cultural logic is clear enough: a masculinity once coded as street, local, and heteronormative can be transformed into a queer form of mature authority. That authority does not have to pass through white leatherman history as its only model.

The same is true more broadly of Black and brown counter-aesthetics. A daddy figure can be built from archives of migration, caballerismo, labor, urban style, diasporic masculinity, revised machismo, and neighborhood survival—not just from the clone, the leatherman, or the white bear. When that happens, the category changes. Authority looks different. Care looks different. Protection looks different.

And perhaps most importantly, history looks different. Once you see these counter-aesthetics, the supposedly universal daddy image starts to look less universal and more like what it always was: one dominant version among several.

## The World Outside English

Step outside Anglophone culture, and the picture becomes more complex still.

There is no large, fully developed non-Western academic field devoted specifically to daddy/son terminology in the same way contemporary English-language discourse might tempt us to imagine. What exists instead are adjacent literatures: on older gay men, queer lexicon, BDSM identity, app culture, kinship language, and age-coded masculinity.

In Chinese-speaking contexts, for example, the semantic work of *daddy* is often split across multiple terms rather than gathered into one. Historical terms like 契父 and 契子 belong to older kinship and fictive-family structures; they are not simply old Chinese versions of app-era daddy/son. Modern queer slang, meanwhile, often uses other systems—同志, 攻, 受, 熟男, 大叔—to sort role, age, masculinity, and desirability. Taiwan has produced valuable scholarship on BDSM communities and middle-aged gay men, while Chinese app culture has its own forms of age-coded masculine appeal. But the translation is functional, not literal.

Japan and Korea show similar patterns. Older-man attraction, hierarchical intimacy, rugged masculinity, and dominance may all exist as recognizable formations, but they are often distributed across different local terms, honorifics, and imported loanwords rather than condensed into one stable daddy category.

This matters because it forces a methodological correction. It is not enough to ask whether other cultures “have daddy.” The better question is: how do they divide up the meanings that English bundles together so easily?

That question is still badly understudied. It may be one of the most interesting open problems in this entire field.

## The Future of the Word

There is something fitting about the fact that daddy remains unstable. It is, after all, a word about relation, and relations change with the media and histories that sustain them.

In one register, daddy is still thick with fantasy: paternal authority, approval, control, protection, rivalry, surrender. In another, it is subcultural history: leather pedagogy, old-guard memory, AIDS-era transmission. In another, it is a modern masculinity aesthetic: beard, solidity, confidence, age-coded appeal. In another still, it is profile metadata—a discoverable category, a market signal, a publicly circulated persona.

Those layers do not cancel one another out. They accumulate.

Which is why the word has endured when so many other sexual labels become dated, too niche, or too specific. Daddy can still feel personal even when it is heavily mediated. It can still sound intimate even when it appears in a drop-down menu. It can still suggest an entire relationship with a single word.

That may be its most modern quality. In an era when digital life has turned so many forms of desire into quick classifications, daddy remains unusually dense. The joke is that everyone knows what it means. The truth is that no one means exactly the same thing by it.

And that may be why people keep reaching for it. Not because it is simple. Because it is not.

# 中文翻譯（繁體中文）

## 我們說「Daddy」時，到底在說什麼

有些詞是突然闖進文化裡的，有些詞則是慢慢滲進去的——起初不太起眼，之後卻無所不在，直到你幾乎想不起它不曾存在的時候。在當代男同志文化裡，*daddy* 就是這樣的一個詞。

它出現在交友 app 個人檔案、Instagram 標語、色情分類、調情對話、皮革圈、夜店，甚至日常聊天裡。有時它指年長男人，有時指支配型男人，有時指有照顧感的男人、粗獷的男人，或者只是那種在快速而表演化的世界裡顯得特別穩的人。如今，它甚至未必真的指年齡。一個夠有鬍子、夠有身材、夠有自信、散發「provider energy」的年輕男人，也可能被叫 daddy。這正是重點之一。

這個詞如今太普及了，以至於人們很容易把它當成表層俚語——像是介於調情和迷因之間的一種裝飾性說法。但真正值得注意的是，*daddy* 一點也不表層。它是一個高度過載的詞。它帶著親屬語言的殘響、權威的氣場、照顧的承諾、陽剛氣質的質地，以及權力不對稱的情色張力。它可以同時暗示溫柔與控制，也可以既像玩笑又像坦白。

正因如此，這個詞才活了下來。也正因如此，儘管它如此常見，它依然難以精準定義。

對研究性／別、酷兒文化與 BDSM 的學者來說，daddy 不只是一個性暱稱。它幾乎是一套濃縮的社會理論。它告訴我們男同志如何想像年齡、權力、安全感、陽剛氣質與歸屬感。它也告訴我們，當舊有次文化角色從酒吧、皮革圈與私密場景中被抽離，變成 app 上可搜尋的標籤時，究竟會發生什麼。

要理解為什麼 *daddy* 變得如此有力，首先得承認一件事：它從來不是單一的東西。它是一層又一層疊加出來的。

## 是父親，但又不是父親

尤其對圈外人來說，最常見的直覺反應就是把這個詞病理化。既然叫 daddy，難道不就是在談父親？難道不就是某種心理創傷、未竟情結，或者童年衝突的情色轉譯？

這個理解之所以頑強存在，不是沒有原因。精神分析在最有洞見的時候，確實指出成人的情色生活從來不只是表面客體那麼簡單。欲望總是經由幻想、認同、失落、禁令與重複而被塑形。在這種框架裡，父親不只是現實中的父親，而是一個心理功能：與權威、承認、競爭、保護、法律、規訓綁在一起的形象。從這個角度看，daddy／son 動態可能在上演的，是一種被看見、被引導、被肯定、被修正、被想要——或者反過來，成為那個可以對別人做這些事的人——的幻想（Freeman 2008；Moss 2013）。

這種洞見不該被太快丟棄。Daddy 動態之所以常常比其他性角色詞彙更有情感厚度，正是因為它把權威與親密收束在同一個人物身上。普通的 dominant 可以發號施令；但 daddy 常常還意味著那種帶著關注的權力。情色吸引力不只來自權力本身，也來自「被個人化的權力」。

但精神分析只說對了部分，而且單獨使用時太狹窄。現代男同志語境中的 daddy，並不只是私人的父性戲劇。它同時也是一種公開而共享的社會語言。人們知道這個詞大概在召喚什麼樣的氛圍。這種社會可辨識性，和心理深度一樣重要。

酷兒理論在這裡提供了另一條路。它不問這個詞隱藏了什麼家庭戲碼，而是問它創造了什麼樣的關係可能。從這個角度看，像 daddy、boy、son、sir 這樣的親屬語言，並不只是指回家庭，而是把家庭語言從其原本的生殖與規範功能裡抽離出來，重新安置到情色與次文化場域中（Butler 2004；Bauer 2018）。家庭語言不再只是家庭語言，而成了親密、階序與歸屬感的替代性裝置。

這也解釋了為什麼 *daddy* 可以同時有家庭感，又有反家庭感；既溫暖又越界；既穩定又帶遊戲性。它借用的是親屬關係的情感架構——照顧、不對稱、信任、引導——卻不是要回到傳統家庭。對許多必須在主流家庭結構之外自行發明關係形式的酷兒來說，這種借用本身就很有重量。

換句話說：這個詞確實不天真，但也不能被簡化。

## 皮革、規訓，與陽剛氣質的繼承

若要理解 daddy 如何長成今天的樣子，就必須回到一個給了它重要情色語法的世界：皮革文化。

二十世紀中葉的男同志皮革世界，從來不只是性行為本身。那是一個由服裝、姿態、階序、說話方式、儀式、學習與編碼化陽剛氣質構成的世界。身處其中的人建立的，不只是約炮圈，而是一套由嚴肅性、支配、服從、紀律與陽剛正當性所組成的審美與社會秩序（Mosher, Levitt, and Manley 2006）。

後來所謂的 “old guard” 皮革文化，常被追憶為一個講究規範、克制與「必須賺得」權威的世界。那裡的年長男人不只是伴侶，還可能是導師、規訓者、引路人、知識傳承者。重點不只是誰和誰做了什麼，而是陽剛氣質本身如何透過階級、舉止與認可被組織起來。

這段歷史很重要，因為它給了 daddy 一件今天在 app 文化中常被壓平、但從未消失的東西：**深度**。在 daddy 成為可選標籤之前，它曾經被嵌在一套必須透過場景、空間、前輩與次文化讀寫能力才能學會的傳統裡。

當然，皮革並不是唯一的起點。Daddy 話語後來走出皮革圈。Stonewall 之後的男同志文化、clone 審美、健身身體、bear 文化，以及成熟陽剛氣質本身的情色化，都幫助這個詞進一步擴散。而愛滋危機又給了年齡差一種更沉重的情感意義。年長男人不只是年長的對象，也可能是倖存者、見證者、記憶保管者。年齡不再只是歲數，而帶有歷史重量。欲望指向年長男人，有時並不只是因為老，而是因為穩、因為懂、因為熬過來了，因為他似乎能把你安放在某個更有方向感的世界裡（Silva 2023）。

到了 app 時代，*daddy* 這個詞已經吸收了好幾重歷史：皮革權威、陽剛表演、世代傳遞，以及成熟本身的情色化。數位平台並沒有發明 daddy，它們做的是把 daddy 變成可以被排序的東西。

## App 沒有創造 Daddy，但改變了 Daddy

數位平台對酷兒語言做的其中一件最重要的事，就是把原本有厚度的角色，變成介面可以辨識的 metadata。

在早期場景生活裡，成為 daddy 也許意味著一套行為、儀式與長時間互動所累積出的認可。但到了平台上，它可以被預先宣稱。它出現在簡介、tag、tribe、搜尋篩選裡。一旦如此，角色就變成了可被發現的類別，進入了注意力經濟。

這個轉變看似微小，實則深刻。角色成了檔案，關係成了品牌。

這並不表示它是假的。但這確實改變了這個角色需要什麼樣的證據。Daddy 不再一定要透過導引、規範或長時間互動來被認識。它現在可以先透過照片、鬍子、身材語言、個人檔案措辭，以及一種精心調配的成熟與自信，在螢幕上被辨識。換言之，它部分地成為一種策展出來的形象。

App 會獎勵那些足夠寬泛、足夠模糊、又足夠吸引人的詞。Daddy 正好符合這種需求。它可以意味著年長、支配、照顧、穩定、成熟、manly、protective，或以上皆是。Boy 也很容易流通。Son 就不一樣。Son 太具體、太明確，太偏向明顯的父子結構，所以比較難被擴張成大眾化的吸引類別。

這種不對稱本身很有啟發性。情色詞彙之所以能成功，往往是因為它讓不對稱存在，但又不把它講死。Daddy 和 boy 可以做到這點，son 往往做不到。

因此，平台不只是讓 daddy 更普及，而是讓它更有彈性。Leather daddy、silver daddy、gym daddy、soft daddy、provider daddy——這些不是偶然湧現的分支，而是一個已經基礎設施化的類別所必然產生的結果。它不再只是兩個人之間的叫法，而是整個慾望顯示與排序系統裡的一環。

## 為什麼這個詞讓人感到安全——以及它為什麼從不無害

Daddy 在當代如此迷人的一個原因，是它的情感經濟。它承諾的是帶著溫度的權威。即使存在明確的支配，它也常常伴隨照顧的語感：知道該怎麼做的人、可以穩住場面的人、能把你接住的人。

這也是為什麼在一個充滿快速印象、碎片化自我與無盡比較的 app 世界裡，daddy 特別有吸引力。它提供了一種「完整性」的幻想。Daddy 不只是性感，他還顯得不那麼散。好像比數位環境本身更穩。

但這種安全感不能被誤認為天真。Daddy 仍然是一套不對稱的語言。它組織的是權力、年齡與權威，不管是以溫和還是尖銳的方式。而這種權威從來不是平均分配的。

近年的研究一再提醒我們，daddy 與皮革美學常常建立在一個白人陽剛常態之上，即使種族本身沒有被明講（Liang 2025）。「經典」皮革男、「old school」陽剛長者、粗獷而有權威感的身體——這些往往透過白人身體被想像成自然。這一點絕不能忽略。

因為誰比較容易看起來像有權威的人，從來不只是個人問題，也是社會問題、視覺問題。白人男性更容易被讀成「經典」「傳統」「自然有父性氣場」；黑人、棕色人、亞洲男性則更容易被讀成某種特例、危險、過度、異國、或 fetish。

從這個角度看，daddy 不只是在說親密，也是在說正當性。

## 改變這個形象的人

正因如此，種族不能只以排除的角度來談，也必須以創造的角度來談。

黑人與棕色酷兒文化並不只是要求被納入既有 daddy 理想之中；它們也在改寫這個理想。它們把不同的陽剛歷史、風格、勞動、街區生活、移民經驗、韌性與照顧感帶進這個場域。結果不只是更多元的 daddy，而是不同語法的 daddy。

這在 Latinx 與 Chicano 的脈絡中特別清楚。像 Richard T. Rodríguez 這樣的學者指出，「homeboy」美學——根植於 barrio 陽剛氣質、階級位置與地方性認受——是可以被 queer 化的，而不是只能被拋棄（Rodríguez 2006）。從 homeboy 到 daddy 不見得是一條被單一文獻完整記錄的直線，但它背後的文化邏輯是清楚的：原本被編碼為街區、地方、異性戀的陽剛氣質，可以被轉譯成一種成熟的、酷兒的權威形式。它不需要以白人皮革男作為唯一中介。

黑人與棕色反美學更廣泛地也都是如此。一個 daddy 形象可以建立在勞動、移民、caballerismo、都市風格、僑散陽剛、修正版 machismo 與地方韌性的檔案上，而不只是建立在 clone、leatherman 或白人 bear 的歷史上。當這種轉譯發生時，類別本身就改變了。權威看起來不一樣，照顧看起來不一樣，保護也不一樣。

而最重要的是，歷史也因此看起來不同了。當你開始看見這些反美學，原本被當成普世的 daddy 形象，就會顯得不再普世。它原本就只是其中一種主導版本而已。

## 英語之外的世界

一旦走出英語世界，情況就更複雜了。

目前並不存在一個成熟、龐大的非西方學術領域，專門像英語世界那樣處理 daddy／son 術語本身。更常見的是鄰近文獻：年長男同志、酷兒詞彙、BDSM 身份、app 文化、親屬語言與年齡化陽剛氣質。

以中文世界為例，英語裡 *daddy* 打包起來的語義，常常被分散到不同詞裡。像 契父、契子 這樣的歷史詞彙屬於較早的擬親屬關係與地方社會脈絡，不能直接等同於 app 時代的 daddy／son。現代同志俚語則多用其他系統——同志、攻、受、熟男、大叔——來分類角色、年齡、陽剛與吸引力。台灣已經有一些關於 BDSM 社群與中年男同志的研究，而中國大陸的 app 文化也有自己的年齡化陽剛吸引範疇。但這些對應是功能性的，不是字面性的。

日本和韓國也一樣。年長男性吸引、階序式親密、粗獷陽剛與支配都可能存在，但往往分散在不同的本地詞彙、敬稱與外來語之間，而不是被壓縮成一個穩定的 daddy 類別。

這迫使我們做一個方法論上的修正：問題不是其他文化「有沒有 daddy」，而是它們如何把英語裡被濃縮在 daddy 這個詞中的那些意義——年齡、權威、陽剛、照顧、支配——分配到自己的語彙系統裡。

而這恰恰是目前最值得研究、卻仍然嚴重不足的問題之一。

## 這個詞的未來

Daddy 之所以一直不穩定，或許正因為它本來就是關係性的詞，而關係總會隨媒介與歷史改變。

在某個層次上，daddy 仍然帶著厚重的幻想：父性權威、肯定、控制、保護、競爭、臣服。在另一個層次上，它是次文化歷史：皮革傳承、old-guard 記憶、愛滋時代的世代延續。在另一個層次上，它是現代陽剛審美：鬍子、厚實、自信、成熟魅力。而在另一個層次上，它又是個人檔案 metadata——可搜尋的類別、可市場化的訊號、可公開流通的角色。

這些層次並不互相抵消，而是累積。

也正因如此，當其他性／別詞彙變得過時、過於小眾或過於精確時，daddy 還能活下來。它即使在高度媒介化的環境裡，仍然顯得私人。即使出現在下拉式選單裡，仍然帶著親密感。它仍然能用一個詞暗示整段關係。

這大概就是它最現代的地方。在一個數位生活把太多慾望壓縮成快速分類的時代，daddy 仍然是一個異常厚重的詞。玩笑是：大家都知道它是什麼。真相是：每個人說它時，指的都不完全一樣。

也許人們之所以不斷回到這個詞，不是因為它簡單，而是因為它不簡單。
